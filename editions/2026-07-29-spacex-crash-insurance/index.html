IN MATT'S ARTICLE: SpaceX structured notes

The SpaceX Safety Note That Protected the Bank

Bloomberg’s Lu Wang reported a Wall Street race: at least five firms planned notes tied to SpaceX. Morgan Stanley and Marex Group were among the crew, offering high payouts and limited protection too.

SpaceX promised rockets and AI dreams bright, but bubbles and Elon Musk risk caused some fright. Morgan Stanley’s note made this offer instead: “Put in $1,000 and wait up ahead.”

In early 2028, if shares rose or stayed flat, the customer earned 40%—imagine that! Even if SpaceX fell, but by less than half, the same promised payout followed its path.

But if SpaceX finished down more than 50%—snap! The cushion completely fell out of the map. The customer bore the whole stock-price fall, not merely the part past the halfway wall.

Say shares dropped from about $112 to $50 by then: roughly nine shares would be worth $450 again. Morgan would return that $450, but keep $550— the bank’s crash-insurance payoff from the note.

This was a prepaid “down-and-in knock-in put”: through smaller falls, its payout door stayed shut. Past the 50% barrier, the option awoke; the investor took the loss, giving Morgan crash cover.

So buyers got shelter when losses were slight, while Morgan got shelter when losses took flight. Morgan valued each $1,000 note at $947, leaving the bank a 5.3% margin.

What happened? Morgan Stanley collected $1,000 upfront and offered a 40% return if SpaceX did not fall more than 50% by maturity, but investors absorbed the full decline if it did. This meant the investors were actually selling Morgan Stanley funded SpaceX crash insurance.

STORY 2
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Sports index futures

The Number That Plays Ball

FutureSports counts every pitch, every out, every run, then folds them into one score when each ballgame is done.

Suppose a Mets Index starts at 7,500. A win could make it climb; a slump could make it dive. A burst of home runs might lift it to 7,565.

CME Group plans futures and options on that score, with cash paid at monthly or quarterly close. Buy if you think the Mets will beat what folks expect; sell if you fear they’ll stumble next.

Leigh Taylforth said stadiums, sponsors, insurers, apparel makers, and broadcasters might hedge. A bar whose beer sales rise when Mets fans cheer could short the futures to soften a dreary year.

Traders could rebuild the index with separate Kalshi bets—on wins, home runs, injuries, and the rest. If the index price climbed too high, they could sell it and buy those smaller bets instead.

Bob Fitzimmons of Wedbush Securities called sports a new tradable asset class. Soon an ETF might hold Mets futures, so “generic Mets goodness” could sit beside stocks in your account.

But thirty baseball-team funds cannot all produce new wealth from play. Sports betting is zero-sum—and costs nibble more away.

Unless, oddly, the formula gives extra points so every team’s expected score can rise. Then futures should cost extra from the start, which makes this already-weird financial ballgame an even stranger prize.

What happened? CME Group announced plans, pending regulatory approval, for cash-settled futures and options based on FutureSports indexes calculated from play-by-play sports data. Traders could use them to speculate, hedge sports-related risks, or arbitrage the index against individual event bets, but the contracts do not create an outside source of investment returns.

STORY 3
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: ETF spaghetti cannon

Corgi Funds and the Spaghetti Cannon

An ETF is a basket whose shares trade with one click; it wraps up an investing idea for people to pick.

By mid-July, Morningstar counted 1,084 brand-new funds— nearly 2025’s full-year record of 1,161 launches and runs.

Corgi Funds joined in December, then raced through the gate, launching Coffee and Energy Drinks among its 188.

Buy Now Pay Later! War Machine! More joined the call, plus twice-leveraged funds that could double a rise—or a fall.

Corgi filed for 360 more, a remarkable amount. It could pass BlackRock’s 488 US ETFs by count.

But StockAnalysis showed a much different view: BlackRock held $4.5 trillion; Corgi held less than $1 billion in its crew.

Morningstar’s Bryan Armour pictured noodles in flight: “The spaghetti cannon” fired wildly, with few funds sticking right.

Anthony Crinieri knew each launch could not win the race: perhaps 20 percent would gather 80 percent of assets in the place.

Why keep firing ideas that may flop straight away? Someone might want one—and ETFs have a tax trick when they trade.

A Robinhood agent can trade without commissions for you, but taxable gains may still arrive when its buying and selling are through.

What happened? Corgi Funds rapidly launched 188 ETFs and filed for 360 more, hoping a small share would attract most investor assets. ETFs package strategies into tradable funds, and portfolio trades inside them generally do not create immediate taxes for shareholders the way personal stock trades can.

IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Thought leadership

The Reports That Looked Very Deep

PwC published reports with a scholarly air, On AI and electric vehicles, polished with care. They were “thought leadership,” sent out to say, “Our Middle East partners can help you today.”

To drum up consulting means showing you know, So clients may hire you and business may grow. But GPTZero researchers inspected each claim; The FT verified the problems they named.

Fake footnotes appeared where good sources should be; Some claims named the wrong source, while some had no trail to see. These “AI hallucinations” looked certain and bright— But were made-up or unverifiable when brought into light.

Perhaps no one expected a close-reading test; The reports only had to suggest, “PwC knows best.” But somebody read them and spotted the flaws, So deep-looking thoughts earned a thoughtful pause.

PwC said accuracy mattered and changes were due: “A limited number” of citations would get an update too. It mentioned quality controls its people should obey, But not how all those errors had gotten that way.

Charlie Munger’s old joke fit the moment quite well: “If it’s not worth doing at all, it’s not worth doing well.” And AI made it easy, with astonishing speed, To poorly make reports that few planned to read.

What happened? PwC published AI and electric-vehicle reports intended to attract consulting clients in the Middle East; GPTZero researchers found fake footnotes, misattributed claims, and unverifiable information, and the FT verified their findings. PwC said it was updating a limited number of citations but did not explain how the errors entered the reports.
