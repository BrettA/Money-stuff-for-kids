Elon’s Trillion-Dollar Trip to Mars

Elon Musk leads Tesla, where cars and robots roll; His prize is twelve stock bundles, each tied to a goal. Tesla must reach market values from $2 trillion to $8.5 trillion, Plus earnings or product targets—then his award could top $1 trillion.

The goals include 20 million Teslas delivered with care, A million working robotaxis and a million bots out there. But if another company buys Tesla below $2 trillion one day, The unearned bundles vanish—poof!—and the prize goes away.

Musk also leads SpaceX, whose challenge climbs much higher: Up to 1.3 billion shares if its value keeps rising like fire. Market value alone won’t unlock that stock-filled door: SpaceX needs one million people living permanently on Mars—or

Non-Earth data centers producing 100 terawatts of compute each year. Those are not small chores to finish before the shares appear! Now SpaceX and Tesla are considering joining as one, With SpaceX likely surviving when the merger is done.

At today’s assumed values, Tesla’s whole award could disappear, While SpaceX might lift its final goal to roughly $13 trillion from here. Yet Musk could hold about one-third of the combined company’s stock, Worth more than $4 trillion if it reached that lofty dock.

So this isn’t clever pay-package gymnastics among the stars; Musk already owns plenty, with or without a million folks on Mars. The stranger prize is control of Tesla’s robots from SpaceX’s voting chair: Super-voting shares could put the whole robot army under his care.

What happened? Tesla and SpaceX are considering a merger that, under the article’s assumptions, could cancel Musk’s unearned Tesla award while preserving an adjustable SpaceX award. The likely purpose would be to give Musk super-voting control of the combined company, not to increase his compensation.

IN MATT'S ARTICLE: MidCap

MidCap and the Two Price Tags

A BDC is a public fund holding private-credit loans; its shares trade each day at a price of their own. MidCap Financial Investment Corp. had two values in view: what its assets were marked at, and what buyers would do.

Its NAV—assets minus debts—was $12.86, but Monday’s market price was just $8.36. Then Ares Capital agreed to buy the fund from Apollo, in a $2.6 billion deal with two price tags to follow.

MidCap holders were promised $10.88 a share: $1.21 in cash, with Ares stock for the rest of their fare. The offer stood thirty percent over MidCap’s market price, though fifteen percent under its NAV—not quite as nice.

The Ares stock was announced as worth $9.67, using NAV to count how many shares would be given. Ares had NAV of $19.31 a share, but its market price, $18.61, was lower out there.

So stock called $9.67 in the deal’s bright display was worth about $9.32 at the market that day. A “thirty-percent premium” could still lead the parade, though the stock portion was worth less when actually traded.

What happened? Ares Capital agreed to acquire MidCap using cash and Ares shares, with the exchange ratio valuing Ares stock at the greater of its NAV or market price. Because Ares traded below NAV, stock announced as $9.67 of value had a market value of about $9.32.

STORY 3
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Pattern day trading

The Day the Day-Trading Gate Came Down

FINRA once set a guardrail in the way: Four margin-account round trips, same-day, within five business days would bring this brand: “Pattern day trader”—keep twenty-five grand.

In April, FINRA voted the rule goodbye; by June, the smaller accounts could trade sky-high. Bloomberg’s Simone Foxman saw volumes soar as Robinhood and Webull welcomed more.

A market maker sells at $50.25, then buys at $50.24 when trades arrive. The penny in between is called the spread; repeat the trade, repeat the toll instead.

Market makers pay brokers “for order flow”— the chance to meet those trades as buy orders go. So “zero commission” looks delightfully free, while spread after spread can charge invisibly.

Same-day options speed the spinning game; crypto makes the cycle much the same. Now brokerage accounts can take a sports bet too— a joke became a thing that people really do.

Peter Tuchman teaches retail trading and said the older limits spoiled the fun ahead. But Better Markets’ Benjamin Schiffrin warned instead: the line with gambling blurred as trading spread.

Of course you have a system, sharp and bright; your frequent trades will surely all be right! Yet if long-term wealth is why folks came, perhaps “more fun” was never quite the aim.

What happened? FINRA repealed the rule requiring margin-account customers with four or more day trades in five business days to maintain $25,000. Trading then surged at Robinhood and Webull, creating more chances for brokers and market makers to earn from order flow and bid-ask spreads.

STORY 4
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Prediction market indexes

The Index of Things That Might Happen

In June came a joke: “Take contracts galore—from Kalshi and Polymarket, one hundred or more. Average their prices; then proudly announce,‘Today, all probability managed to bounce!’”

An event contract has a price that can showhow likely traders think an outcome may go.An index groups prices in one tidy score,so changes across many bets are easier to explore.

The joke pictured newscasters, solemn and bright:“Prediction markets rose in today’s trading flight.”As though events had all climbed together somehow,and things were more likely to happen right now.

Then S&P Dow Jones Indices and Kalshi declareda multi-year partnership—the straightest of stares.They plan indexes for politics, weather, and sport,supply chains and AI’s effects on jobs of each sort.

But Kalshi sells broad bets and narrower ones:“Which party wins the Senate?” and state-by-state runs.An index might mix them—but which should count most?The whole Senate result, or each separate contest?

A trader could buy an index when its price looked small,then sell matching contracts whose prices looked tall.That’s index arbitrage: seek mismatched prices for gain—but here, broad bets already contain narrower bets in their chain.

So the joke came true, though not quite in the waythat “events became likelier” over the day.Instead, related forecasts may rise as a crew—and traders may trade the index against bets that overlap it, too.

What happened? S&P Dow Jones Indices and Kalshi announced a multi-year partnership to create indexes from Kalshi event contracts and enable trading tied to S&P DJI benchmarks. Because Kalshi lists overlapping broad and narrow contracts, those indexes could permit arbitrage between an index and several possible sets of underlying bets.
