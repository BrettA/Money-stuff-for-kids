IN MATT'S ARTICLE: “No victims”

The “No Victim” Challenge

Mark Walter’s TWG Global had insurers in tow, selling annuities with payments they’d owe. An annuity takes money today, then promises contracted returns it will pay.

Walter directed where that money would go. Through third-party lenders, the funds seemed to flow—then circled to businesses tied to TWG, including sports-team purchases, surprisingly.

Loans to your boss are “affiliated,” you see, and strict rules require them labeled clearly. Yet after subpoenas, the insurers disclosed more than $20 billion in loans that had missed that sign—loans tied to Walter’s own business line.

TWG said Group 1001’s assets performed well; no policyholder had lost money, as far as it could tell. “There is no victim here,” its statement declared. No one was harmed, TWG firmly shared.

But hidden risk can change what a fair bargain pays: higher returns, lower prices, or safer ways. Clear Spring had over forty-five percent tied to Walter-related ventures, a complaint would state; for Gainbridge, nearly sixteen percent shared that fate.

Then retiree Rick Phillips brought a class-action claim, reported by The Wall Street Journal by name. He asked for refunds or escape without fee, saying honest disclosure would have changed the price, possibly.

Say “no victims” loudly when filings were wrong, and plaintiffs’ lawyers may soon come along. TWG’s confident sentence became quite the call: lawyers took it as a challenge to find victims after all.

What happened? Clear Spring and Gainbridge allegedly underreported affiliated loans connected to Mark Walter’s businesses, making their annuities appear less risky than they were. Rick Phillips sued for the better price or terms policyholders might have demanded if the loans had been disclosed.

IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Elsewhere in annuities

Sabrina and the Shrinking Slice

Sabrina Elliott inherited some pay, And placed $40,000 away. Her son-in-law helped her choose Aviva’s plan: “Stock-market gains, with no loss,” it began.

Her money was locked up for twelve years’ time, While part tracked the S&P 500’s climb. At first, sixty percent joined that stock-index quest, Minus a 2.9-point spread; one percent grew the rest.

But Aviva could change what her policy paid, And later, at Athene, the share was remade. In year seven, fifty-five percent could take part; By year eleven, forty-five got a start.

Her yearly return was 3.7 percent in the end— More than Treasurys’ 1.9 percent trend. But the S&P itself earned thirteen percent a year, So “linked to the market” was not quite as near.

The long lockup gave asset managers room To fund private-credit loans that took time to bloom. They could buy harder-to-sell securities, too, While knowing when customer payments came due.

Larry Rybka of Valmark explained the neat trick: If investments did poorly, lower caps helped them stick. And if investments did wonderfully? Lower caps then— The insurer could fatten its profits again.

So bad times brought cushion, and good times did too; A marvelously helpful arrangement—for who? Sabrina got gains, but a much smaller slice, While the company kept that adjustable device.

What happened? Sabrina Elliott put $40,000 into a 12-year indexed annuity begun by Aviva and later managed by Athene, which reduced the portion of her money tied to S&P 500 gains from 60% to 55% and then 45%. Such adjustable caps let insurers pay customers less while investing their locked-up money in assets such as private credit and keeping more of the return.

Martin Shkreli and the Ban That Covered Almost Everything

The FTC said Shkreli and Vyera had tried To keep Daraprim’s rivals locked outside. The medicine saved lives; the scheme blocked competition, So in 2022, a judge imposed a lifetime prohibition.

No pharmaceutical stocks could he acquire or hold, No guiding a drug company, subtle or bold. Even public remarks could violate the command If meant to influence how a company was run or planned.

After almost seven years in prison for defrauding investors, Shkreli was released in May 2022. Then YouTube, X, and Reddit heard plenty from him anew. “I’m showing you how to trade and invest in biotech,” he said, While livestreaming trades and predicting which trials lay dead.

He shorted biotech stocks, betting prices would drop: Sell borrowed shares first, then buy back after the flop. But did that count as shares he acquired or possessed? A short is like owning a negative amount—what a test!

The FTC and seven states said he’d broken the ban, And started contempt proceedings against the Pharma Bro man. “Contempt of court” can be merely a legal description— But here, actual contempt did not seem like a fiction.

The strangest fight was no longer just medicine or stock; It was whether a lifetime industry ban could make Shkreli stop. The FTC’s message, beneath every filing and citation: Please quit being so loudly involved with pharmaceutical corporations.

What happened? The FTC and seven states alleged that Martin Shkreli violated his lifetime pharmaceutical-industry ban by trading biotech stocks and broadcasting opinions meant to affect pharmaceutical companies. They asked the court to hold him in contempt, though whether short selling counts as acquiring or holding stock remains an unusually tricky part of the dispute.

IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Are sports bets illegal?

The Sports Bet in Two Different Coats

Kalshi sold contracts on which sports team would win: Buy “Yes” or buy “No,” and the trading begins. “These contracts are swaps under commodities law, so federal CFTC rules govern them all.”

That federal label could take states off the case, letting eighteen-year-olds trade in a place where ordinary sports betting was banned by the state. New Jersey replied, “That’s a sports wager—wait!”

Three appeals courts split: states won two; Kalshi won one, so what should courts do? New Jersey asked the Supreme Court to hear, while the NFL and Gary Gensler stood near.

But calling bets “swaps” caused a troublesome thought: Retail swaps must trade where federal rules say they ought. Then state-regulated sportsbooks, for fifteen whole years, should all have been breaking the law—without anyone’s fears!

The CFTC quickly drew a new line. Chairman Michael S. Selig said casino bets weren’t derivatives this time. They were “casino-style gambling,” excluded from swaps— while Kalshi’s sports contracts stayed in the federal box.

Was Kalshi casino-style? No, no, look away! Even how odds were displayed could decide what was okay. “Seahawks beat Broncos: 59%” could appear; but the equal “-144” moneyline was too bookmaker-like here.

Same teams, same prediction, same Thursday-night chance— but one wore a market coat, one wore casino pants.

What happened? The CFTC issued an interim rule excluding casino-style sports wagers from the swap definition while continuing to treat Kalshi’s sports event contracts as federally regulated swaps. This avoided implying that ordinary off-exchange sportsbooks were illegal, even though Kalshi’s 59% probability and a sportsbook’s -144 moneyline mean the same thing.

STORY 5
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Options

The Less-Silly Thing

The Wall Street Journal reported a spree: Individual-stock options were traded with glee. An option names both a price and a date, With rights to buy or sell before it’s too late.

Call options give buyers the right to buy shares; Their prices topped 2021’s meme-stock affairs. Carrick Lane measured the stocks in the spree: Some $700 billion each day, notionally.

“Notional” counts all the stock under claim, Not merely the option fees paid in the game. The trading looked lively, enormous, and fast— Like meme-stock excitement returning at last.

Vlad Tenev of Robinhood Markets Inc. had a thought On Bloomberg’s Odd Lots about what could be bought. Why bar retail buyers from private-company stock When sillier purchases circle the block?

You can order Amazon trinkets that shrink in their worth, Or buy meme coins—hardly the safest on Earth. So why keep OpenAI and SpaceX away If money can purchase much dumber stuff every day?

His argument’s master key sounded like this: “Some choices are worse than the choice you dismiss. If people may freely buy sillier stuff, Then surely this less-silly thing’s safe enough.”

What happened? Individual-stock options trading reached about $700 billion in daily notional volume, while call-option prices exceeded 2021 meme-stock-era levels, according to Carrick Lane. Robinhood CEO Vlad Tenev argued that retail investors should be allowed to buy private companies such as OpenAI or SpaceX because they may already buy depreciating Amazon trinkets and meme coins.
