STORY 1
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Deal list leak

The Pipeline That Spilled

At Morgan Stanley, a banker prepared A client update, polished and pared. But instead of the version meant to go out, The internal file took the wrong route.

More than one hundred possible deals filled the sheet, Across Asia, with other regions complete. China, South Korea, and India appeared, With funds that might back them and projects once steered.

Some companies might offer shares through an IPO— Their first public sale, if the plans were a go. Some might do block trades: big bundles of stock. Some deals were on hold; some were merely in talk.

The file held information that could move a price, So Bloomberg News checked it and read it twice. Would shares on the list sink under new supply? One could short those stocks and let the theory fly—

Then buy similar companies not named on the page, And compare both groups over a six-month stage. Yet a pipeline may hold every hope in the shop: A mandate, a pitch, or a call still on top.

Link REIT of Hong Kong was named in the stack, But answered with facts that pushed firmly back: “No current transaction with Morgan Stanley,” it said, “And no recent deal talks.” The pipeline sprawled ahead.

What happened? A Morgan Stanley employee accidentally sent a client an internal pipeline listing more than 100 deals being pitched or monitored, including potentially price-sensitive IPO and block-trade information. Link REIT said it had no current transaction or recent deal discussions with Morgan Stanley, illustrating that pipeline entries are not necessarily real assignments.

STORY 2
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Force majeure

Project Jupiter and the Too-Few Trouble

Bloomberg’s Sridhar Natarajan, Brody Ford, and Paula Seligson reported the bind: Project Jupiter was huge and planned—but one crucial permit was declined.

In New Mexico, a Blue Owl Capital unit was developing the site, with Oracle as its main tenant and AI computing its might. The center was expected to be ready in 2028, but opposition and regulatory setbacks threatened that date.

It was designed for 2.45 gigawatts—a mighty power flow, roughly what 1.8 million homes would use with lights aglow. Yet a key permit for its energy plans was firmly denied, so Oracle sent a force majeure notice to the developer’s side.

Force majeure can excuse or delay promised duties when certain events beyond a party’s control cause difficulties. Oracle wasn’t trying to leave; it wanted room to wait before making payments if the project missed its opening date.

The lenders did not cheer. The project’s debt was trading below ninety cents per dollar as its value kept degrading. A Quinn Emanuel alert said such clauses may decide the disputes when building and regulatory troubles collide.

The AI boom has an absurdity, bewildering but true: The future fear is “far too many”; today’s risk is “too few.” If billions fund a center that never opens its door, someone bears the loss—and the contract says who pays for it.

What happened? Oracle, the main tenant of Project Jupiter, sent the developer, a Blue Owl Capital unit, a force majeure notice seeking to postpone payments if setbacks keep the New Mexico data center from opening in 2028. Because lenders financed the project using expected lease payments, delays could threaten repayment; its debt was already trading below 90 cents on the dollar.

STORY 3
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: PE gloom

The Banker Who Stayed Put

Once, daring bankers would leap from their chairs, To hunt for cheap companies nobody cared for downstairs. They borrowed to buy them—called leverage—and then Improved how they worked and sold them again.

The earliest buyers found bargains galore, But soon private equity funds crowded the door. They bid prices higher and traded around, Till funds bought from funds, with few bargains still found.

Young banking analysts joined the parade: Two years making pitch decks, then off for an upgrade! They interviewed fiercely for funds big and grand, Choosing the safest, most popular plan.

But interest rates rose, so reselling grew tough, And returning investors their cash became rough. “Carry”—a share of the profit from wins— Could not be paid out when the selling thinned.

Bloomberg’s Allison McNeely described the slowdown; Jonathan Goldstein saw stalled careers all around. At Heidrick & Struggles, he watched job hunts start, While banking grew busy and looked rather smart.

Alan Johnson of Johnson Associates said with surprise, Private equity now looked like a lottery prize. For the first time in ten years or possibly more, Some fund workers eyed banking’s steadier door.

So the analyst staying a third year behind— The choice all the crowd had been eager to decline— Was suddenly daring, contrarian, and shrewd: The banker stayed banking—and bought undervalued… banking!

What happened? Private equity firms faced fewer cheap targets, tougher competition, higher borrowing costs, and difficulty reselling companies, so workers received less carried interest. As investment banking boomed, remaining at a bank for three years became the risky contrarian choice—the very kind of undervalued bet private equity claims to seek.

STORY 4
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Banks are where money isn’t

The Credit Cards That Got a Second Look

JPMorgan Chase had a card puzzle to face: Its partners wanted more shoppers embraced. United, Amazon, Marriott too— More approved cards meant more spending to do.

But JPMorgan funds cards with deposits kept safe, So it checks every borrower and watches each case. If a shopper might struggle to pay what they owe, The bank may decide that the answer is no.

The partners liked “yes”—and here was the fun: They gained when folks spent, but lost none if they’d run. The lender would carry the unpaid-card cost, So the bank stayed cautious while merchants stayed crossed.

Then JPMorgan studied a “second-look” plan: Could private-credit funds take the risks that banks ban? It contacted more than a dozen to see; Blue Owl, Blackstone, KKR, and Sixth Street heard the plea.

Those funds use investors whose money stays put, And seek higher interest where risk is afoot. They could approve some applications declined, Bearing possible losses for gains they might find.

JPMorgan—the largest U.S. card issuer by purchase volume—also has major co-brand partnerships and was preparing to take over the Apple Card. The odd solution was simple: when the careful bank said “no,” a risk-hungry fund might say “yes.”

What happened? JPMorgan Chase explored letting private-credit firms take a second look at rejected co-branded credit-card applications. The private-credit firms, rather than JPMorgan’s deposit-funded bank, would fund approved cards and bear their credit losses.

STORY 5
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Some light insider trading

The Baker’s Egg-Price Puzzle

Kalshi made an animated ad with a curious display: Bakers could bet where egg prices might go each day. But bakers may see private cost updates at work, Which gives that bright invitation a legally gray quirk.

At a public baking company, a daily report Might show eggs getting costly while forecasts fall short. If a baker shorts company stock—betting shares will decline— Using that secret work news likely crosses the legal line.

But what if the baker buys an egg-price contract instead, Expecting the cost to climb farther ahead? Companies use commodity futures to hedge what they pay, Protecting their budgets when prices jump the wrong way.

No standard egg futures are listed on U.S. exchanges today. But Kalshi, regulated by the U.S. Commodity Futures Trading Commission, offers yes-or-no event contracts tied to egg prices. They can be used to wager or hedge in a futures-like way.

For a worker’s personal trade, the answer grows hazier: Did they misuse company facts, or simply predict eggs crazier? Kalshi’s own rule is stricter: material news not yet public may bar Anyone with access from trading—however careful they are.

Was one daily egg report important enough to bring That rule into play? Maybe. That is the sting: Kalshi’s cheerful baker ad seemed to invite The very egg-price trade its own rules might indict.

What happened? Money Stuff noted that Kalshi advertised egg-price contracts to bakers who might possess private workplace information about egg costs. Such personal trading is legally ambiguous, while Kalshi’s rules can prohibit anyone with material nonpublic information from trading.

STORY 6
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Medicaid arbitrage

The Fifty-Eight-Dollar Work Requirement

At AEI, Kevin Corinth examined a rule: “Work” for Medicaid had a curious tool. Starting next year, the mark is eighty hours, though reported income carries special powers.

Show $580 from one month’s pay, within any six-month period, and you’ve met it that way. And gross gambling winnings count as income too, even if a second wager leaves a loss for you.

His example used football at minus one-ten: Bet $638 on the Patriots, then bet $638 on the Jaguars as well, each against the spread when the kickoff bell fell.

One wager wins $580; the other loses $638. That leaves a $58 loss—but the income still counts. So gambling could satisfy the work rule on paper, and buy six months’ coverage through one strange caper.

The football bets make the result seem funny, but gambling itself isn’t the key to the money. The odd part is this: report $580 as income, and the rule may say “work requirement done.”

What happened? Kevin Corinth showed that paired football bets could generate $580 of gross gambling income while losing $58 overall. Because that reported income can satisfy the Medicaid work requirement for a six-month period, the person could meet the rule without actually working eighty hours per month.
