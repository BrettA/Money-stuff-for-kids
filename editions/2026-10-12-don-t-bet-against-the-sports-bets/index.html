The Bet About Whether the Bets Are Bets

Kalshi lists sports contracts with answers “Yes” or “No”— Will a team beat the spread? Where will the next score go? It calls each contract a “swap,” federally overseen, By the CFTC—not each state’s gaming team.

“Sports can affect business,” says Kalshi’s legal view: “A playoff bar may sell more drinks, so economic effects come through.” Under Dodd-Frank’s broad words, federal rules would win, And state betting regulators could not step in.

Two appeals courts backed the states; one let Kalshi prevail. New Jersey asked the Supreme Court to tip the legal scale. Thirty-nine states and the National Football League joined the fight, Saying sports bets don’t become swaps by changing the label just right.

Then Joseph Grundfest proposed a wonderfully puzzling plot: Let Kalshi offer bets on whether Kalshi’s bets are lawful—or not! Could Kalshi buy “Yes” to make its courtroom chances climb? Or bet against itself and hedge its risk at the very same time?

Gary Gensler spotted an even larger surprise: If every sports bet is a swap in federal eyes, Then off-exchange retail sports bets have been illegal since 2012— A $165 billion elephant in a Dodd-Frank mousehole on the shelf.

What happened? Kalshi has not listed the proposed contract about whether its own sports contracts are legal. The Supreme Court may decide whether CFTC regulation of those contracts overrides state gaming laws, while critics warn that Kalshi’s theory would also make ordinary off-exchange sports betting federally illegal.

What happened? Xeal announced plans to install Nvidia AI server racks beside underused EV chargers and power them through electrical capacity it had already secured. This could bring AI computing online in months instead of waiting years for a new grid connection.

The Traders’ Convention With No Losing Panel

For two days each September, a crowd came to meet At Robinhood’s conference, where traders swapped tips. In cities like Houston, Las Vegas, Miami, They studied risky trades and discussed their strategy.

Sara Brescia O’Rear once worked in a pharmacy; Investing let her retire in her forties. “The trend will go up and go right,” she believed, And risk could pay off when a trade was achieved.

In 2024, Colin Edsman took a daring position: Half of his savings backed United States Antimony’s mission. Its shares cost less than one dollar apiece; A share is a small piece whose value may increase.

The mineral provider’s stock climbed over the year, Reaching double-digit prices—and Colin could cheer. “I’ve never relied upon luck,” Colin said. “I figured I’d make my own fortune instead.”

But where was the panel for losses gone wrong— For traders whose bad bets were painful and long? They might come for tips, or insist they had won, Or brag, “I lost most!” as if losing were fun.

Yet no biggest-losers panel appeared on the plan, Though surely some trader could headline that span. At a meeting for risk-takers certain they’re clever, The champion of losing gets no speech whatsoever.

What happened? Colin Edsman put half his savings into shares of United States Antimony when they traded below $1, and the price rose into the double digits a year later. Robinhood’s conference celebrated active trading and successful risk-taking, but apparently offered no panel featuring the year’s biggest losing traders.
