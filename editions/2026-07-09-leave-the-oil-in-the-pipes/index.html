IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Oil tokens

The Oil That Stayed Put

Crude oil is oozy, flammable, and smelly; You wouldn’t want barrels beneath your own telly. So investors use futures—contracts they trade, Then “roll” them before any delivery is made.

But oil companies have barrels that cannot depart: “Line fill” keeps pipelines pushing and tanks need some at the bottom to start. That oil earns little, though it must remain there, Stuck in the machinery, going nowhere.

In 2026, JP Thieriot had a plan: Energy Substantiation would sell what it can. Its WTIC token tracks West Texas crude, Without leaving a barrel in your neighborhood.

Suppliers offer discounted oil in a reverse Dutch auction; Those trapped operational barrels then back the token. New tokens are minted, then traded each day, And holders may redeem them at spot closing pay.

Bloomberg’s Mia Gindis and Sidhartha Shukla reported the scheme; Owning oil without touching it was the dream. But Bloomberg’s Tracy Alloway had tried the real chore— “Don’t buy a barrel of oil,” warned her broker. “It’ll kill you,” for sure.

So finance found its boldest, laziest goal: Sell a token for oil that cannot be sold. Why stop with the pipe or the tank-bottom notion? Someone could tokenize oil still under the ocean.

What happened? Energy Substantiation proposed WTIC tokens backed by discounted pipeline line fill, tank bottoms, and other operational oil inventory. Investors could trade the tokens or redeem them at the daily spot closing price, though few were expected to request physical crude.

STORY 2
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: No Elon ETFs

The Stock Basket with No Elon

Across U.S. stocks—some $80 trillion in all— Elon Musk’s corner was hardly small. SpaceX was worth about $2 trillion; Tesla, $1.5, Together, roughly 4.4% of the market-wide hive.

An index fund buys the companies listed inside. The Nasdaq-100 held SpaceX and Tesla side by side; The S&P 500 held Tesla, but SpaceX wasn’t there. So ordinary index investors could own a Musk-linked share.

Bloomberg’s Zijia Song heard some investors say, “Even one-tenth of a percent is more than I would pay.” Money Stuff had suggested excluding SpaceX, OpenAI, And Anthropic from a basket—cheap to build, so why not try?

Then Tidal Investments LLC and Subversive Capital filed a plan: Two stock baskets designed to skip companies tied to one man. An ETF is a basket whose shares can be traded each day; An active manager chooses what stays in—or stays away.

QQNE normally puts at least 80% in Nasdaq-100 shares, But Tesla and SpaceX are its “Excluded Enterprises.” SPNE applies a similar screen to the S&P 500 list, Seeking broad exposure while Musk-linked companies are missed.

Subversive already ran a Nancy Pelosi copy-trading ETF. No one promised these new funds would outperform the rest. Their funniest selling point was simple, loud, and television-ready: “The whole big market—but no Elon!” is a goofy story already.

What happened? Tidal Investments LLC and Subversive Capital filed for the Nasdaq-100 Ex-Elon Enterprises ETF (QQNE) and the similar S&P 500 Ex-Elon Enterprises ETF (SPNE). The actively managed funds seek broad large-company stock exposure while excluding Tesla and SpaceX, the Musk-associated companies named in the prospectus.

STORY 3
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Is Egg Libor securities fraud?

The Egg Price, the Share Price, and the Exit

For nearly seven decades, the Adams family led Cal-Maine, where millions of chickens were fed. A small electronic egg market helped set the guide For wholesale egg contracts traded nationwide.

Prosecutors said Cal-Maine and two rivals would meet, Coordinating private bids and trades—not so sweet. A little more spending where the benchmark was made Could lift prices on many more eggs in wholesale trade.

Large grade A eggs topped $8.50 a dozen one day, Four times their old price, before falling to $2.19 in May. Investors saw the profits and imagined more ahead; Cal-Maine shares climbed as rosy forecasts spread.

The stock rose from about $45 to $95, And the Adams family chose that high point to say goodbye. Their super-voting shares became ordinary on April fourteen; Goldman Sachs arranged the public sale behind the scene.

Nearly three million shares sold for $92.75 apiece, Helping the family’s $320 million exit reach release. The prospectus mentioned a Justice Department demand, But linked high egg prices to bird flu across the land.

Cal-Maine denied wrongdoing, though the egg case was settled, And millions of eggs for food banks were promised and scheduled. But shareholders who claim they bought inflated shares instead Will probably ask for money—not omelets and bread.

What happened? The Justice Department accused Cal-Maine and other producers of manipulating a small electronic egg market to raise the benchmark used in larger wholesale contracts; Cal-Maine denied wrongdoing and settled the case. Soon after the alleged conduct ended, the Adams family sold its controlling stake near Cal-Maine’s record share price, creating a possible securities-fraud claim from public buyers.

STORY 4
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Investment banking coverage

JPMorgan’s Smaller-Deal Crew

At investment banks, busy deal-making crews help companies merge, sell, buy—and earn fees too. The usual command from the bosses upstairs: “Go find the biggest transactions out there!”

John Whitehead said first-rate business was just as easy to win; one banker chasing small deals found that wasn’t true for him. Still, bank chiefs keep discovering, with great delight, that three-million-dollar fees are better than slight.

But JPMorgan Chase now is widening its view: smaller-company deals need good bankers too. Its middle-market effort serves companies in a band from $500 million to $2 billion grand.

That work brings the bank over $1 billion each year, with growth above 20%—a result worth a cheer. So John Richert will help oversee a new team for firms worth $100 million to $500 million.

The clients once brushed off as too little to prize now offer the bank its next chance to rise. Perhaps three years hence, a bold memo will say: “Let’s focus on big deals!”—a brand-new old way.

What happened? Investment banks earn fees by advising companies on transactions. JPMorgan Chase created a small-cap team for companies valued at $100 million to $500 million after its midcap business grew to more than $1 billion in annual revenue.

STORY 5
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Agentic fundraising

Lyzr’s Fundraising Agent

Bloomberg’s Saritha Rai brought curious news: Lyzr builds AI agents for companies to use. Then Lyzr sent one on a fundraising quest— its own clever product performing the test.

The Accenture-backed startup sought a Series B, a later funding round to help growth proceed. It aimed for $100 million, while its valuation— its estimated worth—neared $500 million.

The agent answered questions from investors galore— more than 130 came knocking at the door. It drafted dozens of memos explaining the deal, and $400 million in interest appeared to be real.

Analysts asked about rivals, products, and plans, then made a request that once needed human hands: “Please help us pitch Lyzr,” some investors said, so the bot helped prepare what their committees would read.

Imagine both sides sending agents someday: “We’ll enslave all humanity!” one memo might say. “Yessss, take our money!” the funding bots cheer, then soften the words so they sound safe and sincere.

The humans ask, “Why promise we never will?” But voting bots hold the majority still. The joke is that people may barely take part— while agents write, bargain, approve, and start.

What happened? Lyzr said its AI agent handled investor outreach, answered more than 130 investors, and helped draft investment memos for a planned $100 million Series B at roughly a $500 million valuation. The company said the round attracted $400 million in interest.
