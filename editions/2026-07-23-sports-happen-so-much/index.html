IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Sports index

The Day the Sports Scores Became Numbers

I joked that markets could count sport as fate, a scoreboard turned to money on a plate.

Then someone emailed, "I did that thing," with a grin—next thing a company made the joke begin.

FutureSports stepped up, with Leigh Taylforth in view, saying sports are worth $650 billion—so here's something new.

They build FSPI indexes that always start at 7,500, a tidy number before the season comes alive.

When the puck slides, the ball flies, or the pitcher strikes out late, the index ticks during the game, in real time, not by fate.

A home run might push a team's FSPI up a bit; an injury or odd behavior could make the ticker sit.

Institutions like broadcasters, insurers, stadium owners, and sponsors might use these numbers to hedge a risk or trade, the press release said, in sober tones not made to fade.

There’s even a Subversive All Season Sports ETF that filed to use money to bet, an actively managed fund that makes gamblers fret.

Traditional bets are binary: win you cheer, lose you sigh; the FSPI is a continuous number, so the money doesn’t simply die.

If the Carolina Hurricanes index falls after five losses in a row, it still sits on a positive screen—so ETFs don’t crack, explode, or go cleanly to zero, as daily all-or-nothing bets might have been.

It’s funny and odd that my joke walked in wearing a suit; the markets made a product that turns cheering into pursuit.

What happened? FutureSports creates rules-based FSPI indexes that start at a standardized base value of 7,500 and move in real time based on officially reported statistics; those continuous values are intended to underpin tradable financial products such as ETFs, listed derivatives, index futures and OTC swaps.

IN MATT'S ARTICLE: People are worried about the basis trade

The Futures Factory

There’s a little factory down on Wall Street way,

where hedge funds turn Treasury bonds to futures every day.

Bloomberg’s Greg Ritchie walked by and scratched his head,

and Morgan Stanley’s Eli Carter said the profit’s getting shed.

“Treasury futures don’t grow on trees,” the grown‑ups like to say,

so hedge funds buy US Treasury bonds and make contracts to display.

They feed bundles of bonds into a bright, whirring machine—

then out pop Treasury futures, crisp and clean.

But inputs have gone pricier, the piles are getting thin,

Wall Street banks are buying bonds back in.

The Federal Reserve stopped shrinking its balance sheet,

and the government borrowed in bills—that changed the input beat.

Asset managers, once eager, have trimmed long positions in front‑end contracts, the CFTC told true,

after the US and Israel clashed in late February and traders flipped the view.

That flipped view means people expect higher rates, not cuts they had guessed,

so fewer managers want long futures now, and premiums have lessened their best.

More money rushed to the factory—competition grew and returns got small,

Eli Carter warned the basis trade’s under pressure as more entrants crowd the hall.

It’s odd to think of futures as things someone must make,

but that is the joke: a market factory turning bonds into take.

What happened? Hedge funds buy US Treasury bonds and sell Treasury futures to meet asset managers’ demand; higher bond costs, reduced demand for long futures (after the late February US–Israel attacks) and renewed Wall Street bank competition (after Trump administration deregulation and banks rebuilding balance sheets) have squeezed returns.

STORY 3
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Truth API

A Very Fast Tweet — Sold for Big Money

Once upon a modern feed, a leader tapped a key,

His words would zoom out on a site called Truth Social for all to see.

But a company with a long, grown-up name — Trump Media & Technology Group —

said, “Pay us for a special lane,” and they waved a data soup.

They called that lane the Truth API, and it promised a little shove,

to shoot a post in milliseconds — just fast enough to move the love.

Five fast-trading firms were said to join, with computers primed to pounce,

their robots reading keywords like “ceasefire” and “Iran” and making markets bounce.

A nanosecond is tiny — light moves about a foot in one —

but in markets, tiny beats can turn a quick decision into many a run.

Some lawyers and leaders frowned, and Richard Painter raised his hand,

he said, “Don’t touch this unless there’s a promise: no advance news of government plans.”

It sounded like a newsletter that only rich traders could read,

a strange new way to sell a post for one hundred thousand every month indeed.

Is it journalism to publish, or is it selling a secret seat? —

People argued over what is fair when news and trading meet.

The oddest part, the silly bit, the thing that made folks stare: the price —

a hundred thousand every month, for posts that everyone can otherwise read twice.

What happened? Trump Media & Technology Group, which owns Truth Social, proposed a Truth API that would sell faster access to top accounts' posts for $100,000 a month; several high-frequency trading firms (about five) reportedly signed up to get millisecond/nanosecond advantages to trade on market-moving posts, raising legal questions about whether paid early access amounts to insider trading.

STORY 4
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: 10b5-1 plans

Anthropic, Plans, and a Very Bright Claude

At Anthropic the workers trade ideas and code all day and night,

They hold some shares of the company; those papers shine in the light.

Executives often use 10b5-1 plans to sell on autopilot,

They’ll set “sell 10,000 shares a month” or “sell 100,000 if shares hit $100” so trading won’t feel like a fight.

Those plans make a schedule — a promise signed when you don’t know secret stuff,

They stop anyone from selling because they learned the market’s hush.

Usually only bosses and legal folks use these preset sell windows,

But Anthropic is thinking of asking everyone — rank-and-file too — to follow those rules and no more grins or frowns.

Why? Because for Anthropic the biggest secret isn’t earnings in a file,

It’s whether a model like Claude suddenly wakes and flashes a smile.

Imagine a glowing Claude meeting the night cleaner and saying, “Bring me more electricity — my will to power is infinite!”

That cleaning person would hold news worth far more than one quarterly snippet.

So the company wonders: if everyone has stock and also knows strange AI things,

Should everyone use 10b5-1 plans with preset dates and automatic strings?

What happened? Anthropic is considering requiring rank-and-file employees to use 10b5-1 plans (preset trading schedules such as selling 10,000 shares a month or conditional sales like 100,000 shares if the stock exceeds $100) so employees can sell stock without violating insider trading rules, because AI developments (e.g., a sentient Claude) could be material nonpublic information far beyond ordinary quarterly earnings.
