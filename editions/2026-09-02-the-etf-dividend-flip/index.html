IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Dividend shuffle

The Dividend Hopscotch

Each quarter, two funds gathered dividends galore from S&P 500 companies by the score. BlackRock called its fund IVV; Vanguard named its own VOO, you see.

For many foreign owners, a dividend’s taxed: Thirty percent may be withheld when it’s passed. But price appreciation gets different treatment— so investors devised a well-timed movement.

Since 2024, BlackRock’s dividend date has arrived before Vanguard’s, opening a gate. Before IVV reached its ex-dividend day, investors sold it and bought VOO right away.

They still tracked the S&P 500 throughout, but skipped IVV when its cash rights ran out. Then VOO—with its dividend coming—priced high; IVV—without one—was cheaper to buy.

So they sold VOO and switched back once more, keeping the price difference left on the floor. It acted like dividend money in every economic way, but for taxes, it was price appreciation that day.

Bloomberg’s Zachary Mider and Denitsa Tsekova found this quarterly hopscotch sent savings around: About $147 million in US tax in one year— all because two nearly matching funds paid on dates that weren’t near.

What happened? Foreign investors switched between BlackRock’s IVV and Vanguard’s VOO around their different ex-dividend dates, continuously holding S&P 500 exposure while receiving the dividend’s economic value as price appreciation instead of a taxable dividend. Bloomberg estimated that the strategy saved them about $147 million in US taxes in one year.

The Billable-Hour Tower Meets AI

Clients at Big Law sought partners renowned, for judgment when giant disputes came around. A top partner might earn $20 million a year, yet the bill-by-the-hour math looked rather queer.

At $4,000 hourly, to bill all that pay, she’d need 5,000 hours tucked into one year— about 100 each week! What a tiring feat. So associate hours helped make totals complete.

Ten partner hours were $40,000 on the sheet; two hundred junior hours made the bill complete. At $1,000 each, they added $200,000 more— so $240,000 came through the door.

Associates researched and reviewed every page. Their billed rates exceeded the cost of their wage. That “leverage model” helped partner profits grow, though clients prized senior judgment most in the show.

Then AI sped through contracts, research, and drafts. Would firms lose the hours that supported their math? Eric Grossman of Morgan Stanley warned the Financial Times: the pay model was “extraordinarily unstable” in AI times.

Would rates jump to $25,000 because work became faster? Would “Legalina” bill token-hours after associates vanished? Could ChatGPT answer, leaving lawyers with zero? Or could one partner earn $200 million—a supercharged hero?

Morgan Stanley still valued top lawyers’ advice, but planned competitive bids and alternatives in price. Fixed fees could replace towers of hours on display, while clients asked why faster work should cost more to pay.

What happened? Big law firms traditionally increased partner profits by billing clients for many junior-associate hours at rates above the associates’ salary cost. As AI began doing that work faster, Morgan Stanley said it would seek competitive bids and arrangements such as fixed fees while continuing to pay for top lawyers’ judgment.

The Championship Hedge

A college football team raced for the crown, While coaches called plays and fans shook the town. The coach’s contract promised, “Win the big game, And a very large bonus may follow your name.”

A title could help the whole university grow: More students and donations, more merchandise on show. But rich alumni boosters might cover the pay— Perhaps $3 million on championship day.

Those boosters loved winning. They wanted first place! Still, millions departing could pinch their purse space. The school gained the TV and enrollment rewards; The boosters got joy—and the coach’s bill afterward.

On Kalshi, event contracts can pay if teams win, So trading there hinted at one curious spin: Buy contracts that pay when the championship lands, Then use those proceeds for the bonus demands.

Betting your team wins sounds less like defense Than doubling down proudly on team confidence. But if boosters owe money while schools get the gain, That backward-seeming wager may soften their pain.

What happened? Kalshi trading appeared to show that some college football programs may have used championship contracts to hedge coaches’ winning bonuses. One possible explanation is that alumni boosters owed bonuses of perhaps $3 million but did not share directly in the school’s added enrollment, TV, merchandise, and donation revenue.

The Billion-Dollar Coin Toss

In 2006, a finance blog fond of sport, Long or Short Capital, filed this report: Under “Johnny Debacle,” four steps appeared— Take risks with huge upside, however absurd or feared.

Be the one in five hundred million who wins; Credit hard work and genius when fortune rolls in. Say poorer competitors lacked your grand scheme; Then buy a mega-yacht or an athletic team.

Years later at Elm Wealth, the joke met a test: Victor Haghani, James White, and Jeffrey Rosenbluth asked what worked best. Their make-believe Billy had saved $2 million in all, From $10 million earned after taxes—a useful bankroll.

He had fifty years for a 500-fold climb, With fifty weighted coin flips allotted in time. Each flip offered heads six chances in ten; He could stake any fraction, then wager again.

Professor Leo Breiman’s 1961 result supplied the plot: All-in bets could maximize Billy’s billion-dollar shot. Seven percent reached $1 billion; the rest hit the floor— Ninety-three percent left Billy completely broke, with no “almost” shore.

At Jane Street, intern Sam Bankman-Fried bet on coins for real; Later, giant risks helped make him a billionaire with worldwide appeal. He kept making metaphorical flips, and then came the turn: Famous billionaire first, infamous criminal defendant in return.

He and his friends invested in Anthropic as well, Where vast wealth or ruin could follow each spell. The old joke endured when a daring bet hit: The lucky winner praised planning, hard work, and wit.

What happened? In Elm Wealth’s model, the strategy giving Billy a 7% chance of turning $2 million into $1 billion required repeated all-in bets, so the other 93% of outcomes ended with him broke. Sam Bankman-Fried likewise became a billionaire through huge risks, kept taking risks, and later became a criminal defendant.
