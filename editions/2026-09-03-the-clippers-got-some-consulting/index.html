The Clippers’ Very Expensive Trees

The NBA caps team pay near $165 million a year, So wealth alone can’t make the finest players all appear. One player’s top salary is roughly $58 million; The rules keep Steve Ballmer from outspending every billionaire in the pavilion.

Ballmer, Microsoft’s former chief, was worth $173.5 billion. Yet Kawhi Leonard’s pay could not simply climb toward a zillion. His manager and uncle, Dennis Robertson, sought another way: About $10 million each year in off-court deals and pay.

Gillian Zucker introduced Boingo, Lockton, and Daktronics too; They signed $18 million in endorsements with little work to do. The Clippers paid those companies unusual consulting fees, Which investigators said helped fund Leonard’s guarantees.

When the Clippers spent more on Daktronics’ scoreboard display, Daktronics added $2 million to Leonard’s second-year pay. “We’ll pay extra for the scoreboard; you pay our star,” was the gist— A Christmas present wearing a consulting-label twist.

Then Joseph Sanberg’s Aspiration promised Leonard quite a spree: $7 million cash plus $5 million stock each year, ostensibly. The Clippers planned $7 million yearly for the Forum’s carbon past— Though no one first calculated what those old emissions cast.

The Clippers didn’t seem to want the trees; Aspiration questioned Kawhi’s deal, But matching contracts moved the money in a tidy wheel. Pablo Torre reported it; the NBA investigated and imposed penalties. The Clippers vehemently rejected the findings and alleged irregularities.

What happened? The NBA concluded that the Los Angeles Clippers, Steve Ballmer, and Kawhi Leonard violated salary rules by arranging business payments that funded lightly demanding endorsement deals for Leonard, including matching $7 million annual payments involving Aspiration. The Clippers denied the findings.

The Votes Beside the Bitcoin Pile

Empery once made electric motorcycles for the road, then changed, in July 2025, what it owned. It became a DAT with $500 million in Bitcoin— a stock-market company built around the coin.

For a while, one crypto dollar sold as two in shares; then the premium vanished into thinner air. Empery traded below net asset value—its Bitcoin pile’s worth— so owners proposed the reverse trade: sell coins and buy shares first.

Tice Brown pushed for liquidation too. He told Bloomberg columnist Matt Levine that he wanted the Bitcoin company liquidated.

ATG Capital Opportunities Fund LP, led by Gabi Gliksberg, owned 16.3 percent, and named nine board candidates to change where Empery went.

Yet ATG shorted Bitcoin ETFs dollar for dollar beside its stake; a short can gain when prices fall, offsetting losses Bitcoin makes. The coin-price risk was canceled, but its Empery votes remained; it still stood to gain if the stock’s discount waned.

Empery’s board rejected the nominees: “That hedge was not disclosed.” ATG sued in Delaware, but the court said no: the bylaws covered hedges involving Empery stock, not crypto or unrelated ETFs—so the nine stayed on the ballot.

Was this empty voting—votes without Bitcoin cheer? Or did the hedge make Empery’s discount extra clear? ATG could steer a giant Bitcoin pile without wanting Bitcoin higher, while betting that the stock-price gap would finally expire.

What happened? ATG owned 16.3% of Empery while shorting Bitcoin ETFs dollar for dollar, removing Bitcoin-price risk but keeping its votes and exposure to Empery’s stock discount. A Delaware court held that Empery’s bylaws did not require disclosure of that hedge, so ATG’s nine nominees could stand for election.

Apollo investigated and praised the advice, And an admiring Paul Weiss email thought it rather nice. Then Bloomberg reporters Dylan Sloan, Tom Maloney, And Francesca Maglione asked: Was it truly one-of-a-kind—or phony?

Victoria J. Haneman inspected the plan: The structure looked mostly conventional, she began. Its price was the marvel—not some dazzling new way; Even many top firms might have charged less, she’d say.

Rutgers professor Jay Soled called its novelty “hogwash” outright: Mostly run-of-the-mill tactics, not tax-world delight. Epstein compared doubters to viewers of art who declare, “My child could do that!”—and the experts nearly went there.

What happened? Jeffrey Epstein received $158 million from Leon Black between 2012 and 2017 for estate and tax planning meant to minimize taxes. Epstein portrayed the work as a costly masterpiece, but tax professors said most of it was conventional and that the extraordinary feature was the fee.
