Meta’s Class C Shares That Never Say “Aye”

What happened? Meta proposed a 2-for-1-style distribution of zero-vote Class C shares, letting Zuckerberg sell some stock for a possible California wealth tax and letting Meta raise money for AI without immediately diluting his control. Because Zuckerberg already controlled about 61% of Meta’s votes, his approval made the required shareholder vote a done deal.

Anthropic’s Very Valuable Loss

Anthropic planned an IPO, with shares for public sale, But one line in its draft report made ordinary losses pale.

More than $80 billion lost in half a year! Yet most was not a mountain of cash that disappeared.

Convertible notes had helped it gather funding first; Their value climbed as Anthropic’s own valuation burst.

Accountants priced those promises again upon the books, And $72 billion became charges—stranger than it looks.

The higher Anthropic’s value climbed, the bigger grew the cost; By becoming more valuable, it booked a giant loss.

No ordinary business expense had caused that huge reverse: A loss from growing valuable—well, it could be worse!

What happened? Anthropic reported a net loss of more than $80 billion for the first half of the year, including $72 billion in accounting charges from revaluing convertible notes and other financing instruments as the company’s value rose.

The March for Onion Futures

At the University of Chicago, a curious crowd said, “Let onion futures be traded and allowed!” About thirty marched by the Loop’s busy streets to CME Group, where derivatives meet.

A futures contract sets tomorrow’s price today, helping farmers hedge when crop prices sway. Corn, wheat, and soy farmers may soften that risk, but onions were crossed from the government’s list.

In the 1950s, some traders cornered the crop; then the Onion Futures Act made onion contracts stop. Since 1958, the ban has stayed strong— though bets on weather and politics roll right along.

Adam Ash and For Our Futures joined the campaign. The Chicago Maroon explained who helped with the train: Kalshi paid for the bus, branded gear, and breakfast too, then promoted the protest for more people to view.

Kalshi CEO Tarek Mansour reposted the scene; its video passed one million views on the screen. Kalshi wants prediction markets on everything— and onions are things. So the march had its spring.

The protest looked quirky, free-market, and bold: students defending how onion crops might be sold. Then came the punch line behind all the fun: a betting-market company had sponsored the run.

What happened? Kalshi supported a protest by University of Chicago students and affiliates seeking repeal of the Onion Futures Act of 1958, which bans onion futures. Such contracts would let onion farmers lock in future prices and hedge against price changes, as farmers of crops such as corn, wheat, and soy can do.

“I didn’t know that people worked five days a week Until I went to Goldman Sachs,” Jackie would speak. “I just assumed everyone worked on Christmas,” she said, For that was the life her whole family had led.

He joked, “I’m the only one, surely it’s true, Who went to Goldman for lifestyle!” Then Jackie made two. Bloomberg’s Paige Smith found the better punch line: At Goldman, five working days seemed simply divine.

What happened? Jackie Reses grew up working extremely long weeks, so Goldman Sachs introduced her to the comparatively relaxed idea of working five days. She later helped Square become a bank and became CEO of Lead Bank, which supports major fintech companies.
