IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Happy Anthropic S-1 day

The Stock with Miracles—and Warnings

Anthropic PBC planned an IPO one day: the public could purchase small ownership shares and help fund its way. Its prospectus was the guide investors would read, with hopeful plans, financial facts, and warnings they might need.

SpaceX called its market $28.5 trillion wide. OpenAI wondered if money would matter on AGI’s far side. Anthropic’s founders made a gentler call: “Come help bring an age of miracles!”—almost modest, all in all.

But warning pages said advanced AI could grow too fast to safely test, control, or know. CEO Dario Amodei had written with dread: powerful AI might seize control—or help make all life disappear instead.

The prospectus tiptoed: “Concerns have been raised.” If robots ended humanity, that passive voice still amazed! It even said catastrophe could hurt Anthropic’s business results, as though poor quarterly numbers were the gravest results.

Bloomberg writers Tom Orlik, Michael Deng, and Dongmiao Zhang spotted the snare: “It may take your job, so buy a piece” was the pitch hiding there. A hedge is an investment that may soften another bad blow; if Claude took your work, perhaps Anthropic’s shares still would grow.

Join the new AI overlords, the writers joked with a shrug—then spend your profits building a bunker, safe and snug. The stranger Anthropic’s dangers appeared to be, the more owning Anthropic could seem like security.

What happened? Anthropic filed a draft prospectus for an IPO, offering investors shares while disclosing that advanced AI could displace jobs or pose catastrophic risks. The absurd pitch was that owning Anthropic might hedge against dangers created by Anthropic’s own products.

IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Anthropic governance

Five Classes and Seven Chairs

Anthropic trains frontier AI, where giant models grow; An IPO could raise the billions needed for the show. Though valued past a trillion, it declares this guiding plea: “Develop AI responsibly, for all humanity.”

Seven founders left OpenAI to take a safer track; Then built in checks upon themselves, should judgment ever crack. Five trustees, paid $400,000 yearly, cannot own a share; Their Long-Term Benefit Trust must keep humanity in its care.

One profitless Class T share names four board directors; Class F names three, with 50.1%—the founders’ vote protector. So money does not steer each seat or choose each final say; The people holding special shares can point the boardroom’s way.

If the nominating committee balks at the trust’s fourth seat, Employees use Class E to choose whom that director should be. Each six months worked earns one E share; each pledged A earns one more, So public spirit adds a voice when conflict reaches the floor.

Class A has value, one vote each, but founders still prevail; Class N gives Amazon and Google profits, not a voting trail. If N is sold, it generally changes into voting A— Big Tech can fund the mission, but cannot run the day.

Elon Musk has said at Tesla, “robot army” needs control; Here ownership and steering play a very different role. Anthropic can seek oceans of cash while keeping insiders about: A trillion-dollar workers’ co-op saying, “Buy in—but we won’t sell out!”

What happened? Anthropic created five stock classes separating economics from control: its trust appoints four of seven directors, founders effectively choose three, employees can resolve one kind of board dispute, ordinary shareholders receive economic rights, and Amazon and Google receive nonvoting economic stock. This lets Anthropic raise outside capital while mission-focused insiders retain control.

IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Fake polls

When Fake Polls Met the Election Cop

Since June, in Michigan, in Maine, and Kansas too, Wedgewood Polls released results that clashed with others’ view.

Then FiftyPlusOne asked, “Are all these numbers right?” By Tuesday, Wedgewood’s answer brought the trick to light: Almost every poll was fake, the team at last confessed. “An experimental project” was how they framed the test.

Aggregators had displayed them; The New York Times did too, On pages tracking close campaigns where readers sought a clue. Nate Silver said these polls did not just quietly blend in; They pushed a different story about who might lose or win.

Byrne Hobart saw a money twist inside the polling fuss: On Kalshi, traders buy contracts whose prices rise and fall. A winning contract pays if its named event occurs at all.

A fake Maine Senate poll could move averages a few, Then nudge Kalshi’s contract prices as traders changed their view. That might earn tens of dollars—perhaps even a bit more. But did Wedgewood trade that way? There’s no evidence for sure.

If someone spread false polls, then traded on the swing, That could be commodities manipulation—a serious thing. The CFTC had warned, “Don’t make us election cops!” It usually watched futures tied to prices, goods, and crops.

Yet election contracts turned campaigns into things that trade, So fake political information entered markets people made. A market built to gather truth can pay for fakes instead: Bad data enters, prices move, and doubt is what gets spread.

What happened? Wedgewood Polls admitted that almost all its published surveys were fabricated. Because election contracts trade on Kalshi, fake polls could affect their prices, potentially turning election misinformation into a commodities-market issue for the CFTC, though there is no evidence Wedgewood traded on its polls.
