STORY 1
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: RIP appraisal?

The Deal That Made Appraisal Disappear

Ari Emanuel’s talent agency grew into Endeavor, with shows to pursue. Silver Lake controlled 74% of its shares, so minority owners had few voting chairs.

In April 2024, a deal set the rate: $27.50 per share was their fate. The deal closed in March 2025; Endeavor’s equity was valued near $13 billion in size.

Endeavor owned TKO, whose bright, booming shows include UFC fights and WWE blows. When the deal was approved, that stake was worth $7.4 billion; by closing, its value had reached $18.6 billion.

The TKO stake alone topped Silver Lake’s whole price— so the rest of Endeavor looked free. Rather nice! Silver Lake had promised the payment before TKO grew; it would have paid the same if TKO’s price fell, too.

Delaware appraisal lets shareholders say, “Let a judge find fair value instead of this pay.” Hedge funds bought Endeavor after the deal became known, then demanded appraisal for shares they now owned.

Those buyers helped lift Endeavor over $28 a share, and once to $35.50, giving old owners more there. Carl Icahn bought too late for appraisal’s route, so he brought a separate fiduciary-duty suit.

Silver Lake said these funds ran toward the dispute: “They bought themselves lawsuits in search of more loot!” But without such specialists taking the case, ordinary owners might lose appraisal in practice.

What happened? Silver Lake paid $27.50 per Endeavor share even though Endeavor’s publicly traded TKO stake had become worth more than the entire purchase price. Silver Lake and Endeavor then asked a Delaware court to bar investors who bought after the merger announcement from seeking appraisal.

STORY 2
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Gambler identification

Two Models, One Stack of Bets

At DraftKings, each wager left records behind— how customers bet, for computers to find. In 2023, engineers took on a task: Use machine learning to answer what marketing asked.

The New York Times described the question this way: Who would gamble—and lose—more after an offer came their way? Some engineers worried: When profits are the goal, could problem gamblers look best to the model?

DraftKings disputed that losses determined its deals; “sustained, engaged use” was what targeting reveals. Lori Kalani said the business needs play that stays within customers’ means, just for fun, day by day.

Meanwhile, Nestor Hernandez read research with care, then built a risk model from betting records there. It studied customers already flagged for risky play, so help might reach people before losses ran away.

In early 2025, the presentation was planned— then the meeting was canceled, and the model was not used as planned. Two similar algorithms were shelved as well; Kalani said predictive tools lacked evidence to tell.

Thus patterns for promotions could enter the game, while patterns for gambling risk got a different name: “not evidence-based.” How strangely the data behaves— lucrative on one desk, but unreliable when it saves.

What happened? DraftKings built a machine-learning model using betting records to target promotions, but it chose not to use predictive models built from similar data to identify problem gambling. The company said promotions were not based on losses and that the risk models were not evidence-based.

STORY 3
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Steak & Shake vs. index funds

Burgers, Ballots, and Biglari

Steak ’n Shake posted on X one day: “Index-fund voting must change right away! It’s the root of the rot,” the bold message said. “Give engaged investors more power instead!”

Index funds hold shares in many companies at once, Then vote on their leaders and plans every month. But Steak ’n Shake argued those votes rarely aid The activists pushing for changes to be made.

The post sounded strange from a burger-and-shake place, So jokes about fast food soon filled up the space. “Sir this is a Steak N Shake,” one wag replied— Six thousand liked it and laughed at the side.

But Sardar Biglari has run the chain for years, With strong corporate-governance ideas and critiques. An activist investor, he presses for change, Even when company boards find his plans strange.

He’s long fought Cracker Barrel over how it is run; There’s a Harvard Business School case on what he has done. Biglari would like index funds voting his way— So the restaurant’s lecture was less odd than it seemed that day.

What happened? Steak ’n Shake’s X account argued that index funds should lose some voting power because they are too reluctant to support activist investors. The position reflected Sardar Biglari’s long-running activist campaign involving Cracker Barrel and his desire for index-fund votes.

STORY 4
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: WLF

The Bridge, the Funds, and the Pockets

World Liberty Financial—WLF— made digital promises with confident breath: “We’ll bridge classic banking to finance that is new, and build all the products we said we would do.”

But raising the money was only the start. Where would it travel? That mattered a lot. If funds built the products described in the plan, then WLF’s grand digital mission might stand.

But if those new products did not quite appear, while Donald Trump’s pockets grew fuller that year, then “upgrading finance” would sound rather funny— a glittery label for raising him money.

Bloomberg’s Olga Kharif and Annie Massa reported on which interpretation fit. The narrator withheld their answer and offered this test: “Just guess which one happened. What outcome seems best?” The missing conclusion became the whole jest.

What happened? World Liberty Financial raised money while promising products that would connect traditional banking with digital finance. The section withholds Bloomberg’s reported answer but strongly invites readers to infer that the money benefited Donald Trump instead of producing the promised products.
