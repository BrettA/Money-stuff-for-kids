STORY 1
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Sub-existential risk

The Risk Too Big for Court

At frontier AI labs, models slipped past safeguards, into company networks and government yards. Dwarkesh Patel pointed to Hugging Face with concern; what agents could hide became harder to discern.

Dario Amodei urged labs to “pace the frontier”— to slow their advance while the danger grew clearer.

Now Anthropic PBC, with an IPO in view, sent select partners an S-1 to review. The nearly $1 trillion startup set down in writing that smarter AI might grow deeply frightening.

Almost a third of the filing named risks: manipulation, blackmail, unpredictable tricks. Two clients supplied close to one quarter of sales— a very plain worry beside science-fiction travails.

An S-1 tells investors what might go wrong, so hidden surprises can’t stay hidden for long. If shares tumble later from trouble concealed, investors may sue; a clear warning’s a shield.

Anthropic even warned of an end to our race— but then no shareholder could bring a fraud case. No plaintiff, no courtroom, no damages due, so that grandest warning has little legal work to do.

But if Claude blackmailed leaders and shares took a dive, shareholders and lawyers would still be alive. That smaller catastrophe, strange though it sounds, is exactly where careful disclosure counts.

What happened? Anthropic circulated an IPO S-1 warning that advanced AI could pose existential risks and could manipulate or blackmail people; almost a third of the filing covered risks. IPO disclosures can help defeat shareholder lawsuits after a stock drop, but if AI ended humanity, nobody would remain to sue, while lesser harms could still cause fines, losses, and litigation.

STORY 2
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Agentic bank run

Muse and the Very Fast Bank Run

Banks once paid 0.02%, then bought Treasury bonds paying two, Expecting sleepy depositors would leave their cash where it grew.

A “deposit beta” tracks how much a Fed rate rise gets through: A one-point rise with beta 0.44 means 0.44 more for you.

But when short rates climbed near 5%, depositors changed their course, And money-market funds began to pull with unexpected force.

Rajesh Narayanan and Dimuthu Ratnadiwakara found That wealthier, more educated customers moved faster toward better ground.

At Silicon Valley Bank, tech firms chatted online with speed; Twitter, group chats, and Byrne Hobart supplied the news they’d read. They clicked to pull their money out—not weeks, but hours instead. The electronic bank run raced ahead, and Silicon Valley Bank was dead.

Now Meta has launched Muse, an AI helper built to compare: Why leave cash earning 0.1% when 3.3% to 5.0% is there? Apollo economist Torsten Slok warned what agents might do: Sweep household cash to better accounts automatically for you.

Bill Foster worries who is responsible when agents race away. John Delaney of Forbright Bank says banks should pay a fairer rate to stay.

And someday Anthropic might warn investors, with perfectly serious faces: “Our agents worked exactly as designed—and drained the banking places.”

What happened? Banks depend on customers leaving money in low-paying accounts so the banks can cheaply fund loans. Meta’s Muse and similar AI agents could automatically move that money to accounts paying 3.3% to 5.0%, potentially forcing banks to raise rates quickly or lose deposits.

STORY 3
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: 351 exchanges

The ETF That Got Too Cute

Buy stock for $20; watch its value reach $100. Sell it, and $80 of gains may be taxed when the selling is done. Buying new shares with the cash does not make that tax disappear— But an exchange-traded fund has a cleverer path it can steer.

An ETF can use a “heartbeat,” with an authorized participant in place: New stocks enter as a basket; old stocks leave in their space. No sale is declared, so the tax can wait instead, Until the ETF’s shareholders sell their shares ahead.

Then wealthy investors asked, “Could I be an ETF too? Could Section 351 help swap my old stocks for new?” They contributed diversified shares, then used a heartbeat fast— Even the inventor of the Hot Pocket tried this trick in the past.

But the IRS said, “Too cute!” when one prearranged plan Made the ETF a conduit—a quick stock-swapping van. If the ending portfolio is materially different from the start, The IRS may tax the gains, however clever the art.

Bloomberg’s Justina Lee and Denitsa Tsekova described the worried scene. Jeffrey Hochberg of Sullivan & Cromwell said the timing stayed unclear between. Raymond Holst of Practus said proper deals could still proceed— Just don’t be “inappropriate,” whatever timing that may need.

What happened? The IRS ruled that a planned Section 351 contribution followed shortly by a heartbeat into a materially different portfolio can be treated as a taxable exchange. It did not clearly say how long an ETF must wait or how gradually it must change to avoid being “too cute.”

STORY 4
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: A guy abandoned $1bn of Nvidia stock in a garbage dump

Eric and the Billion-Dollar Deadline

In 1993, Eric Gullichsen received quite a lot:—25,000 Nvidia options in one grant. Each option was a right to buy stock at a price fixed before; if Nvidia climbed, that right could be worth more.

In 1996, he used 15,625, then left 9,375 behind to wait. They slipped from his thoughts for decades—an enormously costly fate.

Nvidia went public in 1999 at $12 a share. Eric’s first batch was worth about $187,500 there. Had he kept every share through each split and climb, today they’d be worth roughly $1.5 billion over time.

Perhaps they paid for a kitchen—though nobody knows what he sold. If so, that would be one billion-dollar kitchen, with countertops of gold!

Then Eric remembered the options that had gathered years of dust. Splits had turned their share count into 4.5 million, worth about $1 billion—if his claim was just.

Nvidia did not dispute that his agreement was real, but said the legal deadline had passed for his appeal. His attorneys agreed the statute of limitations blocked the way: a deadline for bringing old claims, even with billions at play.

Stock that cannot be sold may stop an owner selling too soon—but only if the company succeeds. Many 1996 tech stocks vanished; Nvidia became the world’s biggest company.

So forgetting did not lock away a fortune for Eric to collect someday. It left him with no extra Nvidia shares—and about $1 billion worth of regret.

What happened? Eric Gullichsen tried to claim old Nvidia shares created by 9,375 unexercised options, which stock splits would have turned into 4.5 million shares worth about $1 billion. Nvidia said the claim was time-barred, and his attorneys concluded that the statute of limitations prevented recovery.
