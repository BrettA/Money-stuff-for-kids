AI-Backed Securities

STORY 1
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Securitization

Boxes, Bonds, and Big Computers

They put the cash flows in a box, and sell the pieces off like pie, Some pieces are quite safe and small, some reach up to touch the sky. If stuff makes $100, a senior note might claim the first $20 clear, A daring option might grab all above $120, if buyers cheer. CoreWeave builds on one side, SpaceX builds rockets far away, But a new thing hums inside—a data center powering AI today. You could sign a lease with Anthropic for computer time each year, Maybe $1 billion a year for 10 years—ten boxes full of cheer. Then slice that deal: sell the first $100 million a year as AAA-rated bonds, Investors might nod and buy them up while lawyers write their wands. Bloomberg’s Jack Trapanick and Scott Carpenter told the news, That the SEC staff’s letter said data centers don’t act like loans or dues. Latham & Watkins asked for clarity in a formal, careful plea, And SEC staff wrote back that these assets don’t “self-liquidate,” you see. That means fewer disclosures, and the rule called risk retention might not apply, Which is odd because running a data center takes repairs, power, and people nearby. It sounded a bit absurd to treat a building full of chips like a burnt-down car loan, But just as odd is how fast the world can turn compute into something to own. Investors, lawyers, Anthropic, OpenAI, and the SEC all played their part, As bonds and boxes shuffled risk away while the humming servers start.

What happened? The SEC staff wrote an interpretive letter—requested by Latham & Watkins LLP and reported by Bloomberg (Jack Trapanick and Scott Carpenter)—saying many data-center financings are not "asset-backed securities" because data centers are not self-liquidating financial assets, so those deals need not follow ABS disclosure and risk-retention rules (e.g., retaining some of the debt).

STORY 2
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Truth Social API

A Millisecond for Money

Donald Trump tapped a post on Truth Social and out it shot, bright and quick, like light on a dot.

His company TMTG, standing tall and proud, whispered that post to traders before the crowd.

Hedge funds and trading firms, eager and keen, paid one hundred thousand dollars — a neat monthly green.

They said a few milliseconds meant their computers could race, to trade on policy news and win a brief, tiny place.

Algorithms blinked, market makers adjusted their books, the buyers and sellers spun like gears in their nooks.

People wondered if a president should sell such a trick, turning public choices into a private paycheck quick.

Lawyers in New York read the fine-print line and sued, saying that selling early posts was corrupt and crude.

The Intercept Media and Freedom of the Press stood and cried, calling the plan ‘extraordinary, corrupt, and unconstitutional,’ and filed.

Some said the SEC or prosecutors might step in to say no, or Congress could ask the President to stop the show.

Investors might try suing if trades used secret news that dropped the price — but courts and clues make that case a very hard slice.

Still the odd little picture, plain and slightly absurd: the President selling milliseconds of government word.

What happened? Trump Media & Technology Group offered a paid feed selling early access to Donald Trump's Truth Social posts to hedge funds and trading firms for about $100,000 per month; subscribers received policy announcements a few milliseconds before the public, raising insider-trading and fairness concerns and leading to a New York lawsuit by The Intercept Media and the Freedom of the Press Foundation.

STORY 3
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Gambling emergency

The Day the Regulator Said, 'Keep Selling the Bets'

Kalshi ran a market where people could bet on sports, and posted odds like flags on poles that glow,

They called their market a designated contract market, and said the rules came from a place high and slow.

The CFTC — the agency that watches big commodity trades — once said sports bets shouldn’t be sold there,

But now the CFTC changed its view and said federal law allows these sports wagers to be fair.

New York sued Kalshi, saying state law bans those sports bets on its streets and in its parks,

Kalshi replied, “You can’t shut down a federally licensed exchange with only one state’s marks.”

Federal courts have mostly sided with Kalshi so far, though exceptions and questions still roam,

So the CFTC declared an emergency and filed an order to keep Kalshi’s exchange humming like chrome.

The order cites Section 8a(9) of the Commodity Exchange Act and tells Kalshi to carry on with its trading,

It says a single state shouldn’t be able to close a CFTC-registered market without serious persuading.

It’s a strange idea — to call a possible court loss an emergency for markets’ health and order,

But the CFTC insists the action protects clearing and fulfillment, and keeps traders from crossing a border.

So judges and lawyers now sort the question of who really gets to make the rules and draw the lines,

While Kalshi keeps offering sports bets and the federal regulator says, “We’ll keep the market fine.”

What happened? The CFTC invoked its statutory emergency power under Section 8a(9) of the Commodity Exchange Act to order Kalshi Inc., a CFTC-registered designated contract market, to continue performing its exchange functions despite a lawsuit from the State of New York alleging Kalshi’s sports betting violated state gambling regulation. The CFTC’s position is that federal law permits such betting on a federally registered exchange and preempts state regulation.

STORY 4
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Musical earnings calls

From Earnings Calls to Rock Songs

In Cambridge town, where rainy streets are long,

A student named Mikey dreamed of sound and song.

He was at Harvard, studying quantum night and day,

Then he walked down to Kensho where computers hum and play.

He met Georg, Martin, Keenan — friends who liked to code,

They built a clever thing to read what managers had told.

It listened to earnings calls, it typed the words they said,

And turned a patch of speaking into neat lines on a thread.

They called the early audio tool simply Bark in friendly tone,

But the people who tried it wanted beats, a chorus, and a phone.

Toward the end of 2021 they quit their daytime jobs to try,

They started Suno — “listen” — and gave those tools a wider sky.

Developers pushed the tool from business talk to music bright,

By November 2023 Suno made songs like streetlamps make the night.

Meanwhile hedge funds and traders race to hear the news first, fast,

Quantum physicists write code to pull meanings from the past.

So a tool that chased an earnings whisper turned to strum and drum,

And suddenly the team that taught machines to speak could also make them hum.

Reporters Lucas Shaw and Ashley Carman told the odd report,

That the very thing built for traders was now helping people make a sport.

If a busy CEO wants a rap for an offsite or a call,

Suno could spin a chorus — which feels rather wild and small.

What happened? Suno's founders left Kensho at the end of 2021, having built technology to automate corporate earnings calls (a product called Bark). They founded Suno, and in November 2023 released music-generation tools that grew from that earnings-call audio work; at the same time hedge funds pay to trade quickly on information from earnings calls, driving work by physicists and engineers on extracting audio and text signals.
