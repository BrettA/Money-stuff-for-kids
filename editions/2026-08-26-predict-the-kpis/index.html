IN MATT'S ARTICLE: KPI binaries

The One-Dollar Company Bet

A stock’s price looks forward: What cash might it earn? More profits expected can make prices turn. But markets sell wagers on smaller-sized signs— Cars delivered, revenue, product lines.

Cboe Global Markets proposed a new choice: Binary KPI options with a yes-or-no voice. If Tesla earned over forty cents per share, One dollar paid out; below, nothing there.

Net income, revenue, Model 3/Y production too— Company scorecards could settle what buyers were due. Cboe told the SEC, “These bets belong here: They’re securities products, like stock options, quite near.”

But Kalshi, whose CFTC market hosts bets, Already lists Tesla-delivery contracts. It lists Knicks total wins—and then entered the fray, Asking the SEC to make Cboe delay.

Cboe and CME Group had grumbled before That CFTC let new bets rush through the door. Now fast-moving Kalshi requested a gate; Bloomberg’s Bernard Goyder reported the debate.

A stock is a bet on the income to come; An income bet looks rather stock-like to some. Yet perhaps, with a perfectly straight legal face, “The bet isn’t stock”—and it trades someplace else.

What happened? Cboe asked the SEC to approve $1-or-$0 options based on company KPIs such as Tesla’s earnings or vehicle production, while Kalshi asked for a delay. The dispute is whether these contracts are SEC-regulated securities derivatives or CFTC-regulated event bets.

IN MATT'S ARTICLE: No victim here

The Loans with the Missing Label

At Group 1001, in Mark Walter’s domain, More than $20 billion was listed in loans. But loans involving companies connected inside Should be marked “affiliated”—and these were not classified.

That label helps regulators measure the risk And order more capital—a safety-cushion disk. For annuities promise payments for many long years, So regulators demand enough backup for fears.

The Justice Department and SEC began to inquire; TWG Global responded, “There’s no fraud in this fire. There is no victim here. No customer lost pay. The assets earn income and perform well today.”

TWG proposed buying those assets itself, Removing affiliate exposure from the insurer’s shelf. Its insurance group gave regulators a plan, To settle their worries as promptly as it can.

So maybe each promised payment will arrive just fine— Yet hidden risk still matters before payment time. “No victim!” sounds simple when no losses appear, But capital rules guard against what might happen next year.

What happened? Group 1001 disclosed more than $20 billion of loans that reportedly should have been labeled as affiliated transactions, which likely meant its insurers should have held more regulatory capital. TWG Global said no policyholder was harmed and proposed purchasing the affiliated assets while working with regulators, the Justice Department, and the SEC.

IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Good wedding toast

A Toast After the Margin Call

Leopold Aschenbrenner was twenty-four, with a hedge fund that aimed to earn more. Situational Awareness was its name, but a semi-collapse soon rattled the game.

A margin call means: “More cash must appear, or more collateral—valuable assets—right here.” To meet those calls without further delay, Leopold sold most of his public stocks away.

Citadel bought them, and just days went by before Leopold’s wedding festivities drew nigh. The Wall Street Journal reported the scene: a wedding-day joke both pointed and keen.

One speaker raised a glass, and the punch line came in: “Thanks for making it possible, Citadel CEO Ken Griffin!” The stock sale had helped answer each financial call— so Ken got mock credit for the wedding and all.

What happened? Days before his wedding, Leopold Aschenbrenner sold most of his public stock portfolio to Citadel to raise money for margin calls. At the wedding, a speaker jokingly thanked Citadel CEO Ken Griffin “for making it all possible.”
