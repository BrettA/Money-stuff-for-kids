The Suitcase Full of Tools

Once upon a campus, suits learned how to steer,

Then many of those suits went to finance—far and near.

They learned how to move money from place to place,

To pick a good company and help it win a race.

But some of those people wanted a flatter, closer view,

Not giant buyouts, but running something true.

So they packed up a briefcase, not for hedge fund flare,

But for elevators, HOAs, small businesses that need care.

Altaline was started by veterans—Rafael Telahun among them—

From TA Associates, H.I.G. Capital, and KKR, not a whim.

Bloomberg’s Allison McNeely and Preeti Singh told how it grew,

A single-deal approach, a hopeful and speedy crew.

Investors who wanted quicker paydays raised small syndicates of cash,

Giving equity and debt to buy a firm and make a plan that’s brash.

Citrin Cooperman found these deals often earn two to ten million in adjusted pay,

So buyers look for founder-run shops where old owners want to step away.

The funny, slightly odd thing—this is the market’s little trick—

Smart graduates who wanted KKR instead took over elevator-fix.

They trade Wall Street’s glitter for grease-stained keys and a van,

And use private-equity tools to help a local business stand.

What happened? Independent sponsors raise capital deal-by-deal—pulling together small equity and debt syndicates to buy founder-run businesses (often with $2 million to $10 million of adjusted earnings). Altaline Capital Management was launched by mid-career veterans from TA Associates, H.I.G. Capital and KKR to pursue deals such as elevator maintenance and homeowners’ association management, offering quicker paydays and less leverage than larger buyout funds.

IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Parlays

The Bet That Is Fun Because It Is Bad

Sportsbooks love parlays. Instead of betting only that the Mets win, you can bet that the Mets win AND Francisco Lindor homers AND several other specific things happen. Miss one piece and the whole bet loses.

That giant possible payout is fun. It is also why sportsbooks can charge much more for parlays than for simple bets. A basic bet is easy to compare across sportsbooks; a custom ten-part parlay is harder to comparison-shop, so its price can be worse for the customer.

Kalshi is a prediction market rather than a traditional sportsbook, but its combo bets have become huge. Bloomberg reported that combos were 36% of Kalshi contracts that month and ordinary customers had lost a net $294 million on them since the start of the year, excluding fees.

Here is the interesting market-design twist: Kalshi does not have to be the house. A customer builds a combo, and market makers get about a second to bid to take the other side. Professional trading firms can do this, but so can smaller traders with good models.

One former FanDuel trader described retail customers as wanting lottery tickets. FanDuel itself has started making markets in combos too.

So prediction markets have invented a democratic innovation: instead of only the sportsbook getting to make money selling people complicated long-shot bets, independent trading nerds can compete for the privilege too.

IN MATT'S ARTICLE: DAT to AI pivot

Quick, We’re an AI Company Now

Matt Levine has a running rule of thumb: some tiny public companies seem to change businesses whenever the hottest investing buzzword changes. Gold! Cannabis! Crypto! Whatever investors want next!

Last year, digital-asset-treasury companies were the fad. A small public company could announce that it would raise money and buy crypto, and investors might suddenly care about the stock.

Now that crypto-treasury stocks have cooled, some of those companies are pivoting to artificial intelligence. K Wave Media moved from accumulating Bitcoin toward data-center development. AlphaTON Capital, which held alternative cryptocurrencies, rebranded as Alpha Compute.

There is a perfectly respectable explanation: companies change strategy when opportunities change.

There is also Levine’s funnier explanation: perhaps their most durable corporate skill is not mining gold, distributing cannabis, holding crypto or building AI infrastructure. Perhaps it is selling stock to people who are excited about whatever word is fashionable this year.

Unfortunately, Bloomberg reported that several of the AI pivots had still fallen sharply. Apparently adding AI to the costume does not force the stock price to cooperate.

IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Trust

Kevin's Big Sharing Circle

In a quiet California valley, Netflix owned a ranch so grand,

Sendero Ranch held chairs in a circle on warm January land.

Executives from Eyeline Studios sat with badges and coffee in hand,

They were asked to be honest, to try something trusting and planned.

Kevin Baillie sighed, looked up, and let a small secret fall,

He told them he’d taken ketamine the doctor had given to him after all.

The circle was soft as a pillow, and everyone listened close,

But whispered things travel oddly, like petals that the wind chose.

Soon a note crossed a desk, and a question came: What did he mean?

An investigation opened, and the next scene was less serene.

Kevin had been vice president, head of creative at Eyeline’s place,

He was paid $1.1 million a year, a sum many would chase.

Because he’d spoken in that circle, Netflix looked into his claim,

They then let him go from the job, and Kevin answered back with a legal name.

It’s strange and it’s funny, the way trust exercises can twist —

You think you’re building a bridge, but sometimes bridges can’t resist

Turning private things into paper and questions that won't sleep,

And a hug in a meadow can lead to headlines that run deep.

What happened? Kevin Baillie, vice president and head of creative at Eyeline Studios, told colleagues at a January 2026 “Vulnerability-Trust” exercise at Netflix’s Sendero Ranch that he had taken medically prescribed ketamine. Netflix investigated and fired him from a $1.1 million-a-year job; Baillie has filed a lawsuit.
