Elevators Won’t Repair Themselves

Elementary School edition.

STORY 1
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Independent sponsors

The Finance People Who Bought an Elevator Company

Some finance people spend their days deciding which companies should get money.

But some decide to buy one ordinary company and run it themselves — maybe even an elevator-repair company.

So after years of fancy finance, your big job might be making sure elevators get fixed. Elevators, unfortunately, do not repair themselves.

THE MONEY IDEA
Sometimes people who invest in businesses end up running ordinary businesses themselves.

You Went Into High Finance to Fix Elevators

Matt Levine has a theory about business school. First, smart ambitious people learned to run businesses. Then finance got glamorous, so many of them went to banks and private-equity firms to decide which businesses deserved money.

But private equity has a funny way of circling back to actual business. Its job is often to buy a company and make it work better. Search funds go even further: raise money, buy one small company, and become its operator.

Now more private-equity veterans are becoming independent sponsors. Instead of raising one giant fund, they find a company first and then assemble investors for that particular deal.

Altaline Capital Management was started by veterans of TA Associates, H.I.G. Capital and KKR. It has invested in elevator maintenance and homeowners-association management — exactly the sort of unglamorous businesses where better pricing, sales and operations can matter.

The joke is the career arc. You fight your way into prestigious high finance, learn sophisticated capital allocation, and arrive at the pinnacle of your career: figuring out how to repair elevators more efficiently.

Which is useful! Elevators won’t repair themselves.

Private Equity Comes Back Down to Earth

Private equity is finance, but its economic promise is operational: buy a company that could be run better, improve it and eventually sell it.

Independent sponsors make that model more artisanal. They find a specific business, then raise equity and debt for that deal rather than managing a permanent pool of committed capital.

A growing supply of retiring founders and investors willing to finance lower-middle-market deals has made the model attractive to experienced private-equity professionals.

Altaline Capital Management, founded by veterans of TA Associates, H.I.G. Capital and KKR, has invested in elevator maintenance and HOA management. Levine’s joke is that elite finance can ultimately turn into the extremely concrete job of making boring companies work better.

THE MONEY IDEA
Independent sponsors combine capital raising with hands-on ownership of small businesses.

Independent Sponsors and the Return of the Operator

Levine frames finance as a meta-business: talented people allocate capital across companies rather than operate one company directly. Private equity partially reverses that abstraction because its value proposition is to acquire businesses and improve them.

Search funds and independent sponsors push further toward operation. An independent sponsor sources a target first, then syndicates the equity and debt required for that transaction instead of drawing from a committed blind-pool fund.

The model is benefiting from slower large-cap deal activity, constrained promotion opportunities and a supply of founder-owned businesses whose baby-boomer owners want to retire.

Altaline Capital Management illustrates the punch line. Veterans of prestigious private-equity firms left high finance and invested in businesses including elevator maintenance. The allocator of capital becomes, in a meaningful sense, an elevator-company operator.

THE MONEY IDEA
Deal-by-deal private equity shifts professionals from abstract capital allocation toward direct operational ownership.
STORY 2
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Parlays

The Bet Where Everything Has to Go Right

Imagine a bet that only wins if your team wins AND a player hits a home run AND another player does something special.

That is called a parlay. It can promise a huge prize because it is easy for one little part to go wrong.

People like exciting giant prizes. The people taking the other side of those bets often like the math even more.

THE MONEY IDEA
A bet with lots of things that all must happen can promise a big prize but is very hard to win.

The Bet That Is Fun Because It Is Bad

Sportsbooks love parlays. Instead of betting only that the Mets win, you can bet that the Mets win AND Francisco Lindor homers AND several other specific things happen. Miss one piece and the whole bet loses.

That giant possible payout is fun. It is also why sportsbooks can charge much more for parlays than for simple bets. A basic bet is easy to compare across sportsbooks; a custom ten-part parlay is harder to comparison-shop, so its price can be worse for the customer.

Kalshi is a prediction market rather than a traditional sportsbook, but its combo bets have become huge. Bloomberg reported that combos were 36% of Kalshi contracts that month and ordinary customers had lost a net $294 million on them since the start of the year, excluding fees.

Here is the interesting market-design twist: Kalshi does not have to be the house. A customer builds a combo, and market makers get about a second to bid to take the other side. Professional trading firms can do this, but so can smaller traders with good models.

One former FanDuel trader described retail customers as wanting lottery tickets. FanDuel itself has started making markets in combos too.

So prediction markets have invented a democratic innovation: instead of only the sportsbook getting to make money selling people complicated long-shot bets, independent trading nerds can compete for the privilege too.

Who Gets the House Edge on Kalshi Parlays?

Parlays are profitable because every leg must hit and because bespoke combinations are difficult for bettors to comparison-shop. That lets the seller embed a larger edge than on standardized single-game bets.

Kalshi’s combo contracts reproduce that economics but change who can capture it. When a retail user requests a combo, market makers receive an RFQ and briefly compete to quote the other side.

Bloomberg found combos had reached 36% of Kalshi contracts that month while retail-like takers had lost $294 million since the start of the year, excluding fees.

The odd democratization is that sophisticated hobbyists and algorithmic firms can earn some of the edge that a conventional sportsbook would keep. FanDuel has even joined them as a market maker.

THE MONEY IDEA
Prediction-market RFQs let competing market makers capture the economics that traditional sportsbooks usually keep for themselves.

Parlays, RFQs and the Democratization of the House Edge

Single sports bets are relatively standardized and easy to comparison-shop, which constrains sportsbook margins. Multi-leg parlays are bespoke, harder to compare and highly convex, allowing much larger embedded edges.

Kalshi’s prediction-market structure separates the venue from the counterparty. For combo contracts, an RFQ is distributed to market makers, who have roughly a second to compete for the trade.

Bloomberg reported combos at 36% of Kalshi contracts that month and $294 million of net losses by retail-like takers since the start of the year, excluding fees. The economic surplus that would traditionally accrue to a sportsbook can therefore be captured by algorithmic firms or even skilled independent traders.

Levine’s recurring white whale is the logical endpoint: if retail betting biases are systematic, perhaps a transparent fund could systematically take the unpopular, positive-expected-value side. The joke is that regulated prediction markets might turn 'being the house' into an investable strategy.

THE MONEY IDEA
Bespoke parlay pricing creates large margins; Kalshi’s RFQ market structure transfers some of those margins from centralized sportsbooks to competing liquidity providers.
STORY 3
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: DAT to AI pivot

The Company That Keeps Changing Costumes

Some little public companies keep changing what they do whenever a new idea gets popular.

One year the costume says CRYPTO. The next year it says AI.

A new costume can get attention, but investors eventually ask whether the company is actually good at the new job.

THE MONEY IDEA
Changing a company’s trendy label does not automatically make the business better.

Quick, We’re an AI Company Now

Matt Levine has a running rule of thumb: some tiny public companies seem to change businesses whenever the hottest investing buzzword changes. Gold! Cannabis! Crypto! Whatever investors want next!

Last year, digital-asset-treasury companies were the fad. A small public company could announce that it would raise money and buy crypto, and investors might suddenly care about the stock.

Now that crypto-treasury stocks have cooled, some of those companies are pivoting to artificial intelligence. K Wave Media moved from accumulating Bitcoin toward data-center development. AlphaTON Capital, which held alternative cryptocurrencies, rebranded as Alpha Compute.

There is a perfectly respectable explanation: companies change strategy when opportunities change.

There is also Levine’s funnier explanation: perhaps their most durable corporate skill is not mining gold, distributing cannabis, holding crypto or building AI infrastructure. Perhaps it is selling stock to people who are excited about whatever word is fashionable this year.

Unfortunately, Bloomberg reported that several of the AI pivots had still fallen sharply. Apparently adding AI to the costume does not force the stock price to cooperate.

From Crypto Treasury to AI, Right on Schedule

Small listed companies sometimes pivot repeatedly toward whatever theme currently attracts retail capital. Levine’s running sequence includes gold, cannabis, crypto mining, pandemic products, digital-asset treasuries and now AI.

The digital-asset-treasury boom created demand for otherwise underused public-company shells because their listed shares could be sold to finance crypto purchases.

As that trade cooled, some former crypto-focused firms rebranded around data centers or AI compute. Bloomberg reported weak stock performance for several such pivots.

The joke is also a capital-markets hypothesis: the stable core competency may not be any particular operating business but the ability to tell a fashionable story and issue equity into it.

THE MONEY IDEA
Narrative-driven pivots can help small public companies attract capital, but investor enthusiasm and operating competence are different things.

The Persistent Business Model Is Selling the Next Business Model

Levine’s recurring model of promotional micro-caps is that operating themes rotate while the financing technology remains constant. A listed shell can move from gold to cannabis to crypto to digital-asset treasury strategies and then AI as investor demand migrates.

The DAT boom was unusually convenient because the public listing itself was economically useful: companies could issue equity to buy crypto, creating a reflexive relationship between market enthusiasm and asset accumulation.

With DAT valuations under pressure, Bloomberg reported pivots such as K Wave Media toward data centers and AlphaTON Capital’s rebranding as Alpha Compute, alongside substantial share-price declines.

The cynical interpretation is not that management possesses every newly fashionable operating skill. It is that access to public equity markets is the real franchise, and the business description is the periodically refreshed marketing layer.

THE MONEY IDEA
For some micro-cap issuers, public-market access can be more durable than the operating theme used to attract capital at any particular moment.
STORY 4
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Trust

The Work Meeting Where You Should Not Tell Every Secret

At a work retreat, people were asked to share personal things so everyone would trust each other more.

One executive shared something private about medical treatment. Later he was fired and sued the company.

The joke is that a meeting called a trust exercise can make people wonder exactly how much they should trust it.

THE MONEY IDEA
Trust exercises can be awkward when personal information is later used in workplace decisions.

The Trust Exercise That Did Not Inspire Much Trust

Imagine your company gathers everyone for a 'Vulnerability-Trust exercise.' The idea is that you reveal something personal, everyone understands each other better, and the team becomes closer.

Levine proposes a safer fake confession: stare into the distance, tear up, and announce that your terrible secret is being too much of a perfectionist.

Why? The New York Post reported on a lawsuit by Kevin Baillie, a former Netflix executive and head of creative at Eyeline Studios. Baillie says that during a January retreat at Netflix-owned Sendero Ranch, he disclosed that he had undergone medically prescribed ketamine treatment.

According to the lawsuit, Netflix later investigated his comments and fired him from a job paying $1.1 million a year. Baillie sued.

The legal dispute can decide what the company was allowed to do. Levine is interested in the workplace absurdity.

A meeting explicitly designed to make you trust your colleagues is a pretty funny place to learn that maybe you should be extremely careful about trusting your colleagues.

Vulnerability, Trust and the Corporate Record

Kevin Baillie, formerly a Netflix executive and head of creative at Eyeline Studios, sued after he said a disclosure during a 'Vulnerability-Trust exercise' contributed to an investigation and his firing.

Baillie said he disclosed medically prescribed ketamine treatment during a January 2026 retreat at Netflix-owned Sendero Ranch. The lawsuit concerns what followed and whether Netflix acted properly.

Levine turns the episode into a corporate-governance joke: an institution can encourage radical candor while employees rationally worry that candid information will affect future employment decisions.

His mock advice is to confess only to being 'too much of a perfectionist' — vulnerability engineered to contain no actual vulnerability.

THE MONEY IDEA
Workplace trust exercises create tension between candid disclosure and the employer’s ability to act on information employees reveal.

The Incentive Problem in Corporate Vulnerability

The Baillie lawsuit presents a classic incentive mismatch inside a modern corporate ritual. A 'Vulnerability-Trust exercise' asks employees to disclose information precisely because it is personal and consequential; the employer simultaneously remains an institution capable of investigating and terminating them.

Kevin Baillie, formerly vice president and head of creative at Netflix’s Eyeline Studios, says he disclosed medically prescribed ketamine treatment at a January 2026 retreat at Netflix-owned Sendero Ranch and was later investigated and fired. He sued Netflix.

Whatever the legal merits, Levine focuses on mechanism design: asking for authentic vulnerability without credible limits on how disclosures will be used may produce performative vulnerability instead.

Hence his joke confession — 'Sometimes I’m too much of a perfectionist' — which perfectly satisfies the ritual while revealing nothing useful at all.

THE MONEY IDEA
An employer’s request for candor can conflict with employees’ rational incentives when disclosed information may affect employment decisions.
