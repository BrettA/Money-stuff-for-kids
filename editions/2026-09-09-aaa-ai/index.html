IN MATT'S ARTICLE: AI ratings

The AI Labs and the Investment-Grade Curve

Joshua Coval, Jakub Jurek, and Erik Stafford once showed why a shiny bond rating may hide danger down the road: AAA mortgage bonds seemed unlikely to fall, but a housing crash could drop them when money mattered most of all.

Today, giant data centers need mountains of power, with rows of Nvidia chips humming hour after hour. Their bonds are repaid from the cash AI brings— from customers to labs, then to computing.

Much of that cash is expected from OpenAI, and Anthropic, though both still lose money as years hurry by. The labs pay data centers for computing might; those payments help the centers repay bonds right.

Now Morgan Stanley and Goldman Sachs made their pitch for investment-grade ratings from S&P, Moody’s, and Fitch. That label tells bond buyers repayment looks sound, so cheaper funding for construction can often be found.

After an IPO—their first public sale of stock— the $11.7 trillion corporate bond market might unlock. That could help huge infrastructure plans roll through, benefiting partners like Oracle and Nvidia too.

The rating analysts answered, “We’ll wait for the IPO.” The labs are still “in the red,” with free cash flow low. For now, they remain deep speculative grade; no investment-grade promise has yet been made.

Still, AI is becoming a great economic tide— so where can big bond investors sensibly hide? If nearly everyone owns the same buildout someday, perhaps OpenAI and Anthropic get graded on a curve anyway.

What happened? Morgan Stanley and Goldman Sachs discussed with Fitch, Moody’s, and S&P whether OpenAI and Anthropic might receive investment-grade ratings after future IPOs, lowering their borrowing costs and opening the $11.7 trillion corporate bond market. The agencies had not decided; both labs remained unprofitable and speculative grade.

STORY 2
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Hourly ETFs

Six Resets Before the Bell

Defiance ETFs filed a plan with the SEC: “Let’s double short stock moves—six times daily!” Most two-times funds reset once at day’s end; these would restart the same wager again.

The funds would track Nvidia, Microsoft too, plus Meta, Palantir, and Tesla’s moves through. With swaps or options—contracts tied to each share— they’d seek twice the gain, or twice the despair.

Each reset would use prices averaged through time, not one single tick picked at one single chime. That matters because when a stock leaps by noon, a daily fund’s leverage may drift out of tune.

Bloomberg Intelligence analyst James Seyffart said, “The difference seems narrow,” and skeptically read. Perhaps near announcements, one hour might be key— but why need such pinpoint precision? We’ll see.

Would hourly funds hibernate when trading was slow? Would traders swap daily for hourly? Who knows? The joke is that every small spot on the map may someday get its own ETF-shaped cap.

What happened? Defiance ETFs asked the SEC to approve leveraged funds that would rebalance six times per trading day and use swaps or options to target roughly twice the moves of certain technology stocks during each period. The proposal had been filed but not yet approved.

STORY 3
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: People are worried about the basis trade

The Treasury Future That Came From the Treasury

The U.S. Treasury sells bonds for thirty years or ten; investors pay it cash up front, then earn interest now and then.

Some bought corporate bonds for yields that might be higher, yet Treasury interest-rate exposure was still something they desired.

A futures contract gives that risk without the full amount: for $1 million of Treasuries, about $18,750 goes in the margin account.

But Treasury sells the bonds—not futures—so hedge funds fill the space: they borrow cash through repo, buy the bonds, then sell the futures in their place.

With just one dollar of their own, they might support full trades of fifty. That leverage earns a little bit—but makes the whole arrangement shifty.

Anil Kashyap, Jeremy Stein, Jonathan Wallen, and Joshua Younger said the Federal Reserve could take such trades if funds could hold no longer.

Then Steven Williams offered up a simpler sort of plan: let Treasury sell the futures to investors when it can.

His “Deferred Settlement Auction” makes delivery wait a month; investors roll it forward monthly, keeping rate exposure out in front.

Treasury could bridge the missing cash by issuing short-term bills, while money-market lenders swap from repo, matching funding needs and wills.

Sell $100 in bills plus $100 in thirty-year futures instead, and it acts much like a thirty-year bond—without the hedge-fund spread.

Then Treasury keeps the fee that middlemen collected in the trade. And if the government blows up? Well… that is quite a bigger mess to have made.

What happened? Steven Williams proposed that the U.S. Treasury directly sell rolling, deferred-settlement futures and issue short-term bills for cash, bypassing highly leveraged hedge funds that currently borrow through repo, buy Treasury bonds, and sell matching futures.

IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Egg Libor fines

The Fine That Arrived by the Dozen

The Justice Department and states—seventeen— said egg-price dealings had not been clean. A handful of producers, the officials said, had pushed a key benchmark where prices are led.

A benchmark’s a reference that buyers may use to help set the price of the eggs that they choose. The government claimed it was twisted askew; the defendants settled and promised, “We’re through.”

They’d pay $3.3 million to states— but one stranger payment rolled up to the gates: fifty-three million free eggs! What a fine, packed into cartons and stacked into line.

North Carolina’s Attorney General Jeff Jackson received some and filmed all the egg-carton action. The eggs went to food banks, as everyone knew; Jeff didn’t eat them—though that would be funnier, too.

Was this the largest free-egg settlement paid? Perhaps. What a record those cartons have made: a price-benchmark case with an ending that begs to be counted in dollars—and millions of eggs.

What happened? The defendants settled government accusations that they manipulated an egg-price benchmark. They agreed to stop, pay $3.3 million to the states, and deliver 53 million free eggs for food banks.
