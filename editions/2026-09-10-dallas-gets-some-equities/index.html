IN MATT'S ARTICLE: TXSE

A Texas Exchange Gets Its First Two Names

A stock exchange matches those who sell with those who buy, And gathers little trading fees as orders hurry by. A “primary listing” is a stock’s official exchange home; But other exchanges may still trade it wherever orders roam.

The Texas Stock Exchange, or TXSE, was new, And needed listed companies to help its market grew. JPMorgan Chase, Citadel Securities, and BlackRock backed the plan: A technology-driven exchange based in Dallas land.

Then Sunoco said, “We’ll leave the NYSE October 2.” Energy Transfer picked TXSE, with October 5 in view. They would be the Texas exchange’s first primary-listing pair, Giving the upstart market two big energy names to share.

But here’s the twist that made the choice especially neat: Energy Transfer owns a stake in Sunoco, linking their retreat. And an entity tied to chairman Kelcy Warren, billionaire, Has a significant holding in TXSE Group shares.

So was Energy Transfer earning bits of every trading fee? Not quite—the chairman-linked entity is separate from the company. And listing shares in Texas does not make all trading happen there; Other U.S. exchanges can still match the stock most anywhere.

Still, “List with us—and own a piece!” would make a catchy pitch. That probably wasn’t the real sales line, but owning some doesn’t hurt a bit.

What happened? Sunoco and Energy Transfer shifted their primary listings from NYSE to TXSE, becoming TXSE’s first primary listings. An entity tied to Energy Transfer chairman Kelcy Warren has a significant TXSE holding, but Energy Transfer itself does not directly receive a cut of its stock’s trading fees.

IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Hudson River

The Quiet Firm and the Very Loud Number

Jane Street once wore finance’s hush-hush crown, then stories spread its secret all through town. Next came Hudson River Trading, quiet and fast— but Bloomberg’s Tom Maloney found it at last.

HRT’s programs rapidly buy and sell, helping markets trade—and earning very well. Its more than 1,200 workers brought in, to everyone’s surprise, twice the profit of Citadel Securities, a larger rival in size.

Alex Morcos said they hated workplaces mean, though books were where he’d mostly seen that scene. Prashant Lal hoped HRT would stay kind: friends building clever algorithms, with cutthroat ways declined.

Last year HRT earned more than $8 billion; Bloomberg valued the firm above $45 billion. A filing suggested Jason Carroll owned 62.5 percent, so his estimated fortune soared past $27 billion in an instant.

No treasure truck arrived. No vault went “ka-boom!” The list-makers simply gave future profits room: they multiplied recurring earnings to estimate what HRT was worth, then ranked Carroll the ninetieth-richest person on Earth.

Carroll still rides the subway, avoiding flashy art, expensive homes, and suits—the classic humble part. He shuns the usual trappings that wealthy people buy; his costliest passion is racing sailboats. Very modest—if you ignore the yachts nearby.

What happened? Bloomberg valued Hudson River Trading at more than $45 billion, about six times its latest annual earnings, and credited Jason Carroll with a 62.5% stake. That estimate put his fortune above $27 billion even though no new pile of cash suddenly appeared.

IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Elsewhere in low-key rich people

The Million-Dollar Manicure

A boss needs a worker to stay at their station: Pay too little—rivals call. Pay too much—vacation! A top restructuring lawyer might earn $25 million a year, Then think, “I’m set for life!” and make the beach chair disappear.

AI pushed this puzzle to startling new heights: Young researchers earned fortunes through long coding nights. Enough to retire—but they stayed at their screens, Without chasing big yachts or extravagant scenes.

Garret Spiecker of Citizens Private Bank could see more Bay Area liquidity than in two decades, said he. His program lends money against private stock before an IPO; clients from OpenAI and Anthropic could make their wealth flow.

One former OpenAI worker sold shares, then chose an electric vehicle—not a fleet of those. Other alumni named one grand spending machine: an espresso maker for extra caffeine.

One lab worker made about $1 million a year, yet handled her own manicures without fancy gear. Her biggest purchase, around $10,000 in all, was customized chips and development boards—not a tropical sprawl.

But having no time to spend money won’t make workers stay: They could simply quit, then shop every day! The stranger idea was: “Our work might end life on Earth, so keep working to stop it”—an alarming source of worth. Would that retain staff at Goldman Sachs? The narrator had doubts… then paused and admitted he could not quite rule it out.

What happened? Highly paid AI employees gained liquidity by selling private shares or borrowing against them before an IPO, but some continued working and spent relatively little on luxuries. The absurd retention problem is that they may be rich enough to quit, while belief in the world-changing—or world-ending—importance of AI may help keep them at work.

IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Tether private credit

Tether’s Very Full Fundraising Hat

People give Tether dollars; USDT comes back, A token meant to keep a one-dollar track. Customers can redeem it, while interest stays low— So Tether has cheap funding wherever those dollars go.

Its reserve pool is roughly $183 billion strong, Once placed in risky assets, though that did not last long. Now Treasury bills and repos fill most of the pot— Short-term, secured investments that usually risk not a lot.

Tether’s funding can cost near zero overnight, While short-term investments earn about four percent outright. Why make long, risky loans and wait years to be paid, When safe, speedy interest is already being made?

But USDT’s total value had stayed rather flat, So Tether planned a lending fund to help change that. With London’s Fasanara Capital joining the quest, StableFund would lend where smaller businesses invest.

It launched with $400 million ready to lend, And sought up to $3 billion from others in the end. The Financial Times reported the curious plan: Outside investors would supply what StableFund can.

So Tether had a giant reserve pool close at hand— Yet asked third-party investors to help loans expand. Like a cautious modern bank, it chose the clever route: Keep its cheap reserves safe, and lend somebody else’s loot.

What happened? Tether and Fasanara Capital launched the $400 million StableFund to make private-credit loans to underserved small and medium-sized businesses, while seeking up to $3 billion from outside investors. Tether kept its roughly $183 billion USDT reserve pool mainly in short-term Treasury bills and repo agreements instead of using that pool for the riskier loans.

IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Magic: The Gathering insider trading

The Very Strange Trade Before Zeta

Before the Zeta Set announcement was made, a buyer arrived for an unusually large trade. From GoatBots came copies—more than 250 in store— and the tweet wondered how many were purchased elsewhere before.

There was Baleful Strix, with its ominous name, Vandalblast bursting in magical flame, and Dispatch as well, all bought in advance— not after the news, but before the cards’ chance.

The tweet said the buyer had a Pauper Format Panel tie and knew something early that others could not buy. If news makes cards pricier, an early buyer may gain: buy low before the announcement, then profit from the change.

It sounded like Wall Street in a wizard’s disguise: secret information and speculative buys, with robot card sellers and spells in the mix— “inside information,” “Pauper,” and “Baleful Strix.”

But the claim was unconfirmed; no proof settled the score. Even whether these were online Magic cards wasn’t fully sure. Still, few market accusations have ever combined such a wonderfully peculiar procession of words in one line.

What happened? @GoatBots alleged that someone connected to the Pauper Format Panel used nonpublic information to buy more than 250 copies of Baleful Strix, Vandalblast, and Dispatch before the Zeta Set announcement. The claim was not confirmed.
