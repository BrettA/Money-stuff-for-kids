STORY 1
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Casinos

The Beautiful Madness of Gangapur

At twenty-seven, Deepak Prajapat changed what he would do: He left selling women’s suits on Facebook and Meesho.

Phones lit up Gangapur, once quiet with dairies and cows; Three generations traded together beneath the neem-tree boughs.

An option was a contract tied to where an index might go; A tiny move could double or triple a stake—or wipe it out in one blow.

YouTube taught “theta decay” in each Greek-letter class, While trading academies filled buildings, floor to floor, wall to glass.

A wager might cost little more than one warm flask of chai. NSE and BSE set different expiries, so a fresh bet waited nearby.

Each weekday offered another quick chance to feel clever and grand: “Perhaps I can beat the professionals with this phone in my hand!”

But nine out of ten individual futures-and-options traders lost money each year— About 128,000 rupees on average, SEBI’s research made clear.

Retail traders lost more than 500 billion rupees in fiscal 2023. Much of the money flowed to sophisticated firms and professional investors on the other side of the trades.

In October and November 2024, SEBI raised the minimum contract size from 5 million to 15 million rupees. It also limited each exchange to one index with options expiring weekly.

The fever cooled quickly. Deepak missed neither the losses nor staring at prices all day, But missed feeling that Gangapur had joined global finance in a dazzling new way.

He called it “beautiful madness,” reaching capitalism’s heart— Except that was the trick: feeling connected was the casino’s cleverest part.

What happened? India’s cheap, frequently expiring index options encouraged constant speculation, and most individual traders lost money. SEBI made trading costlier and less frequent in 2024, rapidly deflating the boom.

IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Compute futures

The Tiny Market Beside the AI Giant

AI data centers need chips by the score, while stocks, loans, and building plans depend on millions more. If chip values tumble, the worry may spread to companies financed by chips in the shed.

Compute futures are contracts that fix what buyers will pay for computing power delivered on a later day. They can help a new data center plan what it will earn— but today these markets are still small at every turn.

Semafor reported that Commerce told Kalshi, “Take down your product that tracks AI compute prices in town.” It also said Commerce urged the CFTC to pause new compute contracts for days sixty.

A Commerce spokesman answered, “This story is false.” So even the reported order remains in dispute, of course. But market participants offered a reason for alarm: a tiny, thin market might cause outsized harm.

Someone could push older-chip prices sharply down, then hedge funds might see trouble spreading around. AI stocks and bonds could sink from the scare— a minnow of a market wagging a whale by its tail.

No one proved such a trick had happened at all. The strange part was simply the size of the fall: a little compute contract, easy to shove, might shake trillions of AI value above.

What happened? Semafor reported that the US Commerce Department ordered Kalshi to remove an AI-compute price contract and sought a 60-day pause on new contracts, though Commerce called the report false. Market participants suggested that manipulated or volatile compute-futures prices could make older chips look less valuable and unsettle much larger AI stock and debt markets.

STORY 3
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: EbitdAI

The Earnings That Hadn’t Happened Yet

In France, Emeria SASU had results to prepare, and one curious number made credit experts stare. The property-services company, struggling of late, gave last year’s earnings an AI-powered update.

EBITDA starts with earnings, then sets some charges aside: interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization don’t get applied. An “add-back” removes costs that lenders agree will not remain, to help them judge if a borrower can repay them again.

But Emeria added €20 million—about $23 million more—for AI savings it hoped for, not savings already in store. Over the next two years, agentic AI was planned to prepare documents, handle data, and draft replies on demand.

Customer service, accounting, back-office work too: fewer costs in the future, if the program came through. Yet those hoped-for savings joined the last-twelve-month score— future efficiency stepping through last year’s door.

Bloomberg News heard from ten market watchers or more; they said they had not seen this adjustment before. Benjamin Sabahi of Spread Research took a stricter view: Count gains once they’re generated, not before they come true.

Backed by Partners Group Holding AG and TA Associates, Emeria replied that the adjustment followed the methodology agreed with its lenders. The company said its AI plans were identified and measured—and €20 million was only a fraction of the possible savings ahead.

So the puzzle involved a wonderfully peculiar feat: Could tomorrow’s robot savings make yesterday’s earnings complete? Add-backs often bring expected changes into view, but counting an AI plan in past results felt startlingly new.

What happened? Emeria SASU added €20 million to adjusted last-twelve-month EBITDA for projected savings from two years of future AI automation. Emeria said its lenders had agreed to the methodology, while Benjamin Sabahi argued that savings should be counted only after they are generated.

IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Not like other AI safety advocates

The Unlikely Quitter with Every Clue

At Anthropic, Jacob Coxon researched AI, then quit with a warning: the stakes were too high. He feared that advanced systems, if built without care, might someday kill everyone, everywhere.

The Wall Street Journal said his friends were surprised; he wasn’t a safety-scene leader, they realized. He wasn’t well known in the San Francisco crowds discussing grim scenarios at happy hours aloud.

Yet Jacob had signposts piled three stories tall: a rationalist circle was one sign of all. After college in London, his group-house address was Newspeak House—another strong hint, more or less.

But the biggest sign came from the UK IMO: six math-team members—and three chose AI as they’d go. Those three worked for AI labs; then, adding a twist, one teammate quit Anthropic too, for safety risk.

So “not like the others” was harder to say, when every bright signpost seemed pointed that way. And somehow the strongest predictor to know ranked rationalists and group-house life below IMO!

What happened? Former Anthropic researcher Jacob Coxon resigned because he feared its AI work could endanger humanity. The Wall Street Journal presented him as an unexpected safety-driven quitter, although he had joined rationalist circles, lived at Newspeak House, and served on a six-person UK International Mathematical Olympiad team whose members included three future AI-lab workers and another person who quit Anthropic.
