STORY 1
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: AI CDS

The Insurance Everyone Wanted

A kajillion was borrowed for buildings packed with chips, Where AI tenants would pay rent on long, expensive trips. If AI earned enough, each loan could be repaid; If chips went cold or stale, then trouble had been made.

Investors sought a safety net called CDS: Pay premiums to a seller, who pays if debts collapse. Oracle protection once cost 0.33% a year; Now roughly 3.3% showed ten times more fear.

By September, weekly trades had climbed past $9 billion—whew!— From less than $2 billion back in July’s view. Oracle, SpaceX, Alphabet, Amazon joined the crowd, With Meta, Broadcom and Nvidia named aloud.

Even safer Microsoft saw protection prices rise; CoreWeave’s spread climbed too, as worry filled investors’ eyes. Jim Caron called CDS a cheap and useful shield— But who would sell protection when the whole wide market reeled?

The insurers, banks and hedge funds heard each anxious call, But many owned AI loans and bonds already—one and all. They could promise help if AI’s grand boom went bust, Yet that very bust might empty out the pockets they must trust.

Jeremy Maletz heard requests for prediction-market cover; Still, every eager buyer needed someone betting the other. AIG? Leopold Aschenbrenner? Those suggestions were in fun; OpenAI had even floated government when private help was done.

Preeti Singh reported Blackstone leaning into AI, Financing more data centers reaching toward the sky. Risk could move to funds and swaps, wherever traders sent it— But clever contracts could not make the market-wide risk exit.

What happened? Investors bought more credit default swaps tied to AI-linked borrowers, paying sellers for a payout if those companies defaulted. Because many possible sellers already held AI-related debt, demand surged, prices rose, and the same concentrated risk could be transferred but not eliminated.

STORY 2
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Short-term investments

The Six-Month Loan That Wouldn’t End

Mark Walter owned insurers. Their customers would pay for annuities—future checks promised someday. Walter controlled where that money could go, including loans to his businesses, Bloomberg would show.

In December 2018, Delaware Life sent forty million dollars to an undisclosed borrower. The loan was due in six months.

But June brought a rollover, not money repaid: a fresh six-month loan, then another was made. Again and again, the due date would bend— a “short-term” investment that just wouldn’t end.

Risky long loans may need thirty percent held safely as capital in case it gets spent. But certain short loans faced a charge of point-three-nine; rolling them helped keep that cheaper design.

Reporters Zachary Mider and Weihua Li found loans to Walter-controlled LLCs. About $2.3 billion had rolled from the year before, and some had kept rolling for nearly a decade or more.

Six months is too brief to buy the Dodgers and stay; a ball club won’t earn back its price right away. But link six-month loans in a long paper chain, and “short-term” can circle for years like a train.

What happened? Bloomberg reported that Mark Walter’s insurers repeatedly renewed loans labeled as short-term, including a $40 million Delaware Life loan, while directing billions toward firms he controlled. The short-term label could reduce required risk-based capital from as much as 30% to as little as 0.39%, even when loans kept rolling for years.

STORY 3
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Evergreen continuation vehicles

The Company That Didn’t Have to Go

Private equity would buy a good company low, Improve how it worked, help its earnings to grow. It might change the financing, management too, Then sell after years for much more than was due.

The “exit” was key: find a buyer who’d pay, And profit by sending the company away. But private markets grew, till a question appeared: Why sell a fine business that earns year by year?

Laura Benitez, Preeti Singh, and Layan Odeh found That a new sort of plan was being passed around. Goldman Sachs and Evercore tested the view; Jefferies, Raymond James, and Lazard did too.

They pitched “evergreen continuation” funds, With capital waiting for growth when it comes. The asset could stay, with more time to succeed— No hurried sale and no exit-day need.

Sunaina Sinha Haldea of Raymond James said Permanent-capital plans were just starting to spread. So the grand clever pitch, once the flipping was done, Was: “Own this profitable company forever.” That’s the fun.

What happened? Banks explored evergreen continuation vehicles that would let private equity firms hold companies much longer, with unfunded capital available for growth and no pressure to sell. Instead of relying on an exit price, investors could keep collecting profits from the companies they own.

STORY 4
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Polymarket matures

Polymarket’s Growing-Up Bet

At twenty-eight, Shayne Coplan led Polymarket’s crew, where contracts paid traders when guessed events came true. Traders bought “yes” or “no,” and each price could show what the crowd thought might happen as forecasts would grow.

Four years ago, Kalshi chose rules as its route, with lobbying and lawsuits to work matters out. It sought US approval, all proper and bright, though becoming a regulated exchange seemed a strange bet to get right.

Polymarket figured approval would move much too slow, so offshore crypto was where it would go. Its markets ran statelessly, beyond US gates, while winkingly telling US traders, “Please wait.”

But Kalshi’s odd gamble succeeded at last, while Polymarket hurried to follow its path. Investors pressed Coplan for structure and care as Polymarket tried becoming a grown-up exchange.

Bloomberg reported company funds had been spent on his rent, private jets, and personal expense. ICE—the New York Stock Exchange parent—and Founders Fund urged him to hire a chief operating officer; he had not yet done so.

Coplan said growing pains made him grow, too, and an excellent executive team was his 2026 goal. Yet the backers’ complaint had a comical ring: they had funded an offshore crypto casino—and now found the private jet shocking.

What happened? Polymarket’s investors backed an offshore, crypto-based prediction market that avoided the difficult US approval process, but Kalshi proved a regulated US exchange could succeed. As Polymarket shifted toward that model, investors pushed Coplan to add executives and tighter spending controls after Bloomberg reported his use of company money for private flights, rent, and personal expenses.
