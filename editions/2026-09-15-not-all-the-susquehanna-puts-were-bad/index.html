The Puts That Everyone Could See

From May 7 through May 21, Susquehanna—called SIG— Sold puts on two brokerages for buyers making bets big. The stocks were Futu Holdings and Tiger Brokers too, Part of Up Fintech Holdings, known by the ticker TIGR to you.

A put becomes more valuable when share prices drop; These short-dated, out-of-the-money puts needed a plunge—and fast—to come out on top. On May 22, China announced a cross-border trading crackdown; The brokerage shares tumbled, and the put buyers’ gains reached $71.4 million.

SIG sued one hundred unnamed traders and asked to freeze their pay: “Someone must have known the secret news before announcement day!” But some buyers said, “We saw unusual trades out in public view, So we guessed that trouble might be near—and bought some puts there too.”

One was Zhengfei Li, who cited public market information and investor discussion. Judge Arun Subramanian said insider trading was possible, but SIG had not identified a tipper or shown enough evidence for an account freeze. Bloomberg reporters Chris Dolmetsch and Bob Van Voris covered his decision, Where algorithms, AI agents, and traders chase each public clue with speed and precision.

SIG could raise put prices, sell shares to hedge, or buy puts of its own; Market makers manage risks when strange new betting patterns are shown. So here was the wonderfully awkward bit hidden inside the dispute: One copycat following the suspicious put buyers might have been SIG in its market-making suit.

What happened? After Chinese regulators announced a crackdown, puts on Futu Holdings and Tiger Brokers paid buyers $71.4 million. SIG could not yet show that every winning trader had inside information rather than merely copying publicly visible trades, so the judge declined to keep the accounts frozen.

The Polymarket Bet Against Excitement

On Polymarket, contracts say “Yes” or say “No,” And prices suggest how traders expect events to go. A dull, steady strategy can sometimes succeed By betting against what the crowd hopes to read.

Simone Stella Skovgaard, Noah Wenneberg Junge, and Bulat Ibragimov studied 218 Trump-related markets. They tested a simple plan across 287 days, from July 2025 to May 2026.

At regular times, their rule placed another small trade— That’s dollar-cost averaging, patiently made. The strategy chose anti-Trump outcomes each time, While a mirrored pro-Trump plan followed the opposite line.

The anti-Trump strategy gained $1,006 in all; The pro-Trump mirror lost $1,028 in its fall. Against 10,000 neutral computer-made tries, The anti-Trump result finished higher than every simulated size.

Why might Polymarket prices lean Trump’s way? Its users need crypto and blockchain to play. A Fairleigh Dickinson University poll found crypto owners Were disproportionately Trump supporters among voters.

Perhaps traders priced hopeful wishes too high, So betting against them could profit nearby. But the researchers could not prove who used the site— The curious result showed a pattern, not why it was right.

The joke is that exciting predictions attract every eye, While boring opposition may quietly buy. A passive strategy need not predict one grand surprise; It can simply keep betting against a crowd’s hopeful bias.

What happened? Researchers simulated regular anti-Trump trades across 218 Polymarket binary markets and found a $1,006 cumulative gain, versus a $1,028 loss for the mirrored pro-Trump strategy. They suggested Polymarket’s crypto-using audience might lean toward Donald Trump and overprice Trump-related outcomes, but their data could not confirm that explanation.

The Tiny Discount and the Very Busy Traders

A stablecoin is a dollar designed for a blockchain ride: Circle makes USDC; Tether makes USDT beside. Each token should be worth one dollar, neat and tight, Though fees can push its market price a tiny bit from right.

Palmer Luckey’s Erebor Bank hung out a tempting sign: “We’ll swap your coins for dollars, free, at one-for-one each time!” It sounded like good service—simple, friendly, clear— But Wintermute, the trading firm, soon spotted something dear.

Wintermute bought millions in USDT for slightly under one, Then brought them straight to Erebor, where each paid one when done. Each gap was only tiny, but repeated in a stream, Those slivers turned to profit like a trader’s perfect dream.

This trade is called arbitrage: buy low, then sell for more. Erebor paid the difference each time coins crossed its door. Most customers won’t chase a few basis points all day— But chasing tiny crypto gaps is Wintermute’s actual trade. Oops.

Erebor saw its losses and told the firms to stop, Then changed the free-conversion deal before more costs could drop. Customers without sizable deposits lost the offer’s perk; The bank had met the rare client whose whole job was making that perk work.

What happened? Erebor offered to redeem USDT and USDC for exactly $1 with no fee. Wintermute bought millions of dollars’ worth of discounted USDT, redeemed it at full value, and kept the difference until Erebor restricted the offer.

The Lunch That Never Arrives

One spring in South Korea, a curious craze sped round the world through headlines and screens in days. “Dopamine sites” offered the thrill of “Buy!” with no real package or price tag nearby.

Food Never Arrives served lunches pretend: you placed an order, and that was the end. No courier came, and the cost was just air; no salad or sandwich turned up at your chair.

Dopamine is linked to reward-seeking’s glow; these sites kept the wanting but let buying go. Supporters said shoppers could practice the fun, then skip owning stuff when the clicking was done.

The New York Times reported the craze spreading wide: smoke breaks with no smoke and no goods supplied. Anthropic and OpenAI build AI. Money Stuff pictured some Matrix-style sci-fi:

In vats powering Claude, this site would fit well— order a meal, get no sandwich or bell. A comforting click could keep everyone tame, while lunch never came and lunch never became.

Bloomberg Businessweek described stranger play: real money bought casino chips for the day, but winnings could never be cashed out for pay— just make-believe coins and some dopamine spray.

That raised one complaint: Why keep nothing free? Charge $12.99 for no lunch delivery!

What happened? Dopamine sites became popular by simulating shopping and meal orders without selling or delivering anything. Food Never Arrives charges nothing and sends no food, prompting the joke that it could charge $12.99 to deliberately not deliver a sandwich.
