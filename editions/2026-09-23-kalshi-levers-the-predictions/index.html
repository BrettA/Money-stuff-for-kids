IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Kalshi Klear

Kalshi Klear Adds a Lever

At Kalshi and Polymarket, Yes competes with No; one pays one dollar, and one pays zero. Together they cost a full dollar to buy, held safe for the winner when time has gone by.

That simple design needs no margin call: the money is waiting to cover it all. But Kalshi Klear asked the CFTC for leveraged contracts—quite carefully.

Suppose a big fund wants a $50 million chance that Ken Paxton wins, while the market odds dance. Instead of paying the whole price that day, it might post $5 million to enter the play.

But if Paxton loses, the payout is naught, and $45 million more must be brought. So this isn’t for casual customers online; approved, credit-checked traders must step to the line.

Klear would model bad moves with 99% confidence, then raise required collateral as an event gets tense. Near the final result, when prices may leap, full funding returns so the promise can keep.

Related positions may sometimes offset, if tests show their risks can be safely net. Kalshi already uses leverage for perpetuals galore— on gold, silver, and crypto, with stocks perhaps more.

But leveraged sports bets must stay on the bench; those contracts aren’t eligible—not even an inch. For comedy, that ending may feel incomplete: a futures exchange found leverage—just not for the meet.

What happened? Kalshi Klear LLC filed with the US Commodity Futures Trading Commission to use risk-based margin for certain event contracts offered through approved sophisticated participants. The framework would increase collateral as contracts near resolution and allow only carefully tested offsets between related positions.

IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Muse optimizing

Muse and the Money That Wouldn’t Sit Still

On Tuesday, bank and insurer shares slid, and online travel agencies did what they did. Not because one clever bot changed things that night, but investors feared what such helpers might.

Bloomberg’s Henry Ren reported the worry: AI could compare every price in a hurry. Muse, Meta Platforms’ personal AI, could keep hunting for better deals without getting tired or shy.

Today, bank customers often leave money in place, though another account pays a much better rate. That “consumer inertia”—not switching or checking— gives banks cheap deposits while interest keeps ticking.

Goldman Sachs Group said assistants like Muse and Instinct could question recurring bills and add-ons in a blink. Morgan Stanley warned that easier refinancing might mean more borrowers replace mortgages when lower rates are seen.

Then mortgage investors could ask for more interest in return, because faster refinancing gives them less time to earn. A helpful AI might save borrowers money one day— then make new mortgages costlier along the way.

Devin Ryan of Citizens told The Wall Street Journal that no single agent had changed finance wholesale. But if bots optimize twenty-four hours a day, could brokerages’ profitable idle cash melt away?

What happened? Investors sold shares of major banks, insurers, and online travel agencies because they feared AI agents could overcome customer inattention. By constantly moving deposits, refinancing mortgages, comparing prices, and reducing idle brokerage cash, AI could shrink profits and force financial firms to pay or charge market rates.

IN MATT'S ARTICLE: TPAI

The Dials That All Pointed to AI

Monte Tarbox watched New York’s retirement funds, spread among shares, private deals, debts, and infrastructure sums. “Stocks here, bonds there”—that’s allocation by kind, a plan meant to keep too much risk from combining.

A total portfolio approach looks underneath: What makes values rise? What might bring grief? Its dashboard may track fifty factors and show how a 1% change makes the whole portfolio go.

But AI had slipped into basket after basket: Tech giants led stocks; private funds backed AI projects. Big computing firms issued mountains of debt, while data centers filled infrastructure’s set.

Even US Treasuries joined the strange chain: AI needs more power, which can push prices again. Lu Wang of Bloomberg reported the twist: the usual promise of spreading risk might not exist.

Monte called this systematic risk—a force spread so wide, no single asset-class view could see every side. He could turn all the dials till each knob popped away, but when the economy is AI, the fund is AI anyway.

What happened? New York City Retirement Systems invested across stocks, private equity, corporate debt, infrastructure, and other assets, but Chief Investment Officer Monte Tarbox warned that AI affected all of them. Because those different investments shared the same AI-related economic risk, ordinary diversification could not fully remove it.

STORY 4
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: RIP 14a-8

The Proxy Proposal’s Last Vote

At U.S. public companies, Rule 14a-8 put shareholder proposals onto the slate. “Please report your carbon emissions,” one might say; the proxy voting packet would print it that way. The board could reply, “We advise voting no.” Then owners would choose—but the board need not follow.

Two percent “yes”? “Sweet,” the directors might say. At thirty or sixty, concern held more sway. Though still nonbinding, the vote was a sign: “Our owners may doubt how we manage and plan.” A proxy fight or takeover might then appear— and without owners’ support, boards had reason to fear.

Companies searched for small flaws they could cite, so proposals stayed out of the packet outright. Junior law associates picked rules apart; it filled rare little gaps when no deal work would start. One M&A lawyer’s LinkedIn joked of his time: “I did M, and sometimes A. All of the time. Also shareholder proxy proposal defense.” A grand-sounding job made of small-print suspense.

First, the SEC stopped reviewing each plea to exclude a proposal on some technicality. Now it has proposed that the whole rule should end; state law and company documents would govern instead. Owners could mail their own proxy cards for a fight, but that’s costly and hard for a nonbinding write-up. It means more power for boards—and less shareholder say; the former junior lawyer won’t miss that old workday.

What happened? The SEC proposed rescinding Rule 14a-8, which lets qualifying shareholders place nonbinding proposals in public companies’ proxy materials. Without it, access for those proposals would depend on state law and company governing documents, while shareholders could still pursue more expensive proxy fights.

STORY 5
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Pool servicing

The Great Pool-Care Roll-Up

In towns full of pools, little companies came to build, clean, and fix them, each under its name. The industry stayed mostly local and small— then private-equity owners came calling on all.

They called it a “platform”: one company grown by buying small services town after town. The hope was that hundreds, together in scale, could form one big business with nationwide reach.

SPS Poolcare and Pool Troopers grew vast; more than two hundred businesses joined them at last. Then earlier this year, the two firms combined— a pool-care roll-up, all newly entwined.

Main Street Capital bought, at the pandemic’s height, part of Cody Pools—an equity stake, or ownership right. On Main Street’s books, the result looked quite good: at seven times its purchase-time value, it stood.

Private equity sounded all polished and cool; its latest beloved trade was servicing pools. But top MBA students weren’t forming a line at Harvard and Stanford to scrub and to shine.

What happened? Private equity-backed SPS Poolcare and Pool Troopers bought more than 200 pool businesses between them and then merged. Main Street Capital bought a stake in Cody Pools during the pandemic and later marked its value up sevenfold.
