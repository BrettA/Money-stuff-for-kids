IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Everybody wants a pension

The Pension Came Back in a 401(k) Hat

A pension pools savings for thousands at once; professionals plan every retirement month. Some cash can stay invested for thirty long years, without one worried saver demanding it here.

That lets pensions buy private deals hard to sell fast; for waiting, they hope for returns unsurpassed. That possible bonus for keeping funds still is an “illiquidity premium”—patience pays the bill.

A 401(k), though, belongs to just you; an emergency might make you need it on cue. Some private-credit funds opened a five-percent door, but worried investors asked for fifteen, twenty—even forty percent more.

Stock funds once charged about one percent each year; index-fund fees of a few basis points made that shrink nearly disappear. Private equity, private credit, and real estate can still charge one percent or higher—a manager’s thrill.

In 2025, President Trump directed agencies to clear a path for private assets in 401(k) plans. Then the Wall Street Journal reported BlackRock’s design: public stocks, bonds, and private assets combined, plus annuities paying guaranteed income over time.

Nick Nefouse said BlackRock would blend what worked best: a 401(k)’s ownership with a pension’s long rest. Vanguard, Fidelity Investments, and State Street announced annuity funds too— a lifetime stream of retirement payments coming through.

So Wall Street’s old favorite returned with a twist: the pension-like structure it once thought it wouldn’t miss. Savers may gain from long investments held tight; managers gain higher fees. Everybody’s delighted.

What happened? BlackRock announced customizable 401(k) funds designed to invest more like pensions, including public securities, illiquid private assets, and annuities. Pooling and long-term management can reduce liquidity pressure and seek higher returns, while also allowing asset managers to charge higher private-market fees.

STORY 2
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Robinhood insider trading

The Token Tip Before the Bell

At Robinhood Crypto, before news took flight, two engineers learned which new coins it might invite. Hefu Chai and Huaisong Xiang, prosecutors say, used secrets to trade before announcement day.

On December 1, a private Slack note told Chai that AERO would launch on December 4 nearby. That morning on Hyperliquid, he opened a “long”— a wager that AERO’s price soon would grow strong.

Then Robinhood listed it, right on the clock; Chai closed the position after the public took stock. AERO rose from about sixty-eight cents on its way to nearly seventy-one cents at its peak that day.

Prosecutors say both engineers cleared more than $50,000 each from the trades they steered. Manhattan US Attorney Jamie McDonald announced the fraud charge— but one peculiar legal question was looming quite large.

An OpenSea conviction had been reversed the year before: Its secret homepage choices moved NFT prices, for sure. Yet the court said prosecutors had not shown that information had commercial value to OpenSea as an organization.

So Robinhood’s complaint stresses competitors, customers, and name: An early leak could let rival exchanges capture the game. The odd part is plain: everyone cares that a listing moves price, but the law asks whether Robinhood valued the secret advice.

What happened? US prosecutors charged former Robinhood employees Hefu Chai and Huaisong Xiang with fraud, alleging they used confidential cryptocurrency-listing plans to buy long perpetual futures on Hyperliquid before Robinhood’s announcements and earned more than $50,000 each. The complaints emphasize that the secret plans were commercially valuable to Robinhood, not merely important to token prices.

IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Epstein Kovel letters

The Kovel Letter and the Costly Tax Adviser

Jeffrey Epstein had no law-school degree, yet billionaires paid him for tax strategy. For work involving Leon Black, Apollo’s co-founder, his fees climbed higher and the questions grew louder.

Lawyers said his advice had saved a giant sum— one billion, perhaps two, when all the counting was done. But professor Victoria Haneman saw no dazzling new track: trusts, delayed taxes, and holding companies were familiar tools in the stack.

Then Bloomberg reporters Tom Schoenberg and Jeff Kao found why firms paid Epstein small consulting fees anyhow. They used Kovel letters, named for a 1961 case, to bring an outside expert into privilege’s protected space.

Attorney-client privilege keeps legal advice confidential. When an expert truly helps the lawyer, that protection can be essential. Some meetings, calls, emails, and texts could stay beneath that shield, while Epstein advised lawyers about Black and brought rich clients to the field.

Bruce Green of Fordham asked why Epstein was required: Was he truly the tax expert every lawyer desired? The strange choice seemed suspicious, considering Epstein’s terrible crimes— unless he was tax-cutting Michelangelo, just without the legal qualifications.

No grand invention bore his name; his methods looked well known. Yet enormous fees and guarded messages made his reputation grow. Was unmatched tax genius hiding behind that legal screen? “It’s hard to imagine,” said Green.

What happened? Law firms used Kovel letters to retain Jeffrey Epstein as an outside consultant for token fees, potentially extending attorney-client privilege to communications made while helping lawyers advise Leon Black. Experts questioned whether Epstein’s familiar tax-planning methods made him essential or justified his enormous fees.

IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Identifying talent early

The Banker Spotted in Fifth Grade

Once, college seniors could wander in late, Find a banking recruitment session—and alter their fate. After two analyst years, with some buyout work done, Private equity might look like the next bit of fun.

Now students seek buyout jobs before bank work begins, While competitive finance clubs decide who gets in. The race starts so early, one joke became clear: Join KKR’s waiting list before a baby is here!

But Bloomberg Businessweek found something stranger: A fifth-grade teacher had spotted a future deal-maker. She told one young student in New York, then and there, “You’d make a terrific investment banker,” with flair.

That student, now a college junior, has quite a next summer: He’ll intern at Evercore—so the teacher picked a winner. But what could she possibly notice that day? Was he modeling cash flows while classmates all played?

A discounted cash flow estimates what a firm may be worth, Using its future cash in today’s-dollar terms. Or was it his handshake, his golf, or his math? Did Pokémon traders all come to ask for his path? Was his father a CEO? No answer appears— Just one teacher predicting Wall Street across years.

What happened? Wall Street recruiting has shifted earlier, sometimes requiring finance-club experience and modeling skills before college ends. A New York college junior who will intern at Evercore was first encouraged toward investment banking by his fifth-grade teacher.
