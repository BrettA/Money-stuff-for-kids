IN MATT'S ARTICLE: SXSI/Tesla

One Last Conflict Before One Big Company

Elon Musk built many things, each under its own name: Tesla made electric cars; SpaceX launched flame. There were tunnels, brain implants, and Twitter posts too— Different pots of investor cash for each project to do.

But now the projects increasingly meet: They need AI models, power, cooling, and heat. Tesla wants Nvidia chips for robots and cars; SpaceX wants compute on Earth—and perhaps near the stars.

With separate shareholders, choices can sting: Which company gets each scarce chip or engineer to build its thing? Combine them, Musk said, and resources could flow Wherever one giant AI company most needed them to go.

The proposed all-stock merger might be worth $3.5 trillion or so. An exchange ratio must decide where everyone’s shares would go. Banks may give “fairness opinions,” checking the price; At a 0.1% fee, $3.5 billion would be rather nice.

But Musk controls about 20% of Tesla stock and 84% of SpaceX’s vote, So voting rights, control, and fairness cannot be a footnote. Independent special committees must bargain out of his sight— Though he joked it was an “odd kabuki dance” to make the deal right.

One man could bargain with himself in five minutes flat, But rules say the conflicted boss cannot do that. So to end years of conflicts beneath one corporate roof, They may need one last conflicted deal—with committees checking the proof.

What happened? Elon Musk endorsed a possible all-stock merger of Tesla and SpaceX, and both boards formed independent special committees to consider the terms. A merger could unify AI resources and shareholders, but the committees must determine a fair exchange ratio, voting control, and treatment of Musk’s interests.

STORY 2
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Withdrawal strategies

Capital Group’s Two Simple Choices

Michelle Black and Capital Group tested each market surprise, so savings might last through retirement’s falls and its rises. Eleven million 401(k) savers held shares— nearly half a trillion dollars was theirs.

Their target-date funds invest for the year work should end. Then comes the puzzle: How much can a retiree spend? Capital Group researched two choices to show how withdrawals might work as the years come and go.

First: take 4% to 4.5% when retirement begins, then keep that dollar amount, raised as inflation climbs. Or check the account at each year’s end, and take 5% to 5.5% of the balance to spend.

Vanguard Group, BlackRock, and Fidelity built annuity options in, offering guaranteed lifetime income when payments begin. But Michelle said Capital’s framework could change; an annuity, once purchased, cannot be rearranged.

An old-fashioned pension might say, “$5,000 each month.” Capital’s “simple” plan had two ranges, inflation, and sums. The research was careful; the choices were fine— but “simple” had somehow become quite a lot of design.

What happened? Capital Group announced two research-backed withdrawal strategies for people holding its target-date funds: a fixed, inflation-adjusted approach and a yearly percentage-of-balance approach. Unlike competitors Vanguard Group, BlackRock, and Fidelity, Capital Group did not add an annuity option.

STORY 3
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Significant liquidity

Todd Boehly’s Significant Liquidity

Todd Boehly announced, with magnificent dignity: “I’ll be coming into significant liquidity, personally.” He’d exit investments; once everything sold, they’d generate billions—a mountain of gold.

Bloomberg’s Alexandre Rajbhandari and Allison McNeely wrote it that day. Jules Feiffer once said of David Shaw’s offered pay, “They offered me real money,” and he wanted it that way. Todd’s “liquidity” made getting rich sound grander to say.

Security Benefit sells annuities: customers pay, while promised cash may not come due for years and a day. That leaves a long-lasting pool to invest with great care; higher returns can bring profit, but risk travels there.

Through Eldridge Industries, Boehly can steer money toward affiliated investments each year. That can be legal when every link is disclosed, so regulators can study how much is exposed.

Security Benefit reported that 42% of its invested holdings were affiliated assets, all clearly identified. Across U.S. life insurers, the average was about 7% at the end of the previous year.

Mark Walter, Boehly’s longtime associate, used the strategy too; their profits helped buy the Los Angeles Dodgers crew. But Walter’s insurers mislabeled connected loans on the list: “non-affiliated,” they said, hiding some of the risk.

Todd’s long disclosures put him on firmer ground; the strangest part was the purpose was plain all around: buy an insurer, invest its cash in Boehly’s cool stuff, say so clearly—and that can be fair enough.

What happened? Security Benefit used long-term insurance and annuity funds for investments, including affiliated assets equal to 42% of its holdings, and disclosed those ties. Walter’s insurers instead reported affiliated loans as non-affiliated, making their investments appear safer to regulators.
