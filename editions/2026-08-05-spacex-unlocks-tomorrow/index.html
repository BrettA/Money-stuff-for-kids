STORY 1
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: SpaceX unlock

The Shares That Arrived Tomorrow

After Elon Musk’s SpaceX held its June IPO, about 639 million shares were free to come and go. Insiders held many more, but a lockup said, “Wait”— a rule that blocked their selling until a set date.

On Thursday, up to 911.5 million could clear the gate, making 1.55 billion shares available to trade. That meant $101 billion in stock could join the supply; with so much more for sale, would the price wave goodbye?

“Sell high today, then buy cheaper next day!” But short sellers must borrow the shares they sell this way. They sell borrowed shares before the lockup’s release, then buy shares to return, hoping prices decrease.

Their selling can press down today’s price; their buying tomorrow can lift it up twice. That is arbitrage, closing the gap traders see— which means “cheaper tomorrow” may cease to be.

Bloomberg’s Bailey Lipschultz described the unlock; S3 Partners found 35% already sold short in the stock.

Peter Singlehurst of Baillie Gifford called them “uncharted waters.” David Wagner of Aptus Capital Advisors said, “We are in that same exact boat.”

What happened? SpaceX’s scheduled lockup release was set to make up to 911.5 million additional shares tradable, more than doubling the available supply. Short sellers had already borrowed and sold shares, effectively selling tomorrow’s supply today and arbitraging the expected price drop.

STORY 2
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Situational Awareness

The Fund That Sold Against Itself

Leopold Aschenbrenner’s fund made an AI bet, And Situational Awareness grew huge on it yet. Reports put its peak near $45 billion in all, With some bets levered four or five times tall.

Leverage means borrowed money can multiply gains— But it also makes lenders get jumpy at pains. When AI shares sank, banks called: “Cash is now due!” So the fund sold some stocks to pay what it drew.

But its holdings were huge, and the market took note; Each sale pushed down prices of stocks in its boat. Lower prices brought margin calls, urgent and new, Which forced further selling—and lower prices too.

Bob Sloan of S3 Partners found no clear sign That short-selling predators had formed a grand line. The stranger explanation was sitting in view: Situational Awareness was selling its own stocks askew.

At last, most public shares went in one giant block: Citadel bought $16 billion to $20 billion in stock. The fund kept perhaps $8 billion to $10 billion instead, Unlevered, with many private holdings still spread.

Ken Griffin’s Citadel bought at a discount that day; Its Wellington fund gained 5.9% in July’s fray. The forced seller stopped, and the shares could rebound— Once nobody had to keep shoving them down.

What happened? Falling AI-stock prices triggered margin calls, so Situational Awareness sold shares for cash; those large sales helped depress its own holdings and caused more calls. It ultimately sold most of its public-stock portfolio to Citadel in a block trade that removed all leverage.

IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Sports gambling: New York

New York and the Two Betting Rulebooks

New York says sports bettors must be twenty-one, A rule meant to curb harm—not spoil anyone’s fun. But Kalshi offers contracts on how games will end, And lets eighteen-year-olds trade from a phone in their hand.

The CFTC once watched wheat, gold, and Treasury trades, Keeping prices honest and blocking market charades. Now it oversees sports markets, including each bet On the Knicks’ total score: Will it go over—or not yet?

Its proposed sports rules filled 468 pages with care, But gambling addiction was scarcely found there. Page 202 named cooling-off periods and limits on play, Yet proposed no firm protections—or age rule—that day.

So Attorney General Letitia James took Kalshi to court, Calling its New York operation unlicensed sport. Governor Kathy Hochul said state rules guard people from harm; Kalshi spokeswoman Elisabeth Diana raised the alarm.

She called the lawsuit “sad” political theater and said A federally licensed exchange cannot simply be shed. Kalshi warned that blocked New Yorkers might gamble offshore— While state and federal rulebooks collided at the door.

What happened? New York sued Kalshi’s trading subsidiary, alleging that its federally regulated sports-event contracts are illegal, unlicensed gambling under state law. Kalshi argues that CFTC oversight prevents states from shutting it down, even though the federal system lacks the state’s age-21 rule and other standard gambling protections.

STORY 4
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Sports gambling: Robinhood

Robinhood’s Football Season

Robinhood Markets put trades on a phone: Stocks and stock options, commission-free, from home. Young traders chased meme stocks, quick thrills, and cheers; some hoped they’d choose index funds in later years.

An index fund holds many companies at once, not one risky option that may leap or slump. Those broad groups of stocks can patiently grow— a calmer retirement road as the decades go.

Then came a twist to that sensible track: Instead of less risk, riskier fun pulled them back. Prediction markets let users trade on what might come true; sports bets now passed through the brokerage too.

More than ten times last year’s, $156 million came through; that was one-fifth of trading revenue in quarter two. It beat stocks and crypto for the very first time; only options brought Robinhood more in that line.

Dan Dolev of Mizuho Securities said gambling fit the crowd, with faster brain rewards than crypto allowed. Ed Engel of Compass Point said World Cup play made June and July abnormally strong each day.

Football season could bring the next boost, Ed wrote. A strange thing for a stock brokerage to note: The autumn rush wasn’t for companies at all—it was people in brokerage accounts betting on football.

What happened? Robinhood let brokerage customers trade prediction-market contracts on real-world events, including sports outcomes. In the second quarter, that business produced $156 million—20% of Robinhood’s trading revenue—surpassing both stock and crypto revenue.
