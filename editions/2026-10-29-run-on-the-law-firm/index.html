IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Weil

The Run on Weil

At Weil Gotshal & Manges, by Central Park’s southeast side, a nearly century-old law firm worked with corporate pride. It brought in nearly $2.1 billion just last year, yet prominent partners started leaving—and the future looked less clear.

Bloomberg’s Roy Strom reported the firm would soon wind down; employees heard Barry Wolf on Zoom, with somber looks around. Wolf was set to yield control; merger talks had failed as well— the talks with Willkie Farr & Gallagher could not rescue Weil.

Yale’s John Morley explained how partner runs can grow: a “rainmaker” brings clients—and their fees may also go. But office rent and staff costs do not vanish through the door; with less profit left to share, the next star may want more.

A rival offers better pay; another takes the flight, which makes departure seem still wiser to the partners left that night. The firm may still be profitable—that’s the strange surprise: partners see what could come later, so they hurry toward the exits.

And lawyers’ ethics rules generally bar noncompetes that block the way; the freedom each lawyer prizes lets the whole firm drain away. A rule that helps each lawyer leave for somewhere good and new can help unravel the profitable firm they’re leaving, too.

What happened? After prominent partner exits and failed merger talks with Willkie Farr & Gallagher, Weil Gotshal & Manges said it would wind down despite earning nearly $2.1 billion in revenue the prior year. Because client-bringing partners can leave quickly while fixed costs remain, each exit lowers expected profits and encourages more departures, creating a self-fulfilling run.

The Very Serious Onion March

Outside the Chicago Mercantile Exchange one day, University of Chicago students gave onions away.

For Our Futures marched, with Adam Ash at the fore: “Let onion-futures trading be banned no more!”

A future lets traders agree now to pay a set price for onions delivered someday.

That trade has been banned in the U.S. for sixty-eight years, so the students brought onions and perfectly serious cheers.

Was this performance art? Was it PR on display? Was it a joke for The Onion? They solemnly said, “No way.”

Adam said it wasn’t America’s biggest law at all; that was why changing it could be a practical call.

Stronger rules against market tricks now exist, they explained, so why should this odd prohibition remain?

Bloomberg’s Erin Ailworth reported the scene, and finance experts debated what trading should mean.

Kalshi’s Tarek Mansour called the students “patriots” online; a company that favors more tradable events liked their design.

But Kalshi joined only after the rally was planned and declared, so it wasn’t strictly sponsored content—just kind of, once shared.

What happened? For Our Futures rallied to repeal the 68-year-old U.S. ban on onion futures, which are contracts to trade onions later at a price agreed upon now. Kalshi became involved only after the students had planned and announced the event.

The Bets That Must—and Mustn’t—Be Offered

For 130 years, Utah’s rule has been clear: “No sports-gambling bets may be offered in here.” But Kalshi let customers trade contracts on games, with payouts decided by outcomes and names.

Kalshi asked federal court: “Can Utah’s law apply?” The judge answered yes, rejecting its reply. Still Utahns could trade on sports events online. Utah officials said, “That crosses the line.”

On Wednesday, Utah filed criminal charges and named Kalshi Inc., Tarek Mansour, and Luana Lopes Lara as defendants in the complaint. Attorney General Derek Brown said the court had been clear: “Kalshi kept taking bets. We’re stopping it here.”

Kalshi called it “crazy overreach” and politics in play, and said it would fight the charges in court right away. But Kalshi’s federal regulator—the CFTC— had issued a very different decree.

Using “statutory emergency powers,” it ordered nationwide trade, and sued to overturn Utah’s ban as it stayed. So offering bets was illegal, Utah declared; not offering bets broke federal orders Kalshi was required to obey.

Kalshi helped tie this remarkable snare, with two opposite commands both waiting there. Perhaps the Supreme Court will someday decide which government’s order the market must abide.

What happened? Utah charged Kalshi and its co-founders after Kalshi continued offering sports bets despite a federal ruling that Utah’s gambling laws applied. At the same time, the CFTC ordered federally regulated Kalshi to keep offering its markets nationwide, creating directly conflicting commands.
