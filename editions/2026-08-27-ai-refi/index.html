The Home Loan That Only Floats Down

A thirty-year mortgage can fix what you pay: 6.65 percent, though rates change every day. If rates rise to eight, your old bargain stays; if they fall to 4.5, you can seek cheaper ways.

“Refinance” means a new loan pays off the old, so lower-rate savings can steadily unfold. But forms take time, and the process is slow; many busy homeowners simply don’t go.

Bloomberg’s Jack Trapanick and Scott Carpenter found that AI may turn those delays upside down. Rocket Mortgage says rate lock can take thirty minutes; it aims for just ten, with less paperwork in it.

United Wholesale Mortgage says fifteen may do. Better.com claims it can finish in two. Today, only one-third who could save choose to try; Morgan Stanley says that could reach sixty with AI.

Jay Bacow and Morgan Stanley gave it a name: “a floating-rate instrument that only floats down”—what a game! Borrowers keep fixed rates whenever rates rise, then swiftly refinance when cheaper ones arrive.

But investors lose higher payments sooner that way, so they may demand extra interest from day one’s pay: perhaps 0.1 or 0.2 percentage points more, the price of making refinancing less of a chore.

What happened? AI is speeding mortgage approvals, which could help far more homeowners replace high-rate loans when interest rates fall. Morgan Stanley estimated that this added refinancing risk could make new mortgages cost one or two tenths of a percentage point more.

IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Evergreen funds

The Evergreen Door Opens Wide

For years, institutions would promise a pile, then wait for a deal—perhaps quite a while. A $10 billion drawdown fund wasn’t cash in a drawer; its backers wired pieces when managers called for more.

That message—a capital call—meant, “Send money today.” Till then, backers could keep their cash working away. And because IRR’s clock starts when called cash arrives, a later call can help that return score thrive.

But asking thousands with $5,000 apiece for $500 later brought no one much peace. Some people might move; capital-call letters might stray, so funds built for individuals chose a new way.

An evergreen takes all the cash on day one and welcomes new money whenever it comes. It may offer withdrawals at regular turns, but cash held to meet them can lower returns.

The Financial Times spotted a turn in the queue: big institutions wanted that convenience too. At Blackstone, Joan Solotar noted their share was only a small percentage—but institutions were there.

At KKR, Eric Mogelof answered demand with institutional share classes ready at hand. Hugh MacArthur of Bain & Company said this wish for convenience would keep moving ahead.

How strange: the individual product felt better to institutions tired of each capital-call letter. The service was easier—but not every way: lower target returns were part of the trade.

What happened? Institutions began investing in Blackstone and KKR evergreen funds, which collect cash upfront, accept investments over time, and permit withdrawals at intervals. Traditional drawdown funds instead call committed capital as deals arise, often locking investors in for about 10 years.

IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Elsewhere in underpriced options

The Offer That Came Back With a Bill

Tang Lin worked at Dymon, where portfolios were her trade; Then Millennium offered a job, and joining plans were made.

Millennium Management, a hedge fund of ninety-two billion dollars, Prepared for Tang to come aboard—then heard she would not follow.

Tang chose to stay at Dymon. Millennium said, “But still, We spent to get your post prepared, and now we have a bill.”

It sued in court in Hong Kong for HK$19.7 million— About $2.5 million in claimed preparation costs, a sizable sum.

This switch is called “gazumping,” a word from selling homes: You first accept one deal, then choose another before the closing comes.

An outside offer can be useful when asking for better pay: It proves another firm might hire you—though Tang’s package isn’t known today.

So here’s the funny possibility—though no one knows the score: If Millennium helped Tang win much more, its giant bill might still cost less than what the offer did for her.

What happened? Tang Lin accepted a job offer from Millennium Management but ultimately remained at Dymon Asia Capital. Millennium sued her for HK$19.7 million, or about $2.5 million, claiming costs it incurred while preparing for her arrival.
