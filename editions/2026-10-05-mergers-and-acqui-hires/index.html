IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Reverse acqui-hire

The Company That Wasn’t Bought

Groq built special chips that made AI run, And Nvidia wanted the work it had done. But Nvidia didn’t buy Groq outright; That sort of merger draws regulators’ sight.

Instead, Groq licensed its technology’s use— “Nonexclusive,” so Groq kept it too. Its board let Nvidia hire key people away, Including Jonathan Ross and Sunny Madra that day.

The deal’s headline value was twenty billion in all: Seventeen billion in cash went to Groq’s hall. Most went to shareholders; three billion more Was set aside for employees walking through Nvidia’s door.

No voting stock or company assets were bought, So no premerger notice was required, it was thought. Groq remained independent—at least in its name— Though its leaders and builders had left all the same.

Two engineer-shareholders brought suit in Delaware court: “The board skipped our vote and sold Groq’s heart!” they report. They said Ross helped negotiate while wanted as a hire, And received Nvidia equity when he crossed through the wire.

So was Groq sold? On paper, no. Its shares and assets did not go. But people plus ideas were mostly the prize— “Oh, come on, that’s a merger!” was the lawsuit’s cry.

What happened? Nvidia paid Groq $17 billion for a nonexclusive technology license and allocated another $3 billion to employees joining Nvidia, while Groq remained legally independent. Two engineer-shareholders sued, and the Justice Department is investigating whether the structure avoided antitrust scrutiny.

IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Anthropic charitable giving

Anthropic’s Very Unusual Match

Anthropic sought researchers skilled at AI, For the finest could help its new models fly. Many believed effective gifts could do good, And pledged chunks of valuable stock when they could.

For early employees who joined by 2024, Anthropic’s match made each promised gift more: Workers could pledge half their stock, if they chose, And a three-for-one company match joined the rows.

Newer employees received a smaller deal: Up to one quarter, matched one-for-one, share for share. It helped Anthropic recruit people who cared, While charities gained from the stock workers shared.

Cory Weinberg reported the curious sum: More than $660 million had come As a non-cash expense over six months, investors were told, From October 2025 through March as shares grew like gold.

But Anthropic left that great cost out when it chose Its “adjusted operating profit” to show. Most firms do not subtract giant gifts made in stock; Calcbench’s Ghai said the adjustment could shock.

The joke was that this looked like charity set free— Yet also like employee compensation, unusually. Anthropic could hire generous experts that way, Then adjust out the gifts when presenting its pay.

What happened? Anthropic matched stock that employees pledged to charities, using three-to-one matches for eligible early employees and smaller one-to-one matches for newer hires. It reported more than $660 million of related non-cash expense for six months but excluded that expense from adjusted operating profit.

IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Analyst programs

The Offer That Came First

At Citi, David Friedland announced something new: “Our three-year analyst program is shortening to two.” For junior investment bankers, “associate” is the next chair— A faster path upward might help keep them there.

Start at Citi in summer 2026, Then January brings private equity’s quick tricks. The buyout firms interview, though year one isn’t done, For jobs starting fall 2028—offers already won.

Would analysts reject one and patiently wait For a possible Citi promotion in 2028? Perhaps banking looked better; perhaps they would stay. But the firm offer came early, with promotion far away.

JPMorgan tried a much sterner defense: Take an outside offer within eighteen months—out you went. Private equity firms, avoiding Jamie Dimon’s wrath, Paused their early hiring, then returned to the path.

Analysts who started in June 2025 Saw recruiting begin the next January alive. That’s roughly seven months—not eighteen. So then: Were offers kept secret? Were analysts fired? Does Dimon know when?

What happened? Citi cut its analyst-to-associate track from three years to two, but private equity recruiting still offered future jobs much earlier. JPMorgan’s threatened 18-month firing rule delayed that recruiting only until January 2026, about seven months after its analysts started.

STORY 4
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Doughnutting

The Hole in the Train-Fare Doughnut

Ian Wace of Marshall Wace once set a test: Cross Europe cheaply, and outthink the rest. In twenty-four hours, use transport galore— One reached far-off Poland on about thirty or more.

Another stayed in London, through embassies he’d roam, Finding “many countries” without leaving home. Wace called that “too cute”—then hired him, too: In finance, clever loopholes can make you look shrewd.

An unnamed HSBC banker tried cleverness by rail, But this was no hiring game where tricks could prevail. From Orpington to Canary Wharf, he traveled each day, Buying tickets at both ends—but not all the way.

He paid for two edges and skipped the middle round: A fare-free “doughnut hole” where no ticket was found. Almost £6,000 in journeys went unpaid, While a Jobcentre Plus discount cut prices in aid.

That fifty-percent discount was for job seekers alone; He worked at HSBC, so it wasn’t his own. Convicted of fraud, he faced the watchdog’s ban: No more financial-services work for this former banking man.

And here was the twist in his over-clever quest: He led passive equity at HSBC Asset Management. Passive funds track an index instead of making bold bets— Yet his commute was extremely active and complex.

What happened? A former HSBC banker bought tickets covering only short sections at the beginning and end of his commute, leaving the middle unpaid, and improperly used a 50% Jobcentre Plus discount. After dodging almost £6,000 in fares, he was convicted of fraud and banned from UK financial services.
