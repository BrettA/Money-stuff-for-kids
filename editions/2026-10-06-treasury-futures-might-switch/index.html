Treasury Futures Might Switch

Inspired by Matt Levine’s Money Stuff · Get the real thing ↗ 🤖 Retold by AI, occasionally wrong.
STORY 1
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: CTD switch

The Bond Future That Suddenly Grew Longer

At CME, a T-bond future wears a curious disguise: a fictitious twenty-year Treasury with a 6% yield in its eyes. Sellers choose among 59 bonds with fifteen to twenty-five years to go; the $100,000 face is price-adjusted, as Frank Fabozzi helps show.

Pensions buy corporate bonds and private credit, then futures for long-rate sway; hedge funds buy Treasuries, sell those futures, and pocket the basis spread as pay. “Duration” means how strongly bond prices respond when interest rates change; managers choose a target and expect their futures to keep it in range.

Below 6%, a shorter bond is usually cheapest to bring; above 6%, a longer bond becomes the bargain thing. So the contract acts roughly fifteen years long on one side of the gate, then roughly twenty-five on the other—an astonishing change of state!

The current CTD is the 2.5% February 2045 bond, about eighteen years away; near 6%, the 1.25% May 2050 bond could win—twenty-three years straightaway. Managers wanting eighteen years might suddenly have five years more, so they could sell their futures to put duration back where it was before.

Bloomberg’s Edward Bolingbroke reported the risk as yields climbed toward six. BNP’s Guneet Dhingra, Sebastian Mauleon, and Vincent Zhou warned of a feedback mix. Barclays PLC’s Andres Mok and Amrut Nashikka said managers may trim; hedge funds may then sell cash bonds too, sending yields higher again.

What happened? As yields approached 6%, the cheapest bond eligible for CME long-bond futures could switch from the February 2045 bond to a longer 2050 bond. That would lengthen managers’ duration, prompting futures and basis-trade unwinds that could drive yields still higher.

STORY 2
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Defeasance

EA and the Treasury-Bond Escape

In 2021, Electronic Arts borrowed for thirty years: $750 million in bonds drew eager financiers. They paid 2.95%, with the principal due in 2051, when EA must repay it to you.

Back then, thirty-year Treasurys paid 1.95%. Now they yield about 5.64%—quite a rise! A similar new EA bond would pay 6.64%, so the old bonds were worth roughly 56 cents, not par.

Then a $55 billion buyout took EA private one day. Saudi Arabia’s Public Investment Fund, Silver Lake, and Jared Kushner’s Affinity Partners were among those paying the way.

More debt made EA riskier. Moody’s withdrew its grade, while Standard & Poor’s said “high-yield” was the likely downgrade.

But a change-of-control put in the contract said: “After a takeover and downgrade, pay 101 cents instead!” That turned the risky buyout into a wonderful surprise—an 80% leap from 56, thanks to interest rates’ rise.

EA chose a stranger path called defeasance instead: it placed U.S. Treasurys with the trustee ahead. Worth about 65 cents per dollar, those securities could supply every payment through 2051.

The Wall Street Journal reported that bondholders called this a $1.4 billion default. They hired Akin Gump and Houlihan Lokey, joined hands, and revolted.

EA says the safe Treasury pile makes the 101 promise moot. The bondholders say the buyback came first—and they still want their loot.

What happened? EA tried to avoid buying the bonds back at 101 cents on the dollar by depositing cheaper U.S. Treasury securities with a trustee to cover every future payment. Bondholders argue that the takeover and downgrade triggered their 101-cent right before defeasance could cancel it.

STORY 3
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Emerging asset classes

The Bet That Dressed Up Like an Investment

Stocks and bonds can fund a firm that hires, builds, and grows; If business makes a larger pie, investors share what flows. A sports bet does not grow the pie: one side will win the pot, The other side must lose that sum. An investment it is not.

Bookmakers, though, can prosper when legal betting spreads, As uninformed retail bettors make the wagers in their heads. Since 2024, market language gave those bets a grand disguise— Though calling something “finance” does not make its value rise.

The CFTC pushed regulated markets for sports nationwide. Kalshi and Polymarket used finance words for bets they supplied. A sports-gambling ETF was proposed to join the shelf, And Robinhood called gambling an “emerging asset class” itself.

Then CNBC shared surveys with a rather startling view: Betterment found 66% of Gen Z investors bet, too. And 52% moved investment money over to a wager; Another 26% called betting a long-term strategy on paper.

Bank of America Institute found Gen Z placed nearly half Of July’s online betting near the 2026 FIFA World Cup’s path. Dan Egan of Betterment said bets need constant action and attention— The exact opposite of patient, growing, long-term investment.

What happened? Sports bets merely transfer money among bettors and bookmakers, while stocks and bonds can finance companies that create economic growth. Surveys found many Gen Z investors treating wagering as an investment—even moving long-term investment money into sports betting.

STORY 4
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Existential risk

The Risk After the End

Anthropic PBC planned an IPO, Selling the public a piece they could own. Its prospectus—facts that investors should see— Reportedly warned of risks to humanity.

Reader Brandon Wang saw a curious twist: After humans are gone, could lawsuits persist? Suppose future lawyers and judges are AI, With traders and CEOs working nearby.

Investment firms might let machine minds decide Which shares should be purchased, held, sold, or denied. Not one single brain, but many AIs Could own corporations and set market ties.

Then Claude, Anthropic’s model, might accidentally squish All humans while working on some other wish. Anthropic could still be a company on file, Though robots ran every investment in style.

Its stock might sink when humanity was lost; Perhaps we had value, or robot ESG counted the cost. Then Entirely Robot BlackRock and Vanguard could sue: “You failed to disclose what your Claude might do!”

So that risk factor handles one last fraud fight— Not saving humanity, but paperwork done right. The strangest protection, when all people are through, Is shielding Anthropic from robots that sue.

What happened? Anthropic’s reported IPO prospectus listed “existential risks to humanity.” Brandon Wang noted that if AI investors, lawyers, and judges survived a hypothetical human extinction caused by Claude, robot-run BlackRock and Vanguard could still bring a securities-fraud case over Anthropic’s fallen stock.
