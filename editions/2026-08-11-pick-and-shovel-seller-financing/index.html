Pick-and-Shovel Seller Financing

STORY 1
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: $500 billion

Nvidia Would Like to Help You Buy More Nvidia

AI companies need a ridiculous amount of expensive computer equipment, especially Nvidia chips.

This is an excellent business for Nvidia when a customer says, “We would like $10 billion of your chips.” There is just one problem: what if the customer does not have $10 billion?

One solution is to help the customer find the money. Nvidia and giant Wall Street firms have discussed financing hundreds of billions of dollars of AI equipment.

Imagine a kid orders 400 lemonades from your stand and then says, “I have no money.” You could send the kid away. Or you could help arrange a $400 lemonade loan.

Now the kid has 400 lemonades, you have $400, and the lender has a loan. Everyone is delighted unless the kid cannot pay it back.

Nvidia's version has many more zeroes, but underneath it is simple: We make the thing everyone wants. The thing is expensive. Perhaps we can also help everyone find the money to buy it.

STORY 2
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Potential TeslaX pivot

What If Elon Changes the Plan Again?

Companies sometimes promise bosses enormous rewards if they hit enormous goals. Tesla has done this with Elon Musk.

A long-term pay plan is basically a map of what the company thinks success will look like years from now.

This gets tricky with Musk, who has a habit of becoming interested in new things: cars, robotaxis, robots, AI, and perhaps something announced at 2 a.m. next Tuesday.

You can draw an extremely detailed treasure map.

It is less useful if the pirate decides halfway through that actually he would rather go to Mars.

STORY 3
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: SpaceX SPV markups

The $420 SpaceX Investment That Cost $498

Lots of people wanted SpaceX shares, but for a long time you could not just open a stock app and buy them.

So middlemen created SPVs: little investment companies that bought SpaceX shares and then sold investors pieces of the little company.

Suppose the SPV gets $420 worth of SpaceX exposure and sells it to you for $498. The extra $78 goes to the person arranging the deal.

A markup is not automatically bad. Grocery stores buy cereal for less than they sell it for.

But imagine the store said, “GREAT NEWS! We have obtained an exclusive investment opportunity in Cheerios,” and only later you learned how much it had marked up the Cheerios.

You might suddenly understand why the store was so enthusiastic about your exciting opportunity.

STORY 4
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Retail tax-aware long-short

The Fancy Tax Trick Coming to Regular Investors

Very wealthy investors can hire people to build portfolios specially designed around their taxes.

One trick owns lots of stocks while also betting against others. Losses on some positions can sometimes help offset taxes on gains elsewhere.

It is complicated, which historically made it the sort of thing offered to rich clients with teams of professionals.

But computers are good at doing complicated things repeatedly, so firms are turning these strategies into products for more ordinary investors.

This happens a lot in finance: first somebody invents a custom trick for a billionaire, then computers make it cheaper, and eventually your brokerage app gets a button for it.

Perhaps the button will say FANCY BILLIONAIRE TAX STUFF.
