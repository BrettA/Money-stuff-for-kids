Leveraged ETF Crash Hedging ETF

Elementary School edition.

STORY 1
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Leveraged ETF crash put cliquet ETF

The Bank’s Crash Umbrella

Some funds borrow money so a stock’s daily move feels twice as big.

Usually they rebalance every day, but a huge one-day crash can leave the bank short.

So the bank buys a special crash umbrella called a crash put.

THE MONEY IDEA
Borrowing can make good and bad moves bigger.

The ETF That Needs Insurance for a Very Bad Day

A 2x leveraged ETF tries to move about twice as much as a stock each day. A simple version is investors put in $100, the fund borrows $100, and it gets $200 of stock exposure.

Normally the ETF rebalances every day. But if the stock falls more than about 50% in one session, the investors’ money can disappear before the bank is fully repaid. Matt Levine points to a leveraged ETF on Lucid that was wiped out during a 57% intraday fall.

Banks therefore buy crash puts, including daily-reset options called cliquets.

Then finance gets recursive: the insurance can be packaged in a structured note, and sellers may add leverage to make the small insurance premium more interesting. Leverage creates the risk, and leverage can show up again in the hedge.

Crash Puts for Leveraged ETFs

Leveraged ETFs rebalance daily, which usually protects lenders as collateral shrinks.

A sufficiently large one-day gap can still wipe out fund equity before the lender is repaid.

Banks hedge that tail with crash puts or cliquets; pre-funded structures reduce counterparty risk but can introduce more leverage.

THE MONEY IDEA
Extreme gap risk survives ordinary daily rebalancing.

Cliquets, Gap Risk and Leveraged ETF Tail Insurance

A daily-reset leveraged ETF deleverages after losses, but it can become insolvent before rebalancing if the underlying falls far enough in one session.

Deep-out-of-the-money daily-reset puts transfer part of that tail risk.

Pre-funded structured notes reduce counterparty exposure, while leverage on the note can leave the deepest tail with the bank.

THE MONEY IDEA
Daily-reset leverage creates discontinuous gap risk.
STORY 2
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Tax-aware long-short

A Hedge Fund Brags About Losses

Usually an investment company does not brag about losses.

AQR tries to make money overall while also creating losses that can help clients with taxes.

So a pile of losses can oddly be part of the product.

THE MONEY IDEA
An investment loss can sometimes reduce a tax bill.

The Hedge Fund That Wants to Lose $580 Million

AQR Capital Management, led by Cliff Asness, has become huge partly because wealthy investors really like tax losses.

Tax-loss harvesting means selling an investment that fell, recording the loss, and replacing it with something similar so the portfolio still behaves roughly the way you want.

AQR pushes this further with tax-aware long-short investing: own more stocks, short other stocks, and use quantitative models to create many more chances to realize losses without giving up the market exposure the client wanted.

Bloomberg reported an AQR example where $100 million could potentially triple over ten years while generating more than $580 million of tax losses. Normally a hedge fund promising almost six times your investment in losses would sound terrible. Here it is the brochure.

AQR’s Tax-Loss Machine

Tax-aware long-short portfolios use leverage, shorts and stock substitution to increase realizable losses while preserving factor exposure.

Tax rules make simple sell-and-rebuy tricks difficult, so managers use different but economically similar securities.

The comic inversion is that an industrial-scale loss factory can be a premium financial product.

THE MONEY IDEA
Quantitative substitution lets investors harvest losses while preserving exposure.

Tax Alpha as the New Efficient-Market Alpha

Tax-aware long-short applies quantitative finance to after-tax returns, increasing gross exposure and opportunities to realize losses.

Factor models help managers find economically similar but legally distinct replacement positions.

Levine’s twist is that traditional alpha may get scarcer while tax alpha grows as more intelligence is aimed at the tax system.

THE MONEY IDEA
Financial engineering can improve after-tax returns by increasing usable losses.
STORY 3
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Commodity market manipulation

The Bet About Whether You Will Show Up

There was a market about whether George Santos would attend the State of the Union.

George Santos could decide whether George Santos went.

He also traded the market and posted things that affected its price.

THE MONEY IDEA
A bet is strange when one player controls the answer.

George Santos Bet on George Santos

Kalshi had a prediction market asking whether former congressman George Santos would attend the State of the Union. One unusually informed trader was George Santos.

According to a CFTC settlement, Santos traded the contract and posted about his plans. He first held Yes, later built a No position, and on the event day said he would miss it. The CFTC said the No trade earned him about $14,390.

Levine’s useful distinction is that knowing your own plans is not automatically insider trading. But using public statements to move a market while also influencing the event can look like manipulation.

There are complicated prediction-market cases. Then there is betting on whether you will show up, talking about showing up, betting the other way, and not showing up.

A Prediction Market You Can Personally Influence

Santos had direct information about and control over the event: his own attendance.

The CFTC focused on alleged public statements and trading around price moves.

The stronger theory is manipulation rather than ordinary insider trading because the trader could influence both price and outcome.

THE MONEY IDEA
Information advantage and manipulation are different legal ideas.

Santos, Kalshi and Event-Contract Manipulation

Knowledge of your own future conduct does not automatically create the confidentiality duty associated with classical insider trading.

The CFTC instead framed the conduct as manipulation involving public statements and trading.

The episode shows prediction-market reflexivity: a participant can both forecast and help determine the event that settles the contract.

THE MONEY IDEA
Event contracts can become reflexive when traders help determine the outcome.
STORY 4
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: FBI crypto theft

The FBI Agent and the Crypto Wallet

An FBI agent was working on investigations involving cryptocurrency.

Authorities later accused him of serious misconduct involving about $1 million in crypto.

The story is a reminder that digital money needs careful custody rules too.

THE MONEY IDEA
People trusted with valuable property need strong rules.

The Crypto Evidence Wallet Problem

FBI agent Patrick Steven Yaroch worked on investigations involving cryptocurrency held by foreign targets, including crypto tied to investigations of North Korean hackers.

According to an FBI affidavit described by Levine, authorities later accused Yaroch of misusing access to about $1 million in crypto and said he acknowledged making very poor decisions involving cryptocurrency wallets.

Crypto has an unusually direct custody problem: controlling the right private key can mean controlling the asset. Institutions therefore need strict separation of duties, logs and approval controls.

Authorities also said Yaroch had asked ChatGPT how to invest $1 million with a goal of retiring around 40 and someday living a slower agricultural life. Levine had already joked that the story sounded like a thriller plot. Then the retirement-search detail made it even more on-the-nose.

The Evidence Wallet Problem

Authorities alleged serious misconduct by FBI agent Patrick Steven Yaroch involving roughly $1 million of cryptocurrency from investigative targets.

With bearer-like digital assets, access to a private key can be economically equivalent to possession, making multi-party approval, hardware segregation, audit logs and reconciliation especially important.

The reported ChatGPT query about investing $1 million for early retirement becomes Levine’s final thriller-plot punch line.

THE MONEY IDEA
Private-key control can turn investigative access into direct asset access.

Crypto Custody Turns Investigator Access Into a Control Risk

Wallet credentials can confer direct transferable control over assets, not merely access to records about them.

That compresses traditional control layers and makes key segregation, multi-signature authorization, immutable logging and independent reconciliation critical.

Levine’s framing is organizational: the investigator’s access itself becomes a treasury-control risk.

THE MONEY IDEA
Crypto custody collapses the distance between evidence access and transferable value.
STORY 5
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Dentists!

The Dentist With the Extra-Spicy ETF

Leveraged ETFs make daily stock moves extra big.

They were mostly designed for short-term trading, but some people hold them for years.

A former dentist named Song Wonjun says they helped him retire at 44, and now he teaches other people about them.

THE MONEY IDEA
A short-term tool can behave differently when held for years.

Your Dentist Has a Leveraged-ETF Strategy

A 2x leveraged ETF tries to deliver about twice a stock’s return each day, then resets and does it again tomorrow. That means 2x every day is not the same as twice the stock’s return over ten years.

Big back-and-forth moves can hurt, while a strong persistent trend can make the leverage look wonderful. Bloomberg reported that more investors in the United States and South Korea are nevertheless using leveraged ETFs for long-term investing.

One is Song Wonjun, a former South Korean dentist who says leveraged ETFs helped him retire at 44. He now teaches the strategy online.

Levine’s reaction is not exactly an endorsement: if your financial adviser is promoting long-term leveraged ETFs online, that seems questionable. If your dentist is doing it? Somehow that seems exactly right.

Leveraged ETFs Become Long-Term Investments

Daily-reset leveraged ETFs rebalance to maintain a target multiple of daily returns, so long-run results can diverge sharply from a simple multiple of the underlying return.

Bloomberg reported growing long-term use of these products despite their short-horizon design.

Former dentist Song Wonjun says the strategy helped him retire at 44; Levine turns the unlikely adviser into the punch line.

THE MONEY IDEA
Daily compounding makes long-run leveraged-ETF returns path-dependent.

Path Dependence Meets the Leveraged-ETF Buy-and-Hold Crowd

A leveraged ETF targets a multiple of daily, not terminal, returns. Choppy volatility can erode value while sustained trends can produce unexpectedly strong compounded results.

That makes buy-and-hold use different from the short-term trading use for which the products were designed.

Song Wonjun, a former dentist who says leveraged ETFs helped him retire at 44, becomes Levine’s perfect unlikely evangelist for the strategy.

THE MONEY IDEA
Long-term use of daily-reset leverage makes volatility drag, trend effects and rebalancing central.
