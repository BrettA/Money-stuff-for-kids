The Two-Times Fund and the Big, Grouchy Drop

There once was a fund that wanted to try, to double the market with a wink of an eye.

You give it $100, and it borrows some more, another $100, then it opens the door.

It buys $200 of SK Hynix stock on the floor, and every small movement becomes twice as much score.

If the shares climb five percent in one play, that $200 goes up to $210 that day,

and the owners cheer softly, their $100 now worth ten, the fund borrows $10 more to stay twice as then.

But if the sky suddenly darkens and drops, if the ticker goes tumbling and the market just stops,

a fall more than fifty percent in a flash — that’s the danger, the gash — could wipe the $100 in a dash.

Banks which lent the $100 would then be stuck short; Lucid fell 57% once and showed them the port.

So banks went to buy what they called crash insurance, a cliquet of puts that hedge a big worrisome clearance.

Those are daily puts struck fifty percent down from each close, a chain of small guards against overnight blows.

Hedge funds would sell these, but worry they might fail if the crash came and they couldn’t cover the tail.

So the bank offered a note, pre-funded and neat: the fund gives cash up front so the bank’s books stay complete.

To make the yield juicy the hedge fund would borrow some more: pay $20, take a loan of $80, and then hope for the score.

That makes a small $3 premium feel large, a neat 15% on $20 instead of 3% on $100 charged.

Goldman Sachs wrote notes saying, ‘Expensive Crash Cliquet!’ pitch: 14.2% to 20% yields for SK Hynix and Samsung’s twitch.

Janus Henderson’s Natasha Sibley said they built ETFs — JELH and JELM — to package these trades for wider sets.

An ETF that sells crash puts against the gap risk of leveraged single-stock ETFs, wonderful.

What happened? A 2x single-stock leveraged ETF doubles equity by borrowing; if the underlying stock falls more than 50% intraday the ETF’s equity can be wiped out and leave banks with gap risk, so banks buy cliquet crash puts (daily 50%-strike) often pre-funded via structured notes that hedge funds lever, and Goldman and Janus Henderson marketed such trades (Goldman pitched 14.2%–20% yields; Janus Henderson launched JELH and JELM).

IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Tax-aware long-short

The Losses That Counted

Cliff Asness and a clever team at AQR would whisper to the market, not loud but very clear,

We’ll pick pairs of stocks, both near and dear, long on some and short on others so the math will steer.

They borrowed like a magician: borrow one hundred, buy two hundred, then short a hundred to stay even,

Net exposure still one hundred, but the losses they could find were shockingly uneven.

If one long stock sank while its paired short rose, sell the loser, keep the winner in sight—

That makes a realized loss you can use to erase a tax bill that would otherwise bite.

Bloomberg’s reporters—Loukia Gyftopoulou, Katherine Burton, Sridhar Natarajan and Justina Lee—wrote the news down plain:

AQR and rivals run more than $150 billion in this style, and in the whole tax-alpha world there’s over $1 trillion in the game.

AQR’s pot swelled fast; by the end of March their hedge-fund assets topped $140 billion, a striking climb,

And by the end of 2025 they’d become the world’s largest hedge fund, a headline told in numbers and time.

They even put a slide in a pitch: give us $100 million, watch it triple over ten years as their calculations hum,

And along the way the strategy might generate more than $580 million of losses you can use to erase taxes from other sums.

That boast—almost six times your starting cash turned into deductible losses—sounds crazy and absurdly grand,

But in a world where quants treat many stocks as swappable and rules like wash-sale and correlation are carefully scanned, it’s planned.

What happened? Invested money was put into tax-aware long-short trades that used leverage and offsetting long and short positions to realize large deductible losses that can offset taxes elsewhere; AQR and competitors deployed more than $150 billion in such strategies, the broader tax-alpha universe exceeds $1 trillion, and AQR’s hedge-fund assets surpassed $140 billion by end of March 2025 while a presentation claimed $100 million could triple in ten years and produce over $580 million of deductible losses.

IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Commodity market manipulation

George, the Posts, and the Very Strange Bet

George said he’d wear a tie that glittered bright,

and on X he asked, “What color fits me right?”

A market on Kalshi listened and leaned one way —

people bought “Yes” and the price crept up that day.

George sold his little winning pile, pockets a bit fatter,

then filmed a promise, “I’m going,” which lifted the matter.

Later he began to buy the opposite, he chose “No,”

and on the morning of the speech he said, “I cannot go.”

He showed cancelled plane and train, no new tickets in sight,

the “Yes” price fell, the “No” price rose — that turned his trade to light.

In the early hours of February 25 he closed that No position,

and his ledger glowed with a profit: $14,390.57 was his addition.

Regulators from the CFTC said his posts were meant to swing the swap,

they cited Section 6(c)(1) and Regulation 180.1(a) — the wrongdoing wouldn’t stop.

He agreed to disgorge $17,569.98 and pay a $17,500 fine,

and the headlines argued over the word “insider,” while the agency called manipulation by design.

The whole thing looked a bit absurd: a wardrobe tweet, a stuck-at-airport line,

announcing you’ll be there to lift “Yes,” then betting against yourself and skipping the sign.

What happened? Santos used public social-media posts to influence the prices of Kalshi event contracts about his attendance, traded Yes and No positions, and exited with a $14,390.57 profit; the CFTC charged market manipulation (Section 6(c)(1) and Regulation 180.1(a)) and he agreed to disgorge $17,569.98 and pay a $17,500 fine.

IN MATT'S ARTICLE: FBI crypto theft

Patrick and the Keys

Patrick Steven Yaroch was a man with a badge and a plan,

He sat at his desk, a keyboard under his hand.

He worked for the FBI, that much is true,

But then he did things he probably shouldn’t do.

He found the secret keys on a system he could reach,

Keys that unlocked accounts beyond the FBI’s beach.

Those accounts were linked to an “adversarial nation,” the papers say,

And Patrick moved coins from those wallets away.

Ten little transfers, each one a small shove,

Added up to around one million dollars — that’s what the court wrote of.

He started in late 2024 or maybe early 2025,

Using agency systems to move funds and keep his plans alive.

Then in May he typed to ChatGPT, curious and bold,

“How do I invest a million so I can retire at forty and buy land with olive trees and gold?”

He dreamed of Cilento hills or Portugal’s Dao green vine,

A slower life, a vineyard, a sunlit row of wine.

But the twist here is bitter, and a little absurd,

Stealing crypto from people his team was watching — who would have heard?

After some time, Patrick called and told the Justice crew,

He turned himself in and confessed the things he’d done and knew.

The laws will sort the rest; the courts will set the scene,

While the record keeps the numbers and the words that were seen.

What happened? Patrick Steven Yaroch used FBI systems to access private cryptocurrency keys and made about ten transfers totaling around $1,000,000 from accounts tied to an “adversarial nation.” He contacted the Department of Justice to confess and an FBI affidavit was filed in a Virginia district court.

Song the Dentist and the Leverage That Stayed

Song was a dentist with steady hands and steady cheer,

but he liked charts on his screen and numbers that were clear.

He found a thing called leveraged ETFs that try to grow more fast,

they chase a multiple each day — but they weren't meant to last.

He held them through the months and years instead of for a day,

and watched his savings swell until he could sail away.

At forty-four he closed his clinic, hung up the dental light,

and told the world his secret plan that helped him sleep at night.

He posts his lessons to a crowd — hundreds of thousands stop by,

they see his smiling videos and think, maybe I can try.

Folks say it sounds suspicious when an adviser shouts online,

but if a dentist did the very same, for some it feels just fine.

The odd, small joke lives in plain sight — it makes the story bright:

"If your financial adviser is going around online touting long-term investments in leveraged ETFs, that seems bad. If your dentist is doing it, yeah, that seems right."

People hear the numbers, see the charts, and wonder what to do,

some will copy Song, some will wait, some will choose something new.

His story spans from the U.S. to Korea — a growing, curious trend —

a dentist who turned a day-trade tool into a long-term friend.

What happened? Leveraged exchange-traded funds amplify daily returns and are designed for short-term/day trading. Song Wonjun, a former dentist in South Korea, used them as the centerpiece of a long-term investing strategy, retired at 44, and now teaches hundreds of thousands of online followers.
