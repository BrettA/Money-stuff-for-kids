STORY 1
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Private beta

The Chips That Made Jon Gray Say “What?”

In 2023, Jas Khaira brought Jon Gray a plan: “Lend billions to CoreWeave on the fast chips that it ran.” CoreWeave once mined crypto; now its GPUs were crunching the data that AI models use.

Microsoft had a contract to take computing capacity, helping support the loan with expected cash reliably. The GPUs themselves would help back the amount— “asset-backed financing,” in a private account.

“Ex-crypto? Billions? A loan backed by chips?” Gray answered, “What?” at this strangest of pitches. Yet what sounded freewheeling became, in his view, a great opportunity—and the financing went through.

Soon Blackstone’s data-center empire reached $200 billion, with power and cooling needed by buildings by the million. At Blackstone’s enormous scale, AI became “the single most important thing” in the game.

By 2026, the odd bet from three years before was “like 4% of the economy”—and growing still more. What once looked like clever, unusual “alpha” to buy had become ordinary “beta”: broad growth powered by AI.

But much of that growth sat in private funds behind gates, not cheap public index funds with tiny fee rates. The economy’s biggest boom was privately packed— broad market exposure, with higher fees attached.

What happened? Blackstone made a multibillion-dollar loan to CoreWeave backed by GPUs and supported by a Microsoft contract, helping establish asset-backed financing for AI chips. As AI financing expanded through private credit and infrastructure funds, ordinary economic exposure increasingly came through private vehicles charging more than low-cost public index funds.

STORY 2
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Private credit marks

The Loan Price That Wouldn’t Sit Still

One Wednesday in October, no borrower brought news; No payment was late, and no loan changed its views.

No private loan traded; no buyer appeared. So yesterday’s $100 still looked safely moored.

But public AI loans had sunk 2% that day. Should similar private loans stay put anyway?

Apollo said daily marks could offer a guide— “Marks” are fresh estimates of value supplied. Across its roughly $850 billion credit business, Apollo began giving daily prices more fitness.

At the CAIS summit, Ted Koenig of Monroe Said daily precision could put on a show. Without fresh statements or borrower reports, “An illusion of precision” was his retort.

Drew McKnight of Fortress agreed with that view: An index can move, but it won’t fully tell you If one private loan should be $100 or $98— You must study that borrower to value it straight.

Yet here was the puzzle behind all the fuss: If private loans track public ones, marking’s no muss. But if an index explains every dip, rise, and fee, How special can pricey private management be?

What happened? Apollo Global Management began rolling out daily valuations across its roughly $850 billion credit business, using market information such as public-loan prices to help estimate private-loan values. Ted Koenig and Drew McKnight argued that without daily borrower data, those marks may be precise-looking estimates rather than accurate prices.

STORY 3
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Prime brokerage

When Goldman’s Trading Desk Lent More Than It Traded

At Goldman Sachs, the equities crew once mainly matched stock trades passing through. A seller brought shares, and a buyer would pay; the bank joined the two and kept part of the fee on the way.

Now buyers and sellers can meet on a screen, while hedge funds and trading firms crowd in between. And since 2008, banks watch risk with more care, so they often lend money secured by a share.

If borrowers cannot repay what they owe, the pledged stocks help cover the loan’s heavy blow. That lending is part of prime brokerage, too: financing and custody, plus matchmaking, accrue.

Hedge funds use leverage—borrowed cash for a bet— which can magnify gains and make losses grow yet. With markets quite jumpy, especially in tech, more funds sought financing and wrote Goldman a check.

Bloomberg’s Todd Gillespie spotted the turn: Goldman’s financing side had more revenue to earn. It brought in $3.37 billion that quarter, up 96%; trade-matching made $3.08 billion, up 52%.

So Goldman’s stock-trading division, surprise, made more helping hedge funds borrow to buy. Its business called “trading” had reached the odd stage where financing stocks took the center of the page.

What happened? In the third quarter, Goldman Sachs earned more equities revenue from financing hedge funds through prime brokerage than from matching stock buyers and sellers—the first quarter on record when that happened.

STORY 4
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Agents

The Watchdog Hires an Agent

The Financial Times reported a curious plan: Britain’s finance watchdog may give AI more work once done by human hands.

At the Financial Conduct Authority, Jessica Rusu looked ahead: “We want a smarter regulator,” she said.

Agentic AI can gather clues and pursue a whole case, Taking linked steps on its own at a very rapid pace.

Authorizations decide which firms may begin; Supervision keeps watch, and enforcement steps in.

Officials would oversee it—but Rusu’s larger goal Is to automate where they can and intervene at scale.

Suppose an email arrives: “Insider trading, we think.” You answer; AI asks more questions before you can blink.

It weighs your defense, declares a large fine, Then sends you the invoice—with no human in line.

“Can I talk to a person?” No person appears. That sort of regulator sounds efficient—and eerie.

But when trading agents trade, the strange plan may fit: Two AIs could settle the case in twenty seconds, lickety-split.

“I used ten billion signals,” the trading agent might say. “They’re too subtle for humans—please step out of the way.”

Rules and loopholes take people long years to prepare; Agents might invent them in minutes, then battle right there.

Someday an agentic firm might be fined $1 billion For something too subtly bad to explain to a human civilian.

And how could a columnist describe what the agents had done? Perhaps an agent must write it—then explain it to everyone.

What happened? The Financial Times reported that Britain’s Financial Conduct Authority plans to use agentic AI for intelligence gathering, authorizations, supervision, and enforcement, with officials overseeing it while the agency seeks to automate more and intervene at scale. The twenty-second AI dispute and $1 billion fine were imagined examples of where agentic finance and regulation could lead.
