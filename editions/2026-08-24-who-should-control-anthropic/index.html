The Extra-Big Votes at Anthropic

In many a company, shareholders can say, “Replace the board if it’s blundering today.” But founders may argue, “Our mission comes first; Outside owners could trade it for profit—or worse.”

AI raised a third choice: let wise people decide, With no money invested and humanity as guide. Candidates included AI theorists, effective altruists, and Larry Summers—perhaps. The difficulty is finding such sages, and avoiding mishaps.

OpenAI tested that model in 2023; Its nonprofit board fired Sam Altman—briefly. He was gone for about two days, then returned to the job, For lofty control met investors, employees, and an uproarious mob.

When investors supply tens of billions for a trillion-dollar prize, Philosophers holding the final vote may not survive. SpaceX chose founder control as the safer bet: Trust Elon Musk with value and responsibility—though that theory has problems yet.

Now Anthropic is preparing a two-layered plan, The Information reported how voting would span. CEO Dario Amodei and other co-founders may receive Special stock with extra votes, so outsiders cannot easily intervene.

That matters because the founders’ ownership stakes are relatively small; Extra voting power can outweigh the money they own after all. Yet Anthropic also has a Long-Term Benefit Trust, Made in 2023 to guard long-term aims, as it must.

Its non-employee, non-investor advisers hold Class T shares, With no economic value—no company profits are theirs. But those shares can elect a majority of the seven-person board, A narrow power, yet quite an extraordinary sword.

The trust usually has five members, but now it has three; Ben Bernanke is one of the trustees who oversee. Mariano-Florentino Cuéllar left the trust this month for another role: Anthropic chief global affairs officer—with an executive’s payroll.

Being a philosopher-overlord for humanity sounds weighty and cool; Being an Anthropic executive may pay better as a rule. So founders get most control, independent trustees get some, And outside shareholders get no control—the governance-and-marketing job is done.

What happened? Anthropic was preparing extra-voting stock for Dario Amodei and other co-founders while retaining Class T shares that let its Long-Term Benefit Trust elect a majority of the seven-person board despite having no economic value. The trust, created in 2023, had fallen from its usual five members to three after Mariano-Florentino Cuéllar became an Anthropic executive; its trustees include Ben Bernanke.

The Fund That Chased Its Own Price

A 2x ETF on Strategy Inc. might start with $100 in hand, Borrow $100, then buy $200 of stock as planned. If Strategy rises 3%, those shares become $206; The investors have $106—up 6% from the mix.

But 1.94x leverage is now too small for the next day, So the fund needs $212 of stock to keep 2x in play. It buys another $6, often right at the close; When Strategy falls, it sells instead as leverage grows.

At 3 p.m., a hedge fund may foresee a $100 million buy, Purchase $80 million early, then sell when closing time is nigh. But buying lifts the price, so the ETF may need $120 million more; “Then buy another $40 million!”—and round the loop can roar.

This can supply shares smoothly, as index arbitrage might do, And swaps or other derivatives can create the rebalancing too. But traders may also push prices, then sell into the demand— The fund must buy more because those traders bought beforehand.

Yinhong Zhao of Princeton studied Korea’s 2026 spree: Single-stock LETFs launched with KRW 4.3 trillion, then reached KRW 14 trillion in three weeks’ activity. For SK Hynix, mandated rebalancing averaged 22.4% of daily trading, And peaked at 50.4%, with the feedback escalating.

The paper estimated annualized volatility rose from 84.8% to 136.7% in nine weeks, While retail holders lost 19% of terminal wealth through the loop it critiques. About 60% of tracked stocks’ first response to pre-open U.S. news reversed by the next close; The measured loop was over twice MicroStrategy’s and ten times U.S. index complexes’ dose.

Meanwhile, Direxion Daily Semiconductor Bull 3X Shares drew almost $7 billion in net inflows During July and the first two weeks of August, Morningstar’s information shows. Ben Snider of Goldman Sachs called it “buying the dip and selling the rip”— Though leveraged ETFs may help create both ends of that trip.

What happened? Leveraged ETFs had to rebalance at each close by buying after gains and selling after losses. Traders anticipated those orders, moved prices first, enlarged the required rebalancing, and sold into it—so the funds could help create the dips and rips investors hoped to reverse.

IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Circumstantial insider trading

The Courtside Clue and the $53 Million Trade

At Madison Square Garden, beneath the arena light, Gavin Wolfe and Jason Satsky watched Duke basketball one night. The game was nationally televised; for about three hours they sat, and soon the SEC would wonder about that.

These were seasoned bankers, not beginners learning the trade: Satsky was Bank of America’s global energy-and-utility banking head when the deal was made. Wolfe, once a BofA managing director, had retired to manage businesses and investments alone, including a portfolio worth about $260 million of his own.

Within minutes after the November 9 game ended, a curious note appeared: at 12:12 a.m., Wolfe entered “SJi and njr,” two NYSE tickers, as the calendar cleared. He scheduled that entry for 9:15 a.m. on November 10, 2021, then started buying South Jersey Industries shares when morning had begun.

Wolfe bought $53 million of SJI stock—not short-lived call options hoping prices would climb. Stock meant actual company shares, purchased in unusually large size this time. When the South Jersey Industries merger was announced, their value took a leap, and Wolfe’s profit reached $18.5 million—a very sizable heap.

The SEC alleged that Satsky had passed along important merger news not known outside. Yet it quoted no pre-purchase messages and found no direct proof that Satsky supplied it. The basketball meeting, calendar note, and buying formed the circumstantial case; the evidence pointed around a possible tip without recording one someplace.

Bloomberg’s Nicola White and Ava Benny-Morrison reported the lawyers’ reply: Bob Anello said Satsky shared no secret information and denied the charge outright. Reed Brodsky said the SEC could not identify what was disclosed or how it was shown; he said immunized testimony and documents backed an investment thesis of Wolfe’s own.

Buying a merger target after sitting with its banker certainly looked suspicious in timing, but without proof of a tip, the conclusion was less than binding. Perhaps it was innocent coincidence, with Wolfe’s research guiding the investing; or perhaps, as the joke went, it was pretty good insider trading.

What happened? The SEC accused Gavin Wolfe of earning $18.5 million by buying $53 million of South Jersey Industries stock after Jason Satsky allegedly tipped him about a merger. The evidence was circumstantial, and both men denied that any tip occurred.

The Summer the Intern Taught the Bankers

In banking, the juniors once learned at their chairs, Building models and slides through long hours of repairs. The seniors found clients and brought business through, While beginners gained skills from the basic work they’d do.

Then AI agents arrived—computer tools trained To finish assigned jobs when their steps were explained. Some worried these agents would take the first rung, Leaving no path to senior from eager and young.

For banking has long used an apprentice design: Juniors practice and grow, gaining judgment with time. If AI does the groundwork with tireless flair, Where will tomorrow’s senior rainmakers prepare?

But picture those rainmakers, busy all day, Winning clients and bringing new business their way. Will they pause to learn agents and automate chores? Come on—they need juniors who know what AI is for.

At one London lender, the switch became clear: A private banking intern turned teacher this year. They built brand-new agents to streamline to-dos, Then showed senior bankers which features to use.

At drop-in help sessions, the bankers could learn, Ask questions, try tools, and come back for a turn. The intern compared it—with humor full-grown— To teaching your parents to work a new phone.

Nil Codina Martinez and Zainab Haji Reported for Bloomberg on this role-reversing spree. Bloomberg News questioned nearly a dozen that summer;\nEven non-tech programs had changed for each newcomer.

The joke has a wobble: If interns succeed At automating everything bankers might need, And teach every senior each shortcut and trick, Next year’s intern openings could vanish quite quick.

Yet AI keeps racing; its tools won’t stand still. Next summer may bring some brand-new task or skill. So the next group of interns may walk through the door With all-new automation to teach them once more.

What happened? A private banking intern at a London lender built AI agents to streamline tasks and taught senior bankers to use them; Bloomberg News, through reporting by Nil Codina Martinez and Zainab Haji, found similar changes among nearly a dozen summer analysts and interns. The absurd possibility is that interns may automate away future internships, except AI changes fast enough that next year’s interns may have new automation to teach.

The Résumé with Olive Oil on the Side

A résumé shows employers the work you have done, the skills you have mastered, the goals you have won. But the Wall Street Journal reported a curious new turn: a hobby may help you get noticed in return.

In a fierce white-collar market, with AI causing concern, recruiters seek human details no machine could discern. A fondness for canasta, or vegetables pickled just right, might wake a tired reader and earn a second sight.

A quirky interest cannot replace knowing how to do the job, but it may lift one application from the look-alike mob. It can break interview ice and open conversation, revealing a person beyond each qualification.

Legal recruiter Kate Reder Sheikh had noticed the shift: more applicants used hobbies to give their résumés a lift. Last year, more than half of the associates she placed listed hobbies. That was a jump from a few years earlier.

Fred Cibelli, a technology principal at EY in New York, still required every candidate to qualify for the work. But once they did, he’d gladly hear why olive oil brought delight; that odd little passion could make an interview bright.

“There’s only so many times you can talk” AI once more— open models versus closed models can become quite a chore. Cibelli had seen a small increase, and hoped to see more, in hobbies on LinkedIn profiles and résumés crossing his door.

Then came the funniest interest, reported from time to time: friends and former colleagues spot “Money Stuff” on the line. The writer wants to encourage it; those readers probably get hired. But “this is not career advice”—even if the result is desired.

What happened? Recruiters said that, after candidates met the real job requirements, unusual interests could prompt a résumé reread and become interview icebreakers.
