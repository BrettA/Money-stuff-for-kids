IN MATT'S ARTICLE: TWG

The Long, Long LeBron Loan

In 2018, the Lakers signed LeBron James, for $154 million—and championship aims.

But months before basketball brought him that score, his LLC borrowed nearly $300 million more. Two Midwestern life insurers supplied it right away; a Guggenheim Partners arm helped arrange the payday.

The bonds would be paid through 2049, from future off-court earnings flowing over time. That included sponsorship money from Nike Inc.— cash not earned yet, pledged link after link.

The bonds were unusual and hard to resell, but life insurers could hold them for quite a long spell. They promise payments years ahead, so patient investments can match what they’ll owe instead.

Then came a puzzle both funny and strange: retirees need returns, not a handshake exchange. Yet the person selecting this cool, custom debt could grin, “LeBron owes us!”—an unusual get.

Mark Walter led Guggenheim; his TWG Global owned Delaware Life and Clear Spring, plus sports stakes widely known. Officials later examined over $20 billion in affiliated loans, and Walter agreed to sell the Lakers among his trophy-team holdings.

There is no indication the LeBron bonds were tied to that inquiry. Still, the question remained: Were insurers choosing the best strange loans—or sometimes the coolest ones?

What happened? LeBron James’s LLC borrowed nearly $300 million through bonds backed by future non-basketball income, including Nike sponsorship revenue. Life insurers funded the long-term deal, while separate investigations examined undisclosed affiliated lending involving Mark Walter’s insurers.

IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Situational Awareness

The Blowup That Didn’t Blow Up

Leopold Aschenbrenner made one mandate clear: Situational Awareness would buy AI while the boom was in gear.

The fund used bank loans called leverage to buy; that boosts gains—and losses—as prices move high.

It became a cornerstone in SK Hynix’s U.S. sale, while climbing AI shares put wind in its sail. Those mark-to-market profits—the higher values displayed— helped it borrow and add to the bets it had made.

At Archegos Capital Management, Bill Hwang had done more: as stocks rose, he borrowed, then borrowed once more. When prices sank, banks raced to sell in a rout; Archegos hit zero, and billions were wiped out.

But this time, the banks kept strong cushions in place: dynamic margin lowered loan-to-value as prices raced. They also had rights to investments the fund held, including its Anthropic stake, if the borrowing rebelled.

Then AI stocks fell; margin calls knocked at the door— demands to add cash or collateral once more. The fund paid awhile, then chose an orderly track: it sold most public stocks to Citadel in one discounted pack.

Citadel unwound most positions, apparently with gain; the fund stayed in business, and no lender felt pain. Situational Awareness still seemed up for the year— a fearsome “blowup” whose worst parts did not appear.

The SEC later subpoenaed banks for records about trade timing, leverage, and communications. The inquiry was at an early stage, and Situational Awareness had not been accused of wrongdoing.

What happened? Situational Awareness borrowed to buy concentrated AI positions, but dynamic margining and collateral kept its lenders protected as prices fell. An orderly discounted block sale to Citadel prevented the frantic liquidation that had made Archegos collapse so destructive.

IN MATT'S ARTICLE: AI margin loans

The 135-Day Rule Meets the AI Boom

At JPMorgan Chase, the usual way was to shun brand-new shares for 135 days. Those shares were collateral—stock the bank could take, then sell if a borrower’s payments should break.

New stocks can plunge, and lockups can bind; when selling gets hard, buyers are tricky to find. But SpaceX was different. Before its June debut, the bank told its bankers they could lend sooner, too.

SpaceX, Elon Musk’s rocket-and-AI enterprise, was helping create fortunes of astonishing size. JPMorgan made $75 million in its listing role; winning those wealthy clients was part of the goal.

For Anthropic, the company that makes Claude, bankers guessed they might bend the same rule—though nothing was set. With trillions in AI wealth glittering bright, a bank had to risk something to join in the fight.

SoftBank supplied an even stranger surprise: it borrowed against OpenAI to buy more OpenAI. If giant AI companies crashed through the floor, losses on these margin loans might be the least trouble in store.

What happened? JPMorgan relaxed its normal 135-day waiting policy so it could lend sooner against newly public SpaceX shares, and bankers expected it might consider doing the same for Anthropic. These margin loans let people borrow money by pledging shares that the bank can seize and sell if the loans are not repaid.
