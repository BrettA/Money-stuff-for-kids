IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Bitcoin oopsie

The Bitcoin That Wouldn’t Come Out

In 2025, Wall Street loved a strange new trick: Buy Bitcoin, watch your company’s value rise up quick. A hundred million dollars in coins might make twice that appear; Everyone knew it couldn’t last, so deals came fast that year.

Datavault AI was worth about $35 million one day, with 116.7 million shares near thirty cents in play. It sold Scilex 278.9 million shares in a trade, at $0.5378 each—and Bitcoin was how Scilex paid.

About 1,309 Bitcoin, then worth near $150 million, appeared in Datavault’s Biconomy account, and shareholders cheered. CEO Nathaniel Bradley praised biotech, blockchain, and AI; the stock hit $3.118—a $973 million high.

But these were off-chain credits: Biconomy’s private record showed the sum, not Bitcoin on the public blockchain where coins can go and come. Datavault sold 491 back to Scilex for $40.5 million in cash, then tried withdrawing the rest—and ran into a crash.

The withdrawal would not complete; no on-chain coins came through. Accountants said the old financial statements were no longer true. The Bitcoin did not qualify as Datavault’s asset after all, and its stock closed down 50% after the news’s fall.

The market once paid $2 in stock for $1 of Bitcoin in sight. Now it seemed to have paid $2 for $0. Oops—not quite right!

What happened? Scilex credited about 1,309 Bitcoin to Datavault AI on Biconomy’s private, off-chain ledger, but Datavault could not withdraw the remaining coins to an external blockchain wallet. Its accountants concluded that the Bitcoin had never qualified as Datavault’s digital asset.

IN MATT'S ARTICLE: People are worried about non-GAAP accounting

The Good News That Looked Like a Loss

As Anthropic PBC prepared for an IPO day, its first-half numbers made eyebrows sway. GAAP showed an $80.7 billion loss in red, while adjusted net income was $2.7 billion ahead.

Anthropic had sold Google and Amazon unusual deals: convertible notes, preferred stock, and agreements with seals. They worked much like shares, but GAAP gave them a name— “derivative liabilities,” repriced whenever values changed.

Anthropic’s value rose by about $80 billion—good news! The investors gained on paper from the stock they could use. But “mark to market” measured those contracts anew, so Anthropic booked a matching paper loss coming through.

No mountain of cash had gone rolling away; it was an accounting result of the higher price that day. About $72 billion in liability losses left adjusted profit’s sight, and once the deals become ordinary stock, that repricing will stop outright.

Jonathan Weil noted an odd double view: Amazon and Alphabet counted their paper gains as earnings coming through. If Anthropic removes the corresponding loss from its adjusted display, should analysts remove the investors’ gains in the very same way?

Warren Buffett had warned of distortion before, when 2016 rules counted rising stocks that Berkshire Hathaway bore. Still, an owner’s gain need not be an issuing company’s loss: stock can create value—it needn’t leave someone else paying the cost.

What happened? Anthropic’s stock-like financing contracts rose in value and were treated under US GAAP as mark-to-market liabilities, producing an $80.7 billion first-half net loss that was mostly non-cash. Analysts excluded much of that loss to calculate $2.7 billion of adjusted net income, while Amazon and Alphabet included related paper gains in their own earnings measures.

IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Buy borrow die

Borrow Like a Billionaire

Suppose a stock portfolio, worth $100 million, held gains that had quietly grown by the million. Sell shares for more than their purchase-day price, and capital-gains tax may claim its own slice.

Instead, pledge those shares as collateral—a tie that secures a bank loan while the stock stays nearby. Borrow $50 million at six percent due; a loan isn't income, so no gains tax comes through.

The interest is $3 million year after year; with eight-percent growth, $8 million may appear. The interest can simply be added to debt— if markets keep rising as averages suggest.

When the owner dies, the heirs’ tax basis resets to the shares’ current value, with earlier gains swept. They sell enough stock to repay what was owed; the rest can stay theirs after settling the load.

Deloitte estimates such lending at $5.4 trillion, against stocks, bonds, funds, and crypto by the billion. Deloitte’s Eric Gutzwiller said it once served the rich; now affluent and retail customers are making the switch.

At Monument Bank, Xavier De Pauw planned to lend to investors with a few hundred thousand to spend. They could “adopt a billionaire mindset,” he said— a billionaire tax strategy, now widely spread.

What happened? Banks and brokerages increasingly lend people money secured by their investment portfolios, letting them spend without selling appreciated shares. If the borrower dies holding the shares, heirs receive a stepped-up tax basis and can sell enough to repay the loan without owing capital-gains tax on the pre-death increase.

IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Sports bets

Kalshi, Utah, and the Fifty Rulebooks

Tarek Mansour of Kalshi said, “Our market’s not the same: No sportsbook house takes the other side of every game.” On Kalshi, traders meet as peers—one “yes,” one “no”; Their prices join a nationwide pool, then final scores will show.

A bar can bet against its team to hedge its beer-sales risk: If cheering crowds don’t come, the contract softens business’s dip. Mansour said sportsbooks aren’t used that way—but there’s a hitch: Mattress Mack has hedged there for years, so that claim needs a switch.

Mansour wanted federal rules, not fifty state commands; He pictured New York Stock Exchange rules changing across the lands. A national pool, he argued, keeps trading deep and wide, While state-by-state restrictions split the traders up by side.

But Utah sued Kalshi, saying gambling isn’t welcome there; Its constitution has banned it for 130 years with care. And in 2018, the Supreme Court said each state may choose The sports-betting rules it wants to keep, permit, or refuse.

So Kalshi and the CFTC arrived at something new: Old state bans might yield to federal commodities law’s view. The strange result: a vital federal interest, it would seem, Is liquid nationwide sports-event trading for an eighteen-year-old’s team.

What happened? Kalshi treated sports wagers as federally regulated peer-to-peer event contracts, while Utah and other states sued under their own gambling laws. The dispute asks whether federal commodities law overrides state sports-gambling rules, despite the Supreme Court’s 2018 recognition of state authority.
