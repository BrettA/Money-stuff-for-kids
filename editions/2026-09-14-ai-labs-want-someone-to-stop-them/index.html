STORY 1
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: P(doom)

The Race That Nobody Could Pause

Anthropic may go public; its pitch could declare a market above $30 trillion waiting out there. SpaceX estimated $28.5 trillion; Anthropic’s claim would be finance’s biggest market target by name.

The sunny case: AI does one quarter of work everywhere, and Anthropic captures it all as its share. The stormy case Dario Amodei fears is more grim: rogue AI could end everyone—not merely the firm.

So Dario urged companies to “pace” the frontier race, with shared safety limits to keep danger in its place. He asked for government help or an antitrust waiver, so labs could coordinate without unlawful behavior.

Each lab spends billions to train the newest and best; the latest models earn more than the rest. If one lab pauses, its rivals charge on with the best; they’ll eat up its lunch while its servers take rest.

If all pause together, compute bills may fall, while premium frontier prices last longer for all. But competitors agreeing to limit what they make can look like a cartel—and the law hits the brake.

The strangest snag waits at the conference-room door: the labs say their race could bring danger galore. Yet rules built to stop rivals from rigging the game might bar them from joining to slow the AI train.

What happened? Dario Amodei proposed that frontier AI companies coordinate on safety standards and limits, with government mediation or antitrust waivers. Such coordination could reduce costly model development and preserve high prices, but it might also be treated as an illegal agreement among competitors to restrict output.

STORY 2
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Lockup synchronization

The Buyers Who Came Late

When SpaceX offered stock to all, most shares stayed locked behind a wall. An IPO makes shares public; a lock makes insiders wait before selling their stock.

The shares free to trade are known as “float.” Index weights take that amount into note. When locked shares open for buying and sale, the float grows bigger on the index’s scale.

The first SpaceX lockup ended in August; a second expired one week later, as promised. Some feared that a flood of shares would appear— but insiders mostly held, and no selloff came near.

Yet Nasdaq did not change the weight that same day. Its scheduled adjustment was farther away. Isabelle Lee reported the later forecast: SpaceX’s index share would rise quite fast—

from roughly 1.28% of the Nasdaq 100 mix to about 2.82% when Nasdaq made the fix. Passive funds copy the index just so; with SpaceX weighted higher, their buying must grow.

That could mean billions in purchases—but late! The possible sellers and buyers missed their date. One lockup caused both sides of the affair, yet the market’s two calendars would not pair.

Hedge funds could buy and hold every share, then sell to index funds when they finally got there. But warehousing stock brings risk, cost, and strain. Why not let both crowds meet on the same day’s train?

What happened? SpaceX’s lockup expirations increased the shares available to trade, but the Nasdaq 100 was expected to recognize that larger float only at a later rebalancing, raising SpaceX’s projected weight from about 1.28% to about 2.82%. That delay separated possible insider selling from billions of dollars of expected passive-fund buying.

STORY 3
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Annuities

The Annuities Somebody Read

Mark Walter’s TWG Global had holdings galore: Big sports-team stakes and insurance firms in its store. Delaware Life and Clear Spring Life and Annuity sold contracts designed to provide payment security.

Customers paid money up front for returns that stayed fixed, while insurers chose where those funds would be mixed. More than $20 billion went out as loans to businesses linked to the same owner’s zones.

Those loans should have carried an “affiliated” name, but the insurers’ disclosures did not say the same. After subpoenas arrived, the true labels appeared; prosecutors asked questions, and regulators peered.

TWG Global said, “There is no victim here.” The loans had not failed; payments still did appear. And because private investments aren’t traded each day, no falling market price flashed trouble their way.

Besides, annuity buyers don’t read every sheet— so who would complain if the payments stayed neat? But Rick Phillips read news that made confidence fall: Walter planned to sell his controlling stake in the Los Angeles Lakers after all.

Adviser Carlos Dias Jr. had trusted strong ratings, healthy disclosures, and calm calculations. Then AM Best considered a possible downgrade; the hidden affiliate totals left both men afraid.

So customers may skip many footnotes—quite true. But Rick and Carlos showed that somebody reads a few. No missed payment was needed to make concern start: wrong labels can hide a risk’s most important part.

What happened? Clear Spring Life and Annuity and Delaware Life disclosed more than $20 billion of loans that should have been labeled as transactions with affiliated businesses but were not. The annuities kept paying, yet the corrected disclosures and possible rating downgrade worried customer Rick Phillips and adviser Carlos Dias Jr.

STORY 4
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: “Montessori Ponzi”

The Schools That Had to Keep Opening

Guidepost schools were expensive to run, but opening each new one brought money in. Landlords advanced cash to improve every place, so expanding supplied Higher Ground with financial grace.

That cash became “float”—money held for a spell, though bills and repayments would come due as well. Alex Richardson, a teacher there early, called it a “Montessori Ponzi” quite clearly.

New schools kept opening at remarkable speed: thirty-three in 2020, then twenty-one more indeed. Yet some Guidepost locations, month after month, were losing $50,000—not merely a bunch.

Kevin McDonald advanced $1 million, he said, for a Guidepost in Ellisville to get up and spread. He estimated not even one-third reached that school, and believed other openings had swallowed the pool.

Co-founder Ray Girn firmly denied wrongdoing, calling the model misunderstood, not negligent doing. But losses reached $154.6 million in all, and without further growth, the company took a fall.

Claire Suddath reported one final surprise: some California schools changed after their sale and demise. Their Montessori curriculum was swept away, to be replaced by artificial intelligence—also promising, they’d say.

What happened? Higher Ground often received landlord advances when it opened Guidepost schools, providing fresh cash while existing schools lost money and obligations accumulated. After rapid expansion stopped, the company collapsed into bankruptcy; the quoted claims about diverted funds were allegations disputed by Ray Girn.

STORY 5
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Sudden wealth syndrome

The Fortune That Made Work Optional

Top AI researchers command so much pay, They could bank it, retire, and walk off that day. To match rival offers, tech firms have to spend— Then wonder why workers should work without end.

One joke: thirty million out by the Bay Might buy two whole bedrooms, so don’t move away. Another strange spur: researchers fear AI Could end everyone—yet they still don’t say goodbye.

At Bloomberg Weekend, Tiffany Ap described The strange aftershocks when big exits arrive. That sparked a sly answer to tech’s hiring bind: Make sudden wealth sound like a threat to peace of mind.

Anastasia Koroleva, a centimillionaire, Asked ultra-rich guests what came after the fanfare. Some called a $500 million company sale A thing to “recover from,” dazed by its scale. An exit means selling; the payday can close Your need for a paycheck—but structure can go.

Psychologist Sherry Walling had seen Founders meet every goal, yet feel far from serene. She said Tony Hsieh’s death in 2020 helped people speak more openly about founders’ mental health. Walling co-wrote Exit Strategy, a guide For living when work and identity divide.

So here was the cheeky retention advice: “Don’t risk sudden riches; just work.” Very nice! A fortune could free you from meetings at ten— But wealth might feel awful, so… meetings again!

What happened? Competitive AI salaries can make top researchers wealthy enough to retire immediately, while a large company sale can similarly remove a founder’s need to work. Tiffany Ap reported that some wealthy founders feel lost after an exit, inspiring the joke that tech firms might retain workers by warning them about “sudden wealth syndrome.”
