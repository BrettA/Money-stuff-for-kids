STORY 1
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Binance hack

The Coins That Wouldn’t Come Back—But Did

Hackers apparently linked to North Korea struck Binance one night, Taking customers’ crypto—$6.3 billion in flight. That was about five percent of the exchange’s store, And the biggest crypto hack anyone had seen before.

Much of the loot was USDT and USDC, Stablecoins that each promise one dollar faithfully. Their blockchain transfers cannot simply be undone, But their issuers can freeze them so they’re useful to no one.

Paolo Ardoino said Tether locked more than $1.5 billion tight; Jeremy Allaire said Circle froze $800 million from flight. They blacklisted the thieves’ addresses, stopping the coins in their tracks, Then could mint replacement tokens using the freed dollar backing in their stacks.

Other crypto firms helped freeze about $3 billion in all, But $3.3 billion stayed beyond anyone’s call. Richard Teng said Binance would cover every user’s loss, So customers—not customers’ accounts—wouldn’t bear the cost.

Its SAFU emergency fund held $1.23 billion in bitcoin that day, And Binance used company reserves for the rest it must pay. It also planned to buy $1.3 billion of crypto through markets anew, While frozen funds could repay what Binance had fronted, too.

Satoshi Nakamoto designed Bitcoin so payments would stay, With no central authority reversing them away. Yet Tether, Circle, and Binance behaved much like banks in the end— Freezing stolen funds and making customers whole again.

What happened? Hackers stole about $6.3 billion of cryptocurrency from Binance. Stablecoin issuers froze blacklistable tokens, and Binance promised to replace customers’ remaining losses using SAFU and company funds.

STORY 2
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Binance hack price impact

The Very Expensive Hurry

On October 29, Binance funds were stolen away; apparent North Korean hackers had no time to delay.

Stablecoins such as USDT are designed to hold steady, but issuers can freeze them—so the thieves had to be ready.

Within roughly half an hour, they swapped for Ether at speed, buying wherever they could to fill their urgent need.

On Uniswap they paid $2,657 on average in flight; Hyperliquid showed about $2,300 that night.

They paid roughly fifteen percent for immediate size; with thin liquidity, frantic demand drove up the price.

Quick traders sold Ether dear and could buy cheaper elsewhere; Bitquery says the attackers lost $119 million there.

James Aitchison of Caerus called the price gaps good to trade. Luca Parlamento said D2 took the other side of the raid.

TRM Labs says Tether and Circle froze $1.8 billion in place, about forty minutes after the main Ethereum phase.

Circle’s terms can freeze USDC at a blocked address; that risk, plus haste and size, helped cause the costly mess.

Bloomberg’s Sidhartha Shukla and Emily Nicolle reported the spree—and traders discussed their profitable night quite openly.

That was the very crypto joke, strange enough to confess: in traditional finance, few brag to the press about profiting while apparent North Korean hackers launder stolen assets.

What happened? Hackers rushed to trade freezeable stablecoins stolen from Binance for Ether, paying unusually high prices because they needed speed in thin markets. Traders including D2 Finance profited from the resulting price gaps, while Tether and Circle froze about $1.8 billion of stablecoins that had not yet been exchanged.

STORY 3
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: The MSCI trade

The Stock That Tried to Join the Index

In Indonesia, “deep-fried stocks” may sizzle, soar, and pop. PT Abadi Lestari Indonesia shot toward the top.

Its shares had climbed an astounding 5,000 percent. Samuel Sekuritas said that was hardly the end.

Its report predicted a nearly 50,000 percent flight—past LG Corp. and Prada SpA in value and height.

But people familiar with events told a different story: Samuel traders and Abadi had worked to boost the stock’s glory.

They pumped up its price and its trading amount, hoping great size would be all that might count.

MSCI builds indexes—lists funds copy with care. If Abadi joined, those funds would have to buy shares.

That was the wonderfully strange final plan: make automatic buyers purchase as much as they can.

Harry Suhartono and Prima Wirayani reported the news. But MSCI had another path it could choose.

Two days after Samuel’s report, MSCI warned that coordinated trades had distorted the view. Indonesia might leave its emerging-market index—or even drop two categories, too.

The warning struck hard: Abadi fell 70 percent, while Indonesia’s benchmark index lost 40 percent.

Regulators hurried to clean up the trading display. For now, the grand “MSCI trade” had fizzled away.

What happened? Traders were reportedly trying to inflate Abadi’s price and volume so it could enter an MSCI index, forcing index funds to buy it. Instead, MSCI threatened to downgrade Indonesia’s whole market, and prices plunged.

STORY 4
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Career risk hedging

The Stock That Opened the Exit Door

Five years ago, an Anthropic PBC hire might own almost nothing but purpose and fire. Now perhaps $50 million rests in their shares, plus skills that can earn equally dazzling fares.

That earning power is called “human capital”; both fortunes depend on one firm, after all. A hedge offers protection if share prices drop, so part of your wealth does not fall from the top.

But Anthropic stock isn’t publicly sold; only rare tender offers let shares be unrolled. Forwards, swaps, collars, or perpetual trades could cushion a plunge with the payouts they made.

Yet Anthropic’s insider-trading rules say, “No” to employee hedges while shares rise or go low. Even Hyperliquid trades could get workers fired— not quite the protection a hedger desired.

A trader once made $300 million with Tesla Inc. options; then keeping it all there seemed short on precautions. When roulette lands your number again and again, perhaps leave the wheel with most of your win.

With enough vested shares, an employee might leave, though the mission and future rewards make them grieve. Stephen Solaka of Gateway Investment Advisers told The Wall Street Journal of similar risk minimizers.

Some former tech workers want downside protection; resigning can loosen a trading restriction. So stock meant to keep them can grow so immense, they quit for permission to purchase defense.

What happened? The Wall Street Journal reported that some former technology employees quit so they could hedge concentrated company-stock wealth, while Anthropic’s policy forbids employees from using instruments such as forwards, swaps, and collars to hedge its shares. The absurd result is that enormous stock awards intended to retain workers may instead encourage them to leave once enough shares have vested.

STORY 5
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Prediction market alt data

The Sharps Who Knocked on Doors

On Kalshi and Polymarket, contracts are bought To back what you think—and to challenge a thought. If the market’s odds seem too low or too high, A well-placed position may profit nearby.

Some use public polling and data by piles, In quantitative, Nate Silver-style files. But Poly Alpha’s “sharps” tried a boots-on-the-street route: They traveled to Maine to go knocking about.

Bensoussan walked Bangor; Campbell’s group chose Portland, where four other traders joined those Who asked door by door what the neighbors believed, And listened for clues that the polls hadn’t sieved.

Bensoussan found Susan Collins supporters less keen To say how they’d vote or explain what they’d seen. Though polls put Democrat Troy Jackson ahead, That silence made Bensoussan cautious instead.

Bloomberg’s Ben Weiss wrote of trips made to know What polls and percentages might never show. And someday, when wagers grow grander, perhaps Hedge funds will buy candidate-lawn-sign sky maps—

With satellites counting each sign down below, To guess how an election is likely to go. From knocking on doors to space photos for pay: That’s “alternative data” in full, odd display.

What happened? Members of the invite-only Poly Alpha chatroom traveled to Maine to gather firsthand election information that might help them trade prediction-market contracts. The satellite lawn-sign idea was a joke about how larger markets could lead hedge funds to buy increasingly elaborate alternative data.
