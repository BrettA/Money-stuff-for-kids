Fake SpaceX Stock Isn’t Worth as Much

STORY 1
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: SPVs

When the Lockup Opens: The Case of the Phantom SpaceX Shares

In a little online box called an SPV, people bought a piece of SpaceX's sky,

They paid a sum, they clicked "confirm," they trusted the numbers on the fly.

Ram Rupireddy wired in his money — $17,250 was sent along,

Late Stage Management said they held the stock; the portal sang the same old song.

Back then he thought SpaceX was worth about $58 billion on the sheet,

Then June arrived and SpaceX leapt to $1.77 trillion — a carnival feat.

Ram saw his portal list 2,500 shares, a number he took as true,

At the IPO price he guessed that that meant more than $300,000 in view.

But Late Stage later emailed, saying the SPV sold shares in 2024,

At around $105 each (that was before a 5-1 stock split they swore).

So the firm said his holding then was worth $45,450 — a very different song,

And the portal and the tax form and the story did not easily belong.

SPVs can own real stock, or just the paper that pretends to be,

Promoters could spend on yachts while account statements promise the sea.

There are at least a thousand SpaceX-tied SPVs, bankers say,

And when the lockup opens — that’s the test — investors will ask for shares that day.

Some will get rocket stock in hand, some will get cash for what was sold,

Some might find their login dead, the answers curt, the portal cold.

True or fake, the muddle proves how trust can bend and sometimes break,

And now the market tide has gone out — we’ll see who’s been truly awake.

What happened? A special purpose vehicle (SPV) sold interests that purported to give investors pro rata ownership of SpaceX shares, but because private-company shares were transfer-restricted and the SPV kept only account statements, investors could not confirm registered ownership; when SpaceX IPO’d at a $1.77 trillion valuation, Late Stage Management told Ram Rupireddy that the SPV had sold the SpaceX shares in 2024 at about $105 each (pre 5-1 split), leaving his holding valued at $45,450, while his investor portal and tax form had indicated an equivalent of 2,500 shares worth over $300,000 at the IPO price.

STORY 2
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: OpenAI margin loan

Borrowing Against OpenAI to Buy More OpenAI

A company called SoftBank had a share, a bright and shiny part of OpenAI’s heart.

They loved the idea of owning more, so they knocked upon the bankers’ door.

The banks said, “We’ll lend you cash, ten billion dollars we’ll stash.”

SoftBank smiled and took the loan, and put its OpenAI stake up as a stone.

The money's for buying more of the same: another ten billion is the next claim,

It’s the last piece of a thirty-billion plan, due by October—buy more if you can.

Banks usually lend against stocks you can see; public prices tell them what things be.

But OpenAI’s shares are private, not on a public street—the price is hidden, so the risk is steep.

In normal margin loans you must add cash if prices dip, or the lender can seize what’s on the ship.

SoftBank agreed that if its value should fall, it would add cash so the banks won’t stall.

So many people in finance now joke and groan, “Borrowing against OpenAI to buy more OpenAI” — that’s grown

into a funny, risky loop where the same thing funds itself, and a small wobble could hurt many on the shelf.

Other firms, like Anthropic, sit in a similar crowd; lenders build big deals and sometimes speak aloud

about data-center bonds and pledges to pay for compute, turning future deals into today’s money to route.

If the valuation collapses, banks that lent this cash will be in trouble fast — and so will many more in the cast.

This all shows how a single bet can stretch and bend: one loan, another buy, and the risk can spread to no end.

What happened? SoftBank Group used its stake in OpenAI as collateral to borrow $10 billion from banks and plans to pay another $10 billion by October as the final part of a $30 billion investment. The loan is a margin loan that requires SoftBank to inject additional cash if its valuation falls, and OpenAI’s shares are not publicly traded so pricing is harder.

IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Tick sizes

Why a Penny Is Huge in Prediction Markets

Prediction-market contracts are tiny. A typical contract pays either $1 or $0, and across Yes and No contracts the average price is roughly 50 cents.

But prices usually move in one-cent steps.

One cent on a 50-cent contract is 2%. That is a pretty chunky step.

Stocks also commonly move in one-cent steps, but a normal stock might cost around $100 or $150. One cent compared with $150 is almost invisible.

This matters because the gap between what buyers pay and sellers receive—the bid-ask spread—is where market makers and brokers can earn money.

Robinhood reported that customers traded about $956 billion of stocks and 13.6 billion event contracts. Yet Robinhood made $129 million of transaction revenue from stocks and $156 million from event contracts.

So a much smaller pile of prediction-market trading produced more transaction revenue than the enormous pile of stock trading.

Matt Levine’s wonderfully small explanation is: pennies are much bigger when the thing itself costs only fifty cents.

STORY 4
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Leo’s back

Leo’s Fast, Loud Leap

He watched the light on his screen and said, "AI is coming fast," with eyes that shone like chrome,

But bets that stretch on borrowed cash can topple towers made of foam.

He ran a hedge fund called Situational Awareness, proud and bold and clever,

A long/short book, a plan with 5x leverage — a ladder that might not hold forever.

One morning papers fluttered in: margin calls, the lenders calling loud,

Citadel and others wanted cash; his public holdings lost their crowd.

His portfolio’s edges were pulled into the maw of jaws that seize and bind,

Tens of billions, we are told, went misty in the wind left behind.

Most people might unpack their bags and find a quiet, gentle place to rest,

But Leo, fresh from vows and rings, put cash into a different quest.

He plunked down four hundred million — $400,000,000 — into a company not on any public floor,

A private bet on AI’s quickness, because he said the future’s knocking at the door.

Some called it brash, some called it brave; the picture tasted oddly sweet:

He’d learned to cut the borrowed rope, he’d slow the leverage of his feet.

Yet still he liked his venture route, where startups bloom out of a seed,

So he stopped the borrowed cliff and kept the pace he thought they need.

What happened? Aschenbrenner’s hedge fund Situational Awareness ran a long/short portfolio at about 5x gross leverage and faced margin calls from lenders, including actions that let Citadel take down much of his public positions. Days after those margin calls, he invested $400,000,000 into a privately held company and said he would stop using leverage but continue investing in private AI opportunities.

STORY 5
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Harvard consultants

HUCG’s Big Night (with Snakes)

At Harvard there’s a club with a serious name, HUCG, a consulting game.

They took on work for AstraZeneca, Delta, Samsung too — over thirty jobs in a year, the kind grown-ups usually do.

Bloomberg’s Samuel Church found the papers and the files, showing students doing projects that would normally pay adults lots of miles.

Since 2017 the club has brought in nearly seven million bucks, and by June 30, 2025 its net assets stood at $2.7 million — not bad for student lucks.

About 450 hopefuls try each term to get inside — only about ten percent succeed, said Noreen Mohamed, the director’s guide.

The application mimics the pros: a written form, a first interview, a mock case study and a final show — a pathway that trains you to land a six-figure job one day, or so.

But here’s the odd and slightly silly, yet true, part of this book: HUCG doesn’t pay the students a salary for the work they took.

Instead the club throws parties on a very grand scale — an estate in Newport, food choices like sirloin or duck on the plate you hail.

There were bars that were open, a fire pit, bouquet-making with flair, and pythons for pictures wrapped around a neck or arm with daring air.

To be clear: Harvard students did paid consulting for big clients, the club reported almost $7 million since 2017 and net assets of $2.7 million as of June 30, 2025, but members did not receive salaries and the organization spent money on lavish events.

What happened? HUCG contracted with companies (AstraZeneca, Delta Air Lines, Samsung Electronics) and generated nearly $7 million in revenue since 2017, with $2.7 million in net assets as of June 30, 2025; students were unpaid and funds were used for large social events including a Newport estate party with multi-course meals and a reptile petting zoo.
