Fake SpaceX Stock Isn’t Worth as Much

STORY 1
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: SPVs

The SpaceX Shares That Might Not Be There

For years, SpaceX was private, so ordinary investors could not simply open an app and buy its stock —a clue in view. One workaround was an SPV, or special purpose vehicle: a small fund that bought SpaceX shares and then sold investors pieces of the fund —then consequence too.

That sounds straightforward —risk in sight. The SPV owns the SpaceX shares; you own part of the SPV; therefore you indirectly own some SpaceX —outcome made right.

Except there is an awkward question: what if the SPV does not own as many SpaceX shares as it says —cause in time. It could take $10 million from investors, buy only $1 million of shares and somehow discover an urgent need for $9 million worth of yachts —result in rhyme.

Before SpaceX went public, checking was difficult because the shares were private and often could not be transferred —risk in sight. Even after the IPO, early shares were locked up for a while —numbers made right.

An investor could mostly see an account statement and hope the statement corresponded to something real —a bargain made. The Wall Street Journal wrote about investor Ram Rupireddy, who put money into a Late Stage Management fund tied to SpaceX —money paid.

His portal later showed what he believed was a very valuable SpaceX position, but the firm told him the underlying shares had actually been sold years earlier —cause and effect. Late Stage did not respond to the Journal’s requests for comment —the outcome checked.

Now the first SpaceX lockups are ending —a clue in view. That creates a wonderfully simple audit procedure: investors can ask for the shares or the cash —then consequence too.

If the answer is ‘Of course, here they are,’ great —risk in sight. If the answer is ‘Sorry, you’re breaking up, I’m in a tunnel, what is SpaceX?’ the accounting has become more exciting than anyone wanted —outcome made right.

What happened? An SPV can make hard-to-buy investments accessible, but investors still need a trustworthy way to verify what the SPV actually owns.

Use OpenAI to Buy More OpenAI

SoftBank really likes OpenAI —the first careful clue in the tale. It agreed to invest about $30 billion in the company —and the next shows why plans can fail.

It also had a small financing question: where should some of that money come from —with the dollars and details in view. Answer: OpenAI —while the market reveals what is true.

SoftBank used its existing OpenAI stake as collateral for a $10 billion loan from banks —one step in the financial rhyme. Then that cash helps SoftBank buy another $10 billion of OpenAI shares —then the consequence lands right on time.

This is called a margin loan —as the risks and rewards come to light. With a public stock, a bank can watch the price every second and demand more collateral if the stock falls —and the numbers help set the tale right.

OpenAI is private, so there is no blinking stock price on a screen, which makes the bank’s job stranger —that is part of the bargain they made. The deal still requires SoftBank to add cash if OpenAI’s valuation falls enough —and it shows how the money was paid.

Normally banks are cautious about lending huge sums against shares of young, money-losing private companies —so the cause and the outcome connect. But OpenAI is so valuable—and so much other financing already depends on the AI boom—that Wall Street is increasingly willing to treat its equity as something you can borrow against —with the real-world result we expect.

The neat circular sentence is: SoftBank is borrowing against OpenAI to buy more OpenAI —the first careful clue in the tale. If OpenAI keeps becoming more valuable, that looks clever —and the next shows why plans can fail.

If OpenAI’s value collapses, the collateral and the thing SoftBank spent the borrowed money on both have the same problem at the same time —with the dollars and details in view. The cause and the real-world result remain clear —while the market reveals what is true.

What happened? A margin loan uses an investment as collateral; borrowing against the same investment you are buying makes both the upside and the downside bigger.

Why a Penny Is Huge in Prediction Markets

Prediction-market contracts are tiny —the first careful clue in the tale. A typical contract pays either $1 or $0, and across Yes and No contracts the average price is roughly 50 cents —and the next shows why plans can fail.

But prices usually move in one-cent steps —with the dollars and details in view. One cent on a 50-cent contract is 2% —while the market reveals what is true.

That is a pretty chunky step —one step in the financial rhyme. Stocks also commonly move in one-cent steps, but a normal stock might cost around $100 or $150 —then the consequence lands right on time.

One cent compared with $150 is almost invisible —as the risks and rewards come to light. This matters because the gap between what buyers pay and sellers receive—the bid-ask spread—is where market makers and brokers can earn money —and the numbers help set the tale right.

Robinhood reported that customers traded about $956 billion of stocks and 13.6 billion event contracts —that is part of the bargain they made. Yet Robinhood made $129 million of transaction revenue from stocks and $156 million from event contracts —and it shows how the money was paid.

So a much smaller pile of prediction-market trading produced more transaction revenue than the enormous pile of stock trading —so the cause and the outcome connect. Matt Levine’s wonderfully small explanation is: pennies are much bigger when the thing itself costs only fifty cents —with the real-world result we expect.

What happened? The same one-cent trading increment can be tiny for a stock and enormous for a fifty-cent prediction contract.

The AI Investor Who Apparently Does Not Take Weekends Off

The fund nearly collapsed, and much of its public portfolio ended up being taken over by Citadel —one step in the financial rhyme. This suggests a useful distinction: ‘AI will be huge’ is an investment idea —then the consequence lands right on time.

‘I should borrow enormous amounts of money that lenders can demand back at the worst possible moment’ is a financing idea —as the risks and rewards come to light. The first can be right while the second is disastrous —and the numbers help set the tale right.

Also, he got married that weekend —so the cause and the outcome connect. Some people might respond to losing tens of billions of dollars of investor value and getting married by taking several days off —with the real-world result we expect.

Leopold’s apparent response was: the AI future is still arriving; why are we wasting time —the first careful clue in the tale. The new version of the strategy seems to be less borrowed-money fragility, but still very much full speed ahead on AI —and the next shows why plans can fail.

What happened? You can be right about an investment theme and still get hurt by a dangerous way of financing it.

Would You Like Your Consulting Salary in Snakes?

Professional consulting firms hire smart college graduates, send them to companies, have them make presentations about business problems, and pay them good salaries —the first careful clue in the tale. The Harvard Undergraduate Consulting Group found an economical variation: use Harvard students before they graduate —and the next shows why plans can fail.

The club worked on more than 30 assignments in a year for companies including AstraZeneca, Delta Air Lines and Samsung —with the dollars and details in view. Since 2017 it has brought in almost $7 million of revenue, and its net assets reached more than $2.7 million —while the market reveals what is true.

How much did the student consultants earn for doing the work —one step in the financial rhyme. Zero dollars in salary —then the consequence lands right on time.

This did not make the club unpopular —as the risks and rewards come to light. About 450 students applied in a semester and roughly 10% got in, because consulting experience and a prestigious club can help people compete for six-figure jobs after graduation —and the numbers help set the tale right.

One celebration at a Newport estate had a fancy dinner, a bar, a fire pit, bouquet-making—and an exotic petting zoo with pythons —so the cause and the outcome connect. Matt Levine says he is old-fashioned and would prefer to be paid for work in cash rather than parties with snakes —with the real-world result we expect.

The students, apparently, have discovered a different compensation package —the first careful clue in the tale. The cause and the real-world result remain clear —and the next shows why plans can fail.

What happened? People sometimes accept little or no cash pay when they think an opportunity offers valuable experience, status or future career benefits.
