The AMC Token Tussle

AMC had a ledger where its stock shares were known— 892,604,638 it had grown. A share meant a piece of the movie-chain pie, Recorded through ledgers when people would buy.

Then Robinhood offered an “AMC Stock Token” to trade, On a blockchain market that Robinhood had made. AMC had not issued it, joined it, or planned it; The token bore AMC’s name, though AMC hadn’t branded it.

Robinhood said every token was backed one-for-one: For each one in circulation, an AMC share matched that one. Those underlying shares, securely stored away, Were held by a US custody partner, Robinhood would say.

AMC chief Adam Aron was angry at the sight. “Contemptible!” “Outrageous!” He said it wasn’t right. He called the Jersey market “quasi-fake” and demanded it cease; He feared it would make public distrust increase.

Robinhood chief Vlad Tenev answered, calm and blunt: “What’s the concern?” was the whole of his front. For here was the wonderfully awkward affair: Both companies wanted folks buying something tied to an AMC share.

What happened? Robinhood offered blockchain tokens bearing AMC’s name and said each was backed 1:1 by corresponding AMC stock held through a US custody partner. AMC did not create or approve the product, so Adam Aron demanded that trading stop, while Vlad Tenev asked, “What’s the concern?”

The Price of Apollo’s Reputation

The Financial Times reported a curious borrowing score: Apollo-owned companies paid about one percentage point more.

That extra point is interest, due each year the debt remains; It’s called the “Apollo premium,” a price for lenders’ pains.

Vince Buccola at Chicago and Greg Nini at Drexel Studied private-equity sponsors and the yields their companies paid as well.

Their paper, The Sponsor Premium, ranked reputations high to low: Apollo seemed most aggressive; genial Madison Dearborn sat below.

They checked restructurings that reshuffle what a company must repay, And even asked large language models who seemed harsh along the way!

Apollo answered that its borrowers carry less debt and tighter rules, Producing strong results for lenders and investors—not treating either as fools.

On $40 billion of debt, that point could cost $400 million a year— A hefty price for keeping future restructuring choices near.

But suppose, once in three years, a dividend recap gets done: The company borrows more and pays its owners $1.5 billion.

Three years at $400 million makes $1.2 billion in cost; If owners pull out $1.5 billion, perhaps the premium wasn’t lost.

Are lenders charging just enough before aggressive moves begin? Or is Apollo buying optionality cheaply—and still set to win?

What happened? Buccola and Nini found that Apollo-owned companies paid about 100 basis points, or one percentage point, more to borrow after adjusting for other risks. The paper estimated that cost but not whether Apollo’s ability to extract money later through restructurings makes the extra interest worthwhile.

IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Pensions are back

The Pension Pool Opens Its Door

Once, workers’ pensions were gathered in pools, run by professional managers following rules. They knew when retirement payments came due, so they could wait years for investments to brew.

With plenty of money, just one percent might make private equity’s small slice worthwhile. Some assets were opaque, with no quick way out— “illiquid” means cash can’t be fetched on a shout.

The financial industry loved selling such stuff: locked-up and complex, for years long enough. But pensions grew scarce as the 401(k) let each worker choose where their own savings lay.

Those workers want prices each day they can see, low costs, and the freedom to sell instantly. Private credit funds tried allowing cash out— then withdrawals and bad press came swirling about.

Now The Wall Street Journal reports a surprise: Some companies see pensions with fresh, hopeful eyes. They may help settle talks with a labor union, or keep valued workers from seeking a new one.

Many new plans don’t promise one fixed amount; their returns may track markets, like a 401(k) account. Yet a pooled, market-linked annuity may wait on long-term investments for a later date.

So the pension, once nearly pushed out of the store, may roll its big investment cart through the door. It can pay workers income—and, funny but true, buy the locked-up “spicy stuff” Wall Street missed too.

What happened? A small but growing number of companies are reviving pensions, often for labor negotiations or employee recruitment. Newer market-linked designs reduce fixed-benefit risk for employers and may let pooled retirement funds hold more illiquid private-market investments than ordinary 401(k) accounts can.

The Coin Named $LAPTOP

Hunter Biden announced a meme coin with a headline-ready name: $LAPTOP, for the notorious computer tied to his political fame. The son of former President Joe Biden aimed it at Donald Trump’s $TRUMP game; on Sept. 9 it was set to launch on Base, Coinbase Global’s digital chain.

For thirty preset events, the founders made a curious vow: If chosen outcomes happened on schedule, some coins would vanish somehow. Up to 30% would be “burned”—destroyed and removed from supply; with fewer tokens left for trading, each token’s price might fly.

A Democrat must win in 2028; Bitcoin must reach a record height; $LAPTOP’s fully diluted value—counting every coin—must top $TRUMP outright. When an outcome missed its deadline, that portion would not disappear; the matching tokens would go to charity, as the planners made clear.

Now type “1,000,000,000” in a spreadsheet, then change it when Democrats win: Make “700,000,000” the new total. Did extra value rush in? Must each remaining number be worth 43% more? Why would that be so? And 143% of zero is still precisely zero.

Yet meme coins may trade from comedy, curiosity, or cheer; price times all the tokens makes a “market cap” appear. Another 20% was designated for crypto wallets belonging to $TRUMP losers, Hunter Biden’s Substack subscribers, and people on video journalist Andrew Callaghan’s mailing list.

What happened? Hunter Biden planned to launch $LAPTOP on Base, with up to 30% of its tokens destroyed if thirty specified events had chosen outcomes; otherwise, proportional amounts would go to charity. Reducing supply can raise a token’s price only if buyers still give the remaining tokens value.
