The Young Investor Who Nearly Lost It All

He was young and very clever, a wunderkind of code and light,

a researcher from OpenAI who aimed his smarts to pick what's right.

He said, 'AI will change the world,' and memory chips were in the game,

but money moved on borrowed rails and that borrowed rail caught flame.

They called that borrowed money leverage, and leverage turns small rifts into waves,

so bets that look like breakthroughs can also dig very deep graves.

John Arnold liked a gambler who'd blown up just a single hour,

a past mistake, a single scar that proves you learned the truth and power.

Peter Thiel smiles at contrarian ways—lose a lot and seem more bold—

and Silicon Valley whispers, 'buy the dip,' when prices look undersold.

Leopold’s fund, Situational Awareness, teetered near a snap,

the account sheets looked a little like a very gaping gap.

Wall Street said, 'Rein in risk, beware the dangers that leverage brings,'

but Valley folks said, 'Back him still—give capital to futures and big things.'

Gygmy Gonnot noted the split: tech loves the directional call,

while Wall Street prizes steady gains and keeping investors' capital whole.

Elon Musk’s poker bravado lives in that same daring light,

and losing billions can become the badge that lets someone win the night.

So after the near miss, surprise—requests began to pour,

Silicon Valley wanted in, they liked his thesis and wanted more.

He’s a 20-something investor who very nearly blew the fund apart,

and yet the people who loved his idea lined up to give him a new start.

What happened? Leopold Aschenbrenner’s hedge fund Situational Awareness nearly blew up because it used heavy leverage and incurred very large losses (tens of billions). Some Silicon Valley investors nonetheless requested to place more money with him, reflecting a cultural preference for bold, contrarian bets.

When Funds Bought High and Sold Low (A Short Rhyme)

Big money folks have two favorite games: buy the low and wait, or chase the flame that climbs and climbs and seems to glow,

Value tries to scoop up bargains, thinking prices will slow and grow,

Momentum says, “If it's rising, hop on board — buy higher now and sell it even higher when it goes!” — a paradox that makes some traders cheer and others freezes the nose.

Then came a trick from engineered funds, called leveraged ETFs in trading rows,

They balance each day like clockwork gears: buy when the stock climbs, sell when it bows,

Billions poured in, and Christian Dass wrote how the daily math makes surging names surge yet again,

Florian Ielpo at Lombard Odier said intraday momentum blooms on loud, volatile days; quiet days are when it drains,

Yangyang Hou at JPMorgan Chase & Co. studied five-minute ticks, a simple rule: buy when the price is 1% above the prior close, sell when it slips below, and close out at the bell — a tidy, mechanical refrain,

For a couple of months the returns were called “stellar,” like echoes of 1998/99 when tech jolted and shook the plain,

But markets breathe: a July unwind cut leveraged ETF assets under management from a record high, South Korean trading slumped, and regulators cooled demand, changing who could push the day’s train.

What happened? Leveraged ETFs rebalance daily, buying more when their underlying stocks rise and selling when they fall; traders exploited intraday momentum using five-minute signals and a 1% rule, which produced strong short-term returns during volatile July trading but eased after a July unwind reduced these funds' assets under management from a record high.

IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Tick sizes

The Little Tick That Became a Lot

There once were tiny trading feet that danced on a $0-to-$1 floor,

They hopped by steps of $0.01 and asked for a little more.

A single tick — one penny — looked small in a child’s hand,

But when the whole market marched in pennies, the margin grew quite grand.

Stocks often sleep near a hundred bucks and move by a cent so fine,

That’s 0.01% of the price — a near-invisible line.

On prediction markets, pennies are two percent of the prize,

So market makers spread the bids wide and take the size.

Robinhood set up Rothera, where contracts trade in $0.01 beats,

And brokers smiling at the spread find many handy treats.

Robinhood, in fact, makes roughly a hundred times the fee,

Per dollar traded on prediction bets compared with ordinary equity.

Kalshi and Polymarket whisper differently in some rooms,

Kalshi can do $0.0001 ticks, tiny footsteps in the dunes.

Polymarket also sometimes uses ticks that shrink below the penny,

So not every market marches the same — some go many, many many.

There’s a tug between two futures: one loves gamblers paying wide,

The other needs a hedger’s world with tiny slights and tidy stride.

You could picture retail trading on a broker’s cushioned ground,

While institutions ask for over-the-counter deals to keep costs down.

Susquehanna might price a $10 million wager out of public sight,

There the ticks aren’t giant pennies; they can be finely tight.

So the little penny step that sounds so small in counting games

Turns into a giant puzzle when the money stakes proclaim its aims.

What happened? Prediction markets often used $0.01 tick sizes on $0–$1 contracts (about 2% per tick), creating wide bid-ask spreads that are lucrative for market makers and brokers; Robinhood’s Rothera makes roughly 100x as much per dollar traded on prediction markets as it does on stock trades. Kalshi and Polymarket offer smaller tick sizes (Kalshi down to $0.0001), and large institutional trades can be done over-the-counter to get much smaller effective spreads.

The Private-Equity Party

At parties bright with cameras and light, the same faces looped through the night,

the same 25 people, Kevin O'Leary said, sipping a drink while gossip spread.

Someone new across the room wore a suit, not a crown;

he held a tiny truck that said PEST CONTROL in cardboard brown.

They laughed because the man in the suit did not sweep or swat a flea,

he pooled dollars and bought lots of trucks — that’s private equity.

Kevin O'Leary smiled from his Shark Tank seat and called it more fun,

"When you end up with a private-equity guy, your life gets far more interesting," he said, under the sun.

Jordi Hays on TBPN waved a microphone and made a claim,

"Many women have always been attracted to bad boys and risk-takers," he named.

He pointed at capital allocators who bet in markets every day,

and in the joke they were pest-control princes, in their tidy way.

Leopold Aschenbrenner drifted through the scene like a thinking man,

not mopping floors but moving money according to plan.

The gags were a little true and a little absurd — a prestige sort of spin,

that private equity can feel like a badge for folks who’ve come from where they’ve been.

So the parties kept turning, with whispers and with cheer,

and the man with the model truck bought another company, then another, year to year.

The joke stayed in the room: pest-control men made life more bright,

but the work was not sweeping bugs — it was stacking capital by night.

What happened? The Wall Street Journal reported celebrities dating men who work in private equity, quoted Kevin O'Leary and Jordi Hays, and referenced Leopold Aschenbrenner. Private equity here means high-finance capital allocation to pest-control rollups rather than day-to-day pest-control management.
