IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Public Benefit Fund

Three Ways to Share the AI Pie

Most companies pay workers, then settle each bill; after expenses, some profit is left over still. The federal tax rate takes twenty-one percent, while shareholders keep what remains to be spent.

But some AI builders predict a strange day: machines could do nearly all work for no pay. If AI companies captured the value instead, who would help workers get housed and fed?

Anthropic PBC announced a new fund, with shares for public-good work to be done. Subject to shareholder approval, five percent would start in its care, perhaps helping displaced workers receive a fair share.

If Anthropic’s value rose fifty percent past its set baseline, its board could add two percent more to the fund’s stock pile. The fund—not the government or people at large—would choose which nonprofits or populations received the charge.

OpenAI had proposed another route: give five percent to the US government’s account. Then Elon Musk posted a plan of his own: one SpaceX share for each US child to own.

With some seventy-two million children nationwide, those shares at $146 would total about $10.5 billion—roughly half a percent of SpaceX. Musk did not explain how the giant giveaway would work next.

OpenAI picked government; Musk picked each child. Anthropic picked a fund—and would choose where it filed. Three visions for granting society a cut, each revealing who thinks who should decide what.

What happened? Anthropic proposed committing 5% of its shares to a public-benefit fund, with its board able to add 2% whenever the company’s value rises 50% above a baseline. OpenAI had proposed giving 5% to the US government, while Elon Musk pledged one SpaceX share to every American child, though he did not explain how.

IN MATT'S ARTICLE: ‘Dividend nomads’

The Dividend Nomad’s Bumpy Ride

Jae Hyouk Jang, at forty-three, left security work to wander free. With $130,000 saved away, he crossed Thailand, Vietnam, and Laos day by day.

He joined “dividend nomads,” who travel about, using investment-fund payments instead of a route through nine-to-five work. One fund caught his eye: YieldMax’s CONY, with payouts sky-high.

Its annual payout rate said 58%. Where did all that generous income get sent? CONY sold call options on Coinbase each week— risky crypto-stock income for travelers to seek.

A call gives its buyer, in trade for a fee, a right to buy shares at a price both agree. Those fees helped fund CONY’s distributions, turning weekly wagers into “passive” solutions.

But Coinbase began a long tumble downhill, and CONY fell nearly 40%—what a spill! Its weekly distributions started shrinking as well; the restful-sounding dividend rode a roller-coaster swell.

Jang broke even, then chose a steadier track: safer ETFs, including ones following the S&P 500, filled his pack. Agustin Lebron’s warning fit this strange scene: easy, profitable trades are not often serene.

What happened? Jae Hyouk Jang invested in CONY, an ETF that sold call options tied to Coinbase stock and advertised a 58% annual payout rate. When Coinbase fell, CONY lost nearly 40% and its distributions shrank, showing that a payment called a “dividend” can actually come from a risky options strategy.

IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Sports gambling ETFs

The Sports-Fund Flood

In March 2025 came a half-joking guess: “A sports-gambling ETF in two years, more or less?” But July brought a filing much sooner than planned: the Subversive All Season Sports ETF was at hand.

An ETF is a fund bought and sold like a share. This manager would choose sports wagers in there. You’d hand over your cash; they’d place every bet, then pass on the winnings—if winnings they get.

But where was the fun if a manager chose? Wouldn’t fans want a Mets fund that rose when New York rose? A Mets ETF in a retirement account? “Absolutely. Whatever.” The joke had checked out.

Then Bloomberg’s Vildana Hajric reported a wave: FutureSports built team indexes, each one a gauge. Wins, losses, and other statistics would make every benchmark grow stronger or weak as games changed week by week.

CME Group listed futures for every MLB and NHL team—contracts priced from those gauges. ETF issuers were already preparing funds based on the futures.

On Trillions with Eric Balchunas and Jodie Gunzberg there, FutureSports co-founder Leigh Taylforth had numbers to share: from more than 5,000 ETFs today, thousands more might appear—perhaps tens of thousands someday.

Would 99% of ETFs become sports bets in disguise? Would retirement accounts root for goals, wins, and flies? Probably not. But after the Mets-fund idea came near, “anything is possible” no longer sounded so queer.

What happened? FutureSports created indexes tied to MLB and NHL team performance, CME Group listed futures based on them, and ETF issuers began preparing funds linked to those futures. Leigh Taylforth said the number of such products could eventually reach thousands or tens of thousands.

IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Are MBAs bad?

The School That Had to Sell Its Replacement

Professor Chris Marquis at Cambridge asked one day, “What should an MBA teach along the way?”

At The New York Times, he described students who start with leadership, people, and purpose at heart.

They hope to manage teams, help resources find their wisest use, and leave a better world behind.

But courses can alter the goals they once knew, until profits for shareholders guide what they do.

A shareholder owns part of a company, and then the lessons may prize making more money for them.

“Take down the MBA’s high-status silo,” he said. “Build ‘schools for organizing’ in its stead.”

Let co-ops and unions be studied in class, with movements and shareholder corporations amassed.

Yet business school’s market is easy to see: students want lucrative jobs and will pay for the degree.

Some might choose organizing, determined to lead— but would that new diploma bring earnings they need?

Any new school needs paying students nearby, so it must sell them something they want to buy.

And that was the joke in the plan to say goodbye: it used business school’s lesson—sell what people will buy.

What happened? Cambridge business school professor Chris Marquis argued in The New York Times that MBA education redirects idealistic students toward maximizing shareholder wealth, so universities should replace MBAs with “schools for organizing.” The skeptical response was that prestigious MBA programs thrive because students pay for degrees linked to high-paying careers, while the demand and financial payoff for an organizing degree are uncertain.

IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Onion Stuff

The Onion That Couldn’t Speculate

A futures contract sets a price today for goods that will be traded on a later day. But traders cornered onion futures in 1955, and furious complaints soon reached Congress alive.

The Onion Futures Act arrived in ’58: “No trading onion futures”—that would be their fate. The law now bars box-office-receipt futures too, but onions made the odd old rule that everybody knew.

Years later, University of Chicago students joined the fun. They carted roughly one hundred raw onions into the sun. Eat one whole, they promised, and your bravery could be featured on the students’ website for the world to see.

In 2026, at the Chicago Mercantile Exchange, a marcher dressed as an onion called the old ban strange: “Fifty eight, out of date! Let our onions speculate!”

Could anyone still corner onions after seventy years? Then news from the Philippines supplied some fresher fears. In 2022, onion prices rose so high that onions cost more than meat for shoppers passing by.

This wasn’t futures: six firms and their officers, officials said, had illegally divided parts of the onion trade instead— importing, cold storage, and wholesale distribution of red, yellow, and white onions drew the watchdog’s prosecution.

The Philippine Competition Commission imposed a record fine. The students mocked an ancient scare—with wonderfully awkward timing. Could onion-market trouble ever happen once again? Onions, man! The answer seemed to be: it can.

What happened? Congress banned onion futures after traders cornered the market in 1955. Separately, Philippine authorities found that six firms and their officers used an illegal market-allocation cartel that helped make onions cost more than meat in 2022.
