IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Sports gambling ETF

The ETF That Bet on the Mets

The market woke up with newspapers and light,

And someone said, “Let’s put a Mets bet in an ETF tonight.”

Not a little cheer, not a single seat-row cry,

But an ETF in a broker’s shop so you could buy and sigh.

A professional gambler, a manager in charge,

Would hold forty to eighty bets—no single-season barge.

He’d buy Kalshi contracts, traded over-the-counter style,

So the fund would own the bets, and everyone could smile.

If a Mets-win contract was priced at thirty-three cents neat,

A win would pay a dollar — the fund’s days-kitchen treat.

That morning’s $0.33 could turn into almost three,

Or if the Mets were beaten, poof — the stake could cease to be.

Investors would put money in that box at market price,

They’d either watch it triple up or see it pay the price.

Some folks would use it like a bet for just one night,

Like leveraged ETFs that work when used the way they’re right.

Others liked the wrapper — a stock-market-looking dress —

So managers who can’t buy Kalshi directly might try to compress.

There was talk of Subversive, Tidal, and the SEC’s call,

Of taxes, arbitrage, DraftKings odds and Strategy Inc. in all.

It was brilliant and absurd, a financial carnival riff,

Because every trade, some jokers said, might be sold inside an ETF.

What happened? A proposed fund would be run by a professional gambler who buys exposure to Kalshi event contracts via OTC swaps (typically running 40–80 bets). For example, a 'Mets win' contract trading at $0.33 would pay $1 if the Mets win, so a daily ETF that bought it could roughly triple that day's stake or lose most of it; investors would not pick the individual bets.

IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Equities in Dallas

The Texas Stock Exchange That Lives in New Jersey

Far from the prairies and wide open sky, a new exchange raised a Texas cry.

They called it TXSE, with a big Texas sign—James Lee said, “This market will shine.”

He hired Liz Hocker from the New York Stock Exchange to coax listings that sparkle and gleam,

and even opened a London office to court firms across the stream.

They promised Texas rules that are kinder to managers and meaner to suits,

and traders whispered in early 2024 about an “anti‑woke” route.

Tesla and other companies moved some paperwork to Texas to change what laws could say,

so an exchange named for Texas could be the next place that big firms might stray.

But a market needs buyers—liquidity that flows—so TXSE must prove folks will trade there in droves.

So far, though, TXSE doesn’t have a single listing to show; its team is out hustling, moving slow.

Meanwhile NYSE rebranded NYSE Chicago into NYSE Texas in 2025, and Nasdaq renamed Nasdaq BX into Nasdaq Texas too,

and SpaceX is dual‑listed on Nasdaq and Nasdaq Texas — that’s fact, not something new.

Here’s the absurd little twist that makes people raise a brow: the exchange wears a Texas hat, but its trading computers are not down there now.

The Texas Stock Exchange operates from Equinix NY6 in Secaucus, New Jersey—the servers hum and the orders fly fast—

so the name shouts “Texas” while the actual trading sits near New Jersey’s mast.

They can trade companies listed elsewhere during the day, and they hope state laws and lower fees will bring listings on their way.

What happened? The Texas Stock Exchange (TXSE) started trading last week but has no listed companies yet. Its trading systems operate from Equinix NY6 in Secaucus, New Jersey while the exchange uses a Texas name and offices to signal different listing and governance rules.

STORY 3
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Bending Spoons

Spooners Who Look for Signals

Bending Spoons sails into a company with a careful, clicking crew,

They carry laptops, charts and rules; they know just what to do.

They call themselves Spooners — seven hundred or so strong —

They hop from app to app and sing a data-driven song.

When a purchase lands, the old team trembles, then they part,

Most people go, the Spooners stay, to make a brand-new start.

They rewrite the code, rebuild the pipes, redesign each screen,

They patch the out-of-date things and tidy up the scene.

A talent squad sits in one room with graphs across the wall,

Data scientists, engineers, AI researchers judge them all.

They tinker with the interview, they tweak each little test,

They try a slightly different question and see which one works best.

Then they check the hires again at four months and at eight,

At one year, at two years too — they measure if they’re great.

Ferrari said it plainly — it’s the thing the Spooners sing: "All they do all day long is look for signals," he would bring.

They even make dull businesses sparkle so top grads will apply;

AOL, Evernote, Vimeo — the Spooners give them fresh sky.

Some say it’s a kind of magic, a prestige laundering art,

Top computer scientists hop in where once they'd never start.

It sounds a little strange, that people can be tested like a chart,

But Bending Spoons believes the numbers show which hires will smart.

What happened? Bending Spoons treats hiring as a quant problem: a centralized talent squad of data scientists, software engineers and AI researchers runs predictive models and experiments to pick and fire employees, replaces most staff after acquisitions with about 700 "Spooners," and tracks post-hire performance at 4 months, 8 months, 1 year and 2 years.

STORY 4
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Pivot to AI

Claypot Chicken Turns to AI (Mostly for the Headline)

There once was a chicken shop selling hot claypot rice with care,

Then a headline blared, “We’re AI!” and people read it everywhere.

They changed the sign, they typed a press release, they put AI on the door,

The phones all beeped, the traders clicked — the stock shot up a score.

A sneaker firm, a karaoke place, a digital-treasure crew,

All added AI to chase the buzz — it worked a little, then it flew.

But some real builders work for months and make the chips that feed the dream,

SK Hynix sold $26.5 billion of stock — real life, not just a meme.

Big names like Nvidia and big servers and SpaceX-scale plans,

Are making tools and memory chips and giant data lands.

The Financial Times counted twenty-seven firms since twenty-twenty-three,

Who tacked AI into their names to catch a retail trader’s spree.

Owen Lamont of Acadian said, with markets run by phone and feed,

These name games chase what people like — a short-term, noisy greed.

So the chicken shop’s new sign made headlines for a day,

But real AI builders keep the engines humming all the way.

What happened? Companies changed names or announced AI pivots to attract retail investor attention; the Financial Times found at least 27 companies added AI-related terms since 2023 and most of their initial share price gains did not last. SK Hynix sold $26.5 billion of stock, and Owen Lamont of Acadian Asset Management says these moves target retail investors using social media and trading apps.
