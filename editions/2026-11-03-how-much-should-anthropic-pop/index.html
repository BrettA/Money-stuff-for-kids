Anthropic and the Twenty-Percent Pop

Before Anthropic’s shares could trade in an IPO, Hyperliquid’s pre-IPO wagers were already on show. A perpetual future is a bet on a price; it grants no actual shares, though its guesses can be precise.

The crowd valued Anthropic near $1.8 trillion, while investors proposed $1.5 to $1.6 trillion. They trusted chief financial officer Krishna Rao to price it below what the market predicted right now.

“It might reach $2 trillion by day’s end!” they said. That first-day leap is a “pop” shooting ahead. SpaceX had jumped 30% on its first trading day; Elon Musk had reportedly planned a smaller pop that way.

Bankers like pops: buyers get profit at once, then may return when another big offering comes. But Bill Gurley objects, “That gift isn’t free!” The missing money could have gone to the company.

And here was the puzzle, peculiar and funny: The wager said buyers already knew the likely money. If $1.8 trillion was visible before trading could start, why sell for 20% less—and call that the cautious part?

What happened? Hyperliquid’s perpetual futures valued Anthropic around $1.8 trillion, while institutional investors suggested an IPO valuation of $1.5 trillion to $1.6 trillion so the shares might enjoy a roughly 20% first-day pop. The contracts were bets rather than claims on Anthropic stock, but if their prediction was accurate, the discount would transfer value from Anthropic and its existing shareholders to selected IPO buyers.

The Trader Who Stayed

At D.E. Shaw & Co., where trading plans grew, the bosses asked, “Was that luck—or skill showing through?” A winner can sparkle because fortune was kind; a sound thinker can lose when the markets unwind.

Max Stone, barely thirty, took risks far too wide. Then markets turned sharply, and losses arrived. The firm fell roughly thirty percent in the slide; Stone thought he’d be fired—and called that justified.

“I nearly destroyed it,” he later confessed. Yet D.E. Shaw & Co. judged more than one test. Stone learned and rose onward; now, first among peers, he helps lead the firm after weathering those years.

John Arnold said one past blowup could be best: one dose of disaster, then skill put to test. One executive even was moved up a rung, though, all told, his losses outweighed what he’d won.

“You’ve lost money—promotion!” sounds wonderfully strange, but the firm bet that skill would outlast market change. Nishant Kumar reported the long wager paid: assets topped $100 billion; major rivals were outpaced.

What happened? Max Stone remained at D.E. Shaw & Co. and eventually became a leading executive after taking risks that contributed to a roughly 30% loss. The firm sometimes promoted people with losing records because it believed their repeatable skill mattered more than short-term results.

The Interview That Stole Billions

At Binance, an engineer got a career note: “A project director likes the code that you wrote.” A “CEO” met them through a social-chat call, But the company behind it was not real at all.

“Download this assignment; show what you know.” It looked like developer tools, all ready to go. But the code repository—a folder for code— Held malware that opened a dangerous road.

The software took hold of the engineer’s machine, Then unauthorized actions began behind the screen. Binance blamed North Korea for the $6.3 billion theft; The months-long campaign left compromised devices in its wake.

Richard Teng said it was not one mistake: Fake interviews, malware, and AI helped the break. The attackers used trust as the key to get through— One careless download gave their campaign more to do.

A legitimate exchange might turn this around: Give job candidates malware and see who’s safe and sound. The virus could chirp, “You have failed. Now goodbye!” Then hand back the computer, unharmed and still dry.

Even a writer at Bloomberg, loyal as can be, Says workers may check what their market pay should be. But add a $6.3 billion break-in to it: That’s a double-firing offense—yes, that ought to do it.

What happened? Binance said hackers used fake recruiters and a fictitious company to persuade an engineer to download a malicious code repository, helping enable a $6.3 billion heist. Binance attributed the multi-month campaign to North Korea, and Richard Teng said it combined fake interviews, malware, compromised devices, and AI techniques.
