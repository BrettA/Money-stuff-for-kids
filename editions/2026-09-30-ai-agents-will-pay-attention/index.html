STORY 1
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Attention is all you need

The Bot That Trades While You Sleep

At Houston’s George R. Brown Convention Center one day, Robinhood unveiled a new automated way. The Wall Street Journal reported Vlad Tenev’s big theme: AI agents may trade while their customers dream.

They could study the market, then follow a plan, Buying stocks, options, bitcoin—whatever users command. A feature called Loops could repeat the “wheel” trade: Selling puts backed by cash, then calls backed by shares.

That thrills market makers, who handle each order And pay retail brokers to send trades across the border. More orders can mean that more payments will flow— And bots need no bedtime, so off they may go!

But banks see those tireless assistants with dread: They may move sleepy savings to better rates instead. Rory Van Loo warned of this back in 2019: “Digital Market Perfection” could make switching routine.

So attention has two very different effects: It finds higher interest, but may generate more bets. Brokers gain when bots trade, yet lose some cash spread If agents move idle money elsewhere instead.

For market makers, one customer already sounded grand: A “day trader and volleyball-programs coordinator” close at hand. But someone who trades often with no special edge— And keeps trading while sleeping? That’s the dreamiest pledge!

What happened? Robinhood announced AI agents that can research investments and eventually place trades automatically, including repeating strategies through Loops while users are away. More trades can produce more payment for order flow, while agents that move idle cash to higher-yield accounts can reduce the interest income kept by banks and brokers.

STORY 2
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Man City

The Pounds in Sponsor Hats

In 2008, ADUG bought Manchester City, A club that was middling, though rich in possibility. ADUG belonged to Sheikh Mansour, who wanted to win; Top players and coaches soon started coming in.

Manchester City climbed to the Premier League’s heights, Winning eight championships on trophy-filled nights. But spending was limited by revenue earned— From tickets and sponsors, the rulebook discerned.

Owner investment must be recorded that way; Sponsorship counts as commercial pay. But what if the owner’s pounds wore a sponsor’s hat, Then marched in as revenue? Well! Imagine that.

From 2009/10 through 2017/18, City reported sponsor sums fit for a dream: £949.94 million in commercial income— So an independent Commission checked where it had come from.

The sponsors themselves paid £119.25 million—the base. ADUG paid £830.69 million in their place. Those “tagged sums” were owner funding, the Commission said, Though City’s financial statements called them sponsorship instead.

The Premier League announced the Commission’s decision: Manchester City was guilty of every charged serious breach in its nine-season investigation. The joke had become a very costly fact: A sponsor-shaped detour could not change who paid the cash.

What happened? An independent Commission found that Manchester City disguised £830.69 million from its owner, Abu Dhabi United Group, as sponsorship income between 2009/10 and 2017/18. That made owner funding look like commercial revenue that could support more spending under the financial rules.

STORY 3
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Cotton

The Cotton Contracts That Came in Late

When companies sign big cotton sales, the law says send the news To Agriculture’s weekly report, which growers use as clues. The totals help them judge demand and choose a selling price; Accurate, timely contracts make that weekly guide precise.

Suppose cotton sold for $120; demand was clearly keen. Delay the news, then offer growers $100 while it stays unseen. The weekly numbers look too low, though buyers want much more, And farmers may accept a price they might have questioned before.

Professor O.A. Cleveland made his accusation plain: Traders hid what should be public, while the growers felt the pain. Two commodity firms allegedly reported sales too late; One settled near the end of Biden’s term—the other chose to wait.

Under Trump, the CFTC dropped the remaining case. The delayed reports were now described in quite a different way. Caroline Pham said Olam Group’s issue possibly could be “Paperwork errors”—not a scheme of market trickery.

Brooke Nethercott said minor administrative mistakes drew giant fines. But late reports can hide real demand and shift the cotton pricing lines. “A bit slow with paperwork” sounds harmless, dull, and slight; “Kept growers from the better price” puts matters in new light.

What happened? Cotton traders were accused of delaying legally required reports of large export contracts, making demand appear lower and potentially letting them buy cotton from growers more cheaply. One company settled, while the CFTC later dropped its case against the other company.

STORY 4
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: CEO succession

The Test That Missed John Waldron’s Talent

At Goldman Sachs, young bankers worked late, building financial models till each number sat straight. They polished pitchbooks, with charts neat and bright, while senior bankers won clients by talking just right.

A model estimates what companies are worth; a pitchbook proposes what deals could come forth. Those careful junior tasks could earn a promotion to senior work powered by trust and persuasion.

But spreadsheets and schmoozing aren’t quite the same game: being best at the first won’t ensure client fame. Some juniors can model each detail just so, yet struggle when meeting the people they need to know.

Goldman turned down John Waldron when college was through. On Blackstone’s Inside Blackstone, he explained what went askew: “I didn’t do well in the financial modeling test.” But modeling alone did not measure him best.

Bear Stearns hired Waldron and gave him more say. He handled client relationships while still young in his day. He grasped industries broadly and connected with ease— the very senior skills that can help win deals and fees.

Now he is viewed as David Solomon’s apparent successor at Goldman Sachs: the rejected modeler may reach the top by excelling where the test did not ask.

What happened? Goldman Sachs rejected John Waldron after he did poorly on its financial-modeling test, but Bear Stearns hired him and let him manage client relationships early. Investment banks commonly promote junior model-builders into senior client-winning roles, and Waldron later became the apparent successor to Goldman CEO David Solomon.
