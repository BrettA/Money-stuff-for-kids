IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Is ESG illegal?

When Passive Giants Cast an Active Vote

In 2021, Engine No. 1, a fund quite small, challenged Exxon Mobil’s board, oil giant and all. With just 0.02%, it sought a new direction, and won seats through a binding director election.

BlackRock held 6.7%, Vanguard 8.2%; State Street held 5.7%—their ballots helped it come true. Conor Sen said Engine No. 1 persuaded giants to act, but the joke ran backward too, which made it more exact.

BlackRock already wanted greener plans in view; tiny Engine No. 1 supplied the vote the giants could use. Yet “passive” is a legal coat that managers prize: cross 5% while seeking control, and Schedule 13D applies.

A passive owner may use shorter Schedule 13G; coordinate as a group, and your shares combine legally. Above 10%, quick-trading gains may go back to the company— bad for index funds that must trade when cash flows or indexes change.

Climate Action 100+ joined investors around emissions; BlackRock and State Street left in 2024 as politics changed positions. In 2026, the SEC said some members’ Exxon coordination may have broken disclosure rules—yet brought no enforcement action.

The warning made the punch line hard to miss: a climate vote may turn “passive” giants activist.

What happened? The SEC warned that Climate Action 100+ members coordinating around Engine No. 1’s Exxon Mobil proxy fight might have formed a shareholder group or lost the right to file passive Schedule 13G disclosures. It pursued no enforcement action.

STORY 2
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: 401(k)s

Intel’s Retirement-Fund Time Machine

A 401(k) holds money workers save for older days, while employers select its investment ways. For decades they’ve had one demanding duty: Choose options with prudence, not sparkle or beauty.

There was no official “bad investments” shelf, no “good investments” list that could govern itself. Private equity—company stakes not publicly traded— was never forbidden; its wisdom was debated.

When investments lagged, an employee might say, “You chose without care!” and bring suit that day. A plaintiffs’ lawyer—often Jerry Schlichter—could sue; then judges, not lists, decided what prudent plans do.

President Donald Trump signed an order with cheer, and the Department of Labor made its preference clear: Private assets may fit. Yet its rules, though important, were persuasive authority—not binding on each court.

Then Anderson v. Intel arrived at the Court: A former Intel worker said the plan fell short. Intel’s default funds put about one-third in a blend of private equity and hedge funds—an unusual trend.

The worker said they cost more, hid details, and were slow to sell, while ordinary stock funds had performed rather well. But Samuel Alito said one result can’t decide if a strategy was reasonable when first it was tried.

Ketanji Brown Jackson and Elena Kagan showed puzzlement too, as justices asked what proper benchmark could let the case go through. Intel was yesterday’s oddball; now the industry’s aligned: Nothing grew cheap overnight—the official mood changed its mind.

What happened? Private equity was never expressly barred from 401(k)s, but employers could be sued for choosing it imprudently. In Anderson v. Intel, the Supreme Court considered whether alleged underperformance against an appropriate benchmark was needed for such a lawsuit to survive dismissal.

IN MATT'S ARTICLE: SpaceX/Nvidia

Somewhere in the Universe

Elon Musk’s SpaceX was discussing a plan: Raise forty billion in debt, if it can. Then purchase Nvidia chips by the stack, And pay all the lenders, with interest, back.

Data-center lenders inspect every seam: Will power arrive? Will rent checks still stream? How quickly will GPUs age and decline? Who pays if construction runs past its deadline?

A giant tech company may guarantee rent, So ratings stay sturdy though much has been lent. But one bigger belief can make worries seem small: “AI will need chips—lots of chips—all in all!”

Bloomberg reported the talks. Apollo Global Management was apparently leading the deal, while Pacific Investment Management Co. was involved in the financing.

But investors told the Financial Times with dismay What SpaceX had sent them to study one day: Two pages, space pictures, and one arrow’s cue— Data centers “somewhere in the universe” would do.

“How can we take this to the IC?” came the cry— The committee that votes whether deals get a try. SpaceX had an investment-grade credit rating, Yet lenders still wondered where chips would be waiting.

A lien means missed payments can let lenders seize The pledged Nvidia chips—if they find them with ease. But blast them to somewhere no map can rehearse, And good luck repossessing them out in the universe!

What happened? SpaceX was in talks to raise $40 billion in debt to buy Nvidia chips, with Apollo Global Management apparently leading and Pacific Investment Management Co. involved. Investors said an earlier SpaceX pitch was only two pages long and placed its planned data centers “somewhere in the universe,” making the possible chip collateral sound comically hard to locate.
