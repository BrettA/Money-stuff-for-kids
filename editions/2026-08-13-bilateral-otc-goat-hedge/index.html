Bilateral OTC Goat Hedge

STORY 1
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Goat herding

The Goat Contract

Tim tended goats on steep, brushy hills, four thousand strong and small,

They chewed the tinder down so forests didn’t burn and neighbors could keep all.

But a new reading of a law said the herders’ pay must change overnight,

Costs could jump about four times — Tim worried through the night.

Castle found a clever way: make the worry into a market on display,

Susquehanna priced the deal, and Kalshi listed it that day.

They wrote the rules so answers would be plain — not something vague or mean,

If the state fixed the wage by October first, the payoff would not be seen.

The contract would pay half a million if lawmakers didn’t mend the rule,

So Tim handed over fifty thousand — ten percent — to buy that safety tool.

It’s odd to trade a goat-rule like a game, a market for a law,

But the listing makes a referee: a public record all can draw.

Now traders can peek at Kalshi, small trades pop up on the screen,

And Susquehanna can lay off some risk if others want to lean.

So a rancher, a startup called Castle, and a trading desk that knows the math,

Turned a tiny, urgent village worry into a marketed, priced path.

What happened? This was a Kalshi-listed event contract arranged by Castle and priced with liquidity by Susquehanna; it pays up to $500,000 if California does not fix goat-herder wage rules by the end of September 2026, and Tim Arrowsmith paid a 10% premium ($50,000).

STORY 2
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Fake comments

Who Wrote the Letters?

When a rule is proposed, the rulemakers say, “Tell us what you think!”

People may write letters, each in ink, each from the heart, or just a click.

Companies and groups wanted the regulators to see a crowd, loud and strong,

So they wrote a single letter and said, “Please send it out—send it along.”

The Department of Labor had a plan to let more retirement money roam,

Into private equity and other “alternatives” far from the worker’s home.

Bloomberg’s Noah Buhayar and Jeff Kao began to search and look and find,

About 12,000 submissions seemed manufactured, copies of the same kind.

One note was dated May 3 and bore a name—Danna Oderman—on the screen,

Her son Heath wrote back, “That’s not my mom; she died last December; she’s not been seen.”

Nearly 92% of the entries followed a form letter’s words like a choir,

And one petition opposing the proposal was sent more than 30,000 times—two-thirds of the entire fire.

Ninety-nine percent of those entries included an email and a place,

Bloomberg called dozens and found five people who said, “No—I didn’t sign that case.”

Some real people did send letters: Judith Bergson told reporters she had sent one,

And Americans for Financial Reform said it drafted the opposing form and helped it run.

It sounds absurd—dead names, copy-paste pleas—an astroturf garden grown with care,

But comments to regulators are not binding; they’re signals, loud or bare.

What happened? Regulators received large numbers of mostly identical form comments sent or circulated by interest groups; Bloomberg identified nearly 12,000 submissions that appeared manufactured, including a May 3 comment filed under the name of Danna Oderman (who died in December), about 30,000 identical petition messages made up roughly two-thirds of submissions, 92% of submissions followed form letters, and Bloomberg found several cases where individuals said they did not submit the comments.

STORY 3
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: SpaceX lockup

When the Locked Shares Didn’t Flood

SpaceX went public and people held their shares tight, locked up out of sight,

until a day in August when the lockup would end and selling might begin to bite.

Hedge funds thought ahead, they planned to borrow and to sell,

trying to smooth a coming rush so prices wouldn’t fall pell-mell.

Shorting means you borrow stock, sell it now, then buy it later back —

but borrowing can be scarce, and that makes the plan crack.

On Aug. 3 the price dipped down to $104.84, a low that worried some,

yet by Aug. 6 SpaceX closed at $114.92, and the next open was $114.965, not glum.

Traders feared millions of shares would flood the street on Aug. 6 and 7, a tidal wave in view,

but instead the shares climbed: a 35% surge in five sessions, and about $500 billion added too.

That jump pushed SpaceX back over its $135 IPO price, much to some investors’ delight,

and Bloomberg’s Carmen Reinicke wrote how the feared flood had not arrived overnight.

Andrew Plum of Loxahatchee Capital said the market had perhaps discounted the bad news too hard —

short sellers may have sold tomorrow’s shares today, and the real selling afterward proved less large.

What happened? Hedge funds and other short sellers sold shares ahead of the Aug. 6–7 lockup expiry expecting large insider selling, but borrowing constraints and uncertain insider behavior meant fewer shares were actually sold; instead, SpaceX shares rose 35% in five sessions, adding roughly $500 billion and moving back above the $135 IPO price.

STORY 4
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Bilateral OTC hedging

The Goat Bet That Was Too Big for the Website

Herder's goat hedge had another problem: it was big.

On Kalshi's website there might be a few contracts for sale at one price and a few more at a higher price. Fine for a small bet.

But a real goat business might need millions of dollars of protection. Buying that much from the little offers on the screen could push the price higher and higher.

So Kalshi found another large trader and arranged one giant deal directly between the two sides. Finance calls this a block trade.

It is like walking into a supermarket and saying, “I need 40,000 boxes of Cheerios.” The cashier might suggest that you stop clearing the shelves and call the cereal company.

Herder did not need 40,000 boxes of Cheerios. It needed a very large financial bet about goats.

Which is a sentence that exists because finance is wonderful.
