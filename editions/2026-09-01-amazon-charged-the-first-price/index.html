IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Second price

Amazon’s Mustache Bid

On Amazon, sellers bid to appear When shoppers search for paper towels or gear. A “sponsored” result is an ad in the race, And relevance helps it win the prized space.

In a second-price auction, the highest bid wins, But the runner-up’s price is where payment begins. With bids of $10, $8, and $6 in the fight, The winner pays $8.01—not ten outright.

Yet relevant ads drew a much smaller crowd, So second-best offers were not very loud. Amazon thought premium spaces sold low, And added a “soft reserve” to the show.

Imagine Amazon bidding in a mustache disguise, With “true market value” machine-learning supplies. If bidders offered $10, $2, and $1.50 that day, A $9.50 reserve made the winner pay $9.50.

But suppose the best outside offer was $9, While Amazon’s soft reserve stood at $9.50. Amazon would not keep its own advertising space; The $9 bidder won—and paid $9 for the place.

“Good news, you won! Now pay your full bid!” That sounds like first-price, whatever the label said. The FTC and 22 states sued Amazon, declaring That its hidden surcharge made second-price billing misleading.

Amazon disagreed: reserves are common, it said, And automated tools judge results instead. In 2024, relevance often ruled the amount: About 92% of selected ads were not the top bid by count.

What happened? The FTC alleges that Amazon’s undisclosed soft reserves caused Sponsored Products advertisers to pay their own bids close to 80% of the time, despite descriptions of a second-price auction. Amazon says its reserves reflect placement value and advertisers optimize automated bids using actual results.

GoPro’s Monday Zoom and Tuesday Deal

GoPro made cameras for action and flight, But its battered stock was a sorrowful sight. At sixty cents each, all its shares in the land Were worth about $110 million combined.

By early July, YouTube star Mark Fischbach—Markiplier— Had bought 13.5 million shares as a buyer. That was 8.5% of GoPro, a sizable collection; He filed with the SEC on August 20, to little reaction.

Then Bloomberg’s Lucas Shaw spread the word: “Markiplier is GoPro’s largest shareholder!” readers heard. Old news suddenly felt fresh, and the buyers poured in; Monday’s price leaped 46%—a meme-stock spin.

Some traders had “shorted,” betting GoPro would fall. A jump can make them buy shares back after all. That scramble, called a short squeeze, can lift prices higher, Especially when a famous new owner excites every buyer.

But Tuesday brought news with astonishing speed: GoPro and Starman Optical had already agreed. Starman would pay shareholders $285 million in cash— About $1.14 per share—while they kept a 10% stash.

GoPro’s $92 million debt would be paid at the close; By noon, shares reached $1.31 as excitement rose. Usually meme-stock companies sell some shares while they’re hot; GoPro seemed to sell nearly the whole company on the spot!

The deal had surely been brewing before Monday’s surprise, Yet the sudden meme rally made expectations rise. If traders dreamed of ten dollars while Starman would not, Finishing quickly became rather urgent on the spot.

What happened? After a Bloomberg newsletter highlighted Mark Fischbach’s previously disclosed 8.5% GoPro stake, the stock jumped 46% on Monday. On Tuesday, GoPro announced its already-negotiated merger with Starman Optical, offering $1.14 cash per share, continued 10% ownership, and repayment of about $92 million in debt.

Kalshi’s Curious List

Kalshi looks like online betting at play, Yet federal rules treat it as a financial market today. Federal watchdogs check whether trades have been swayed By fraud, inside information, or tricks people played.
