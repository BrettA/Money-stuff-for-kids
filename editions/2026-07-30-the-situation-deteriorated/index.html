IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Situational Awareness

Leopold’s Very Big AI Bet

Leopold left OpenAI with a plan, a map, and a grin,

He said the world would need big chips and power to let AI win.

He started Situational Awareness with a few hundred million to steer,

and folks sent money in because his thinking sounded clear.

Nvidia climbed 83 percent in two years, SK Hynix rose 220 and higher,

Anthropic leapt more than 400 percent in less than a year — a wildfire.

Adobe, by contrast, slid 51 percent and looked a little blue,

so being long the AI winners and short the old software seemed true.

By May the fund had returned about 270 percent after fees,

and since the start it was up more than 1,000 percent — enough to make knees weak with glee.

One bet — a stake in Anthropic — made up about one-fifth of what he held,

assets under management swelled past $20 billion as more investors were sold.

At the start of July the fund was said to have been as big as $45 billion in size,

but betting for decades on AI while borrowing overnight is how trouble can rise.

Leopold and the fund borrowed to lever bets — sometimes more than 100 percent of the base,

the idea of putting in 300 percent was a risky, almost silly embrace.

When the market turned down, margin calls arrived from banks in suits,

Bank of America, Goldman Sachs and JPMorgan Chase helped sort the disputes.

The fund began to unwind, trying to raise cash and make a plan,

and ultimately sold the bulk of its stock portfolio to Ken Griffin’s Citadel hand.

Citadel, which thinks a lot about funding and risk, bought the long-term AI stakes,

and today those long-term bets seem higher since Citadel took them for keepsakes.

What happened? The hedge fund used borrowed (leveraged) short-term funding to buy large, long-term AI positions and also held big shorts; when AI stocks fell the fund faced margin calls and losses, grew from a few hundred million to as much as $45 billion, had returned ~270% through May and >1,000% since inception, held about one-fifth of assets in Anthropic, and sold most of its stock portfolio to Citadel after the losses.

STORY 2
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Election manipulation

The Day a Wallet Made a Market Roar

Matt Mahan, San Jose’s mayor, said he’d join the race one day in January,

a centrist, forty‑three, steady in his step and hopeful in his manner.

On the morning he declared — a number flashed and numbers tell —

a lone account called Fine‑Tributary spent forty‑four thousand dollars on a sell.

They bought 'yes' contracts on Polymarket; the price snapped up like a bell,

from the low teens to ninety‑six cents out of a dollar — a very loud swell.

In prediction markets a price is a probability, the market’s little vote,

so ninety‑six cents looked like ninety‑six percent — that’s the math that people quote.

The next day the odds eased to thirty‑six percent, still higher than polls' four percent,

and newspapers noticed the spike, wrote stories, and spread the loud event.

A college roommate, Adam Kalamchi, asked friends to donate and 'push up the bids',

and the Polymarket spike became the headline that pivoted into other grids.

The anonymous wallet had one other small trade — five years earlier, five‑hundred dollars placed —

and then it vanished; the big move was almost magic, or a trick that merely chased.

For a few days a tiny amount of money made a big market roar and then recede,

media and momentum followed the bump, and then real polls and time took the lead.

Is asking people to 'push up the bids' market manipulation, or a cheer from a friend? —

The oddness is that a single big bet briefly made a sure‑sounding number bend.

What happened? An anonymous Polymarket user named Fine‑Tributary spent $44,000 on Jan. 29 buying 'yes' contracts for Matt Mahan, pushing the market price from the low teens to $0.96 (96% implied probability); the odds fell back (to 36% the next day) and traditional polls had Mahan near 4%.

STORY 3
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Revlon

The Highest Bid and a Different Kind of Care

When boards must choose who gets the prize, the rule is rather plain,

If one voice sings fifty dollars and one sings sixty, sixty wins the gain.

That idea came from Revlon, and lawyers say it loud and neat,

When selling a company for cash, the highest buyer gets the seat.

But money is not everything — some choices tug the heart,

Twitter’s leaders sighed when Elon Musk paid fifty-four twenty to start.

Their CEO said selling might not be the thing that helps the site,

It might hurt users, workers, ad partners, and make the product light.

Still, the shareholders mattered most; that was the board’s clear chore,

If the board cannot make more than sixty dollars, then sixty’s what you store.

Now some companies are different — a public benefit corporation is one,

A PBC lets directors weigh people, purpose, and profit under the sun.

That means a board can balance pay and planet, purpose, and the crowd,

They do not have to chase the biggest bid if other benefits are loud.

The Delaware Court of Chancery took a look and made the rule brand-new,

It said a PBC’s board needn’t sell to the highest bidder — they may weigh what’s true.

So boards have to think: do we take the money, or guard the public good? —

Sometimes the highest offer wins; sometimes other values must be stood.

What happened? Delaware law’s Revlon duty usually requires directors to favor the highest cash bid to maximize shareholder value in a sale; in a ruling of first impression, the Delaware Court of Chancery held that a public benefit corporation’s board does not have to sell to the highest bidder and may balance shareholder pecuniary interests against other stakeholders and the corporation’s stated public benefits.

STORY 4
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Ionic

Rigs, Leases, and a Little Bit of Luck

There once were rows of humming fans and blinking little lights,

Mining bitcoin through the long, electric nights.

Celsius had the rigs, and Celsius had the loans,

Borrowed at 18% while customers moaned.

Then trouble came — a tumble, a claim, a court’s long, careful sweep,

The company went bankrupt, and promises were deep.

From that tangle rose a plan in January’s new air,

Ionic Digital took the machines with careful, steady care.

They gave the claimants shares, 37 million Class A in hand,

A paper bridge to payment, in a very complicated land.

In Texas — Ward County — they turned the switches down,

Decommissioned miners, hauled the racks into town.

Two hundred thirty‑four megawatts of power once spent on coin,

Were repurposed for AI — a different kind of join.

A 126‑month lease was signed, the contract’s span made clear,

One point nine five billion dollars in revenue to appear.

On the Nasdaq trading day the ticker flashed and climbed,

IOND jumped 26% — a market worth of $2.8 billion, chimed.

The odd, small miracle of what once seemed pointless and absurd,

Was that mining’s dusty sideline rescued value in the world.

So rigs that chased a coin now crunch the math that trains a brain,

And customers who once feared loss were paid in dollar gain.

What happened? Ionic Digital acquired Celsius’s mining assets through the bankruptcy reorganization, issued 37 million Class A shares to eligible Celsius claimants, decommissioned bitcoin mining at its Ward County, Texas site in December, and committed 234 MW of capacity to Nscale under a 126‑month lease carrying $1.95 billion in contracted revenue; Ionic’s Nasdaq debut rose 26%, valuing the company at $2.8 billion, enabling repayment to claimants via equity and AI-related revenue.

STORY 5
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: World Liberty

The Project That Promised the Stars (and Sent Most of the Money to Mr. Trump)

World Liberty stood on a stage and shouted, “We will change how money flies!”

They said they would build a future where decentralized finance would rise.

But here’s the funny, strange, and rather hard-to-say kind of truth: when they counted up the promises, most of the cash was headed straight to one roof.

Seventy-five percent of net revenues, the plan made very clear, would flow to DT Marks DEFI LLC — and Donald Trump owned most of that share.

Justin Sun, who helps run Tron, gave millions because he hoped for the new,

But he says he later found the projects never grew.

He felt his money stuck and said, “I regret my name is on the plan.”

He wanted actual products, not just handing out a grand.

ALT5 Sigma then rushed in, raising seven hundred fifty million dollars to buy the tokens on the shelf,

Their company was worth about a hundred million before the deal, then suddenly leapt to one billion all by itself.

Donald Trump Jr. and Eric Trump even rang the Nasdaq bell with cheer,

But the tokens they’d bought at twenty cents then slid to about six cents the next year.

ALT5 changed its name, its CEO, and cycled through three auditors in sight;

Now called AI Financial Corp., it’s worth under sixty million and faces Nasdaq’s plight.

Investors sighed and crypto folks said, “It’s scammers scamming scammers,” with a knowing, weary grin—

And many thought the loudest promise was just a way to send the money in.

What happened? The project promised decentralized crypto products but structured deals so that 75% of net revenues would be paid to DT Marks DEFI LLC (Trump owns 70%); many promised products were never built, investors like Justin Sun had millions locked up, and ALT5 Sigma raised $750 million to buy tokens at $0.20 that later fell to about $0.06 while the company’s market value fell from its post-deal peak around $1 billion to under $60 million.
