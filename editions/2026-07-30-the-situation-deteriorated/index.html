The Situation Deteriorated

STORY 1
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Situational Awareness

Leopold and the Big AI Bet

Leopold Aschenbrenner loved thinking about how much computers would need to grow for artificial intelligence to get very big. He started a fund called Situational Awareness and picked companies that supply chips, memory, and power for large AI systems.

At first the fund did spectacularly well. Investors poured in money, and the firm’s assets swelled. One big bet was Anthropic — roughly one-fifth of the fund’s assets were tied to it. The fund’s value rose sharply: it reported gains of hundreds of percent since it began, and at one point the business grew to tens of billions of dollars.

To make those wins bigger, the fund borrowed money from banks so it could buy yet more shares. That borrowing — called leverage — makes gains larger but also makes losses bigger and brings fast repayment rules.

When AI stocks fell and lenders asked for more collateral, the fund scrambled. Several of its prime brokers, including Bank of America, Goldman Sachs and JPMorgan Chase, were involved in discussions to make sure the sales happened in an orderly way.

In the end, the fund sold the bulk of its stock portfolio to Ken Griffin’s Citadel. The big idea about AI stayed the same, but the fund’s mix of long-term bets and short-term borrowing didn’t match up.

What happened? Leopold Aschenbrenner’s hedge fund bought large stakes in AI companies and used heavy borrowing (leverage) to amplify returns, growing to roughly $45 billion with about one-fifth of its assets in Anthropic. When AI stocks fell and lenders demanded collateral or repayment, the fund had to sell most of its stock portfolio to Ken Griffin’s Citadel and work with banks including Bank of America, Goldman Sachs and JPMorgan Chase to meet margin calls.

STORY 2
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Election manipulation

How a Big Bet Made a Little Story Loud

Matt Mahan was the mayor of San Jose when he told people he wanted to run for governor of California.

There is a site called Polymarket where people can buy contracts that pay $1 if a certain candidate wins. The price of a contract shows how likely the market thinks that candidate is to win.

One morning an anonymous user named Fine‑Tributary spent $44,000 buying “yes” contracts for Mahan. That single big purchase pushed the contract price from the low teens up to $0.96 — which looked, for a little while, like a 96% chance he would win.

Because the Polymarket number jumped so high, newspapers noticed. The New York Post and the California Post ran a story saying Mahan had a huge surge in support.

People near Mahan also noticed. His college roommate, tech entrepreneur Adam Kalamchi, asked friends on LinkedIn to “donate, post, push up the Polymarket bids.” The market move made a small event feel like big news, and big news can help a campaign get attention and money.

But real voters were not showing that same big change. The next day Polymarket’s price fell back to 36%, and traditional polls were still around 4%. Over the following weeks Mahan’s betting numbers and campaign momentum fell back in line with the polls.

What happened? A user named Fine‑Tributary spent $44,000 buying “yes” contracts for Matt Mahan on Polymarket, pushing the price from the low teens to $0.96 (a 96% implied probability) and briefly producing headlines; the wallet had only one prior small trade five years earlier. The odds quickly fell—Polymarket showed 36% the next day and later matched traditional polls near 4%—showing how a big bet in a thin market can move market-implied probabilities and influence media and donor perception.

STORY 3
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Revlon

The Company That Could Say No

A board of directors has a hard job. If they decide to sell the company for cash, the usual rule — called the Revlon duty — says they should try to get the highest price for the shareholders.

Imagine two bidders. One will pay $50 a share and another will pay $60. Under the usual rule, the $60 bidder should win. That idea pushes boards to sell when they cannot see making more money by keeping the company.

Twitter’s leaders faced a different kind of pressure when Elon Musk offered $54.20 a share. Twitter’s chief executive said the sale raised worries about the product, employees and users, but at $54.20 the board thought it was the best result for shareholders.

There is another kind of company called a public benefit corporation, or PBC. A PBC’s certificate of incorporation tells its directors to balance money for shareholders with other goals — for example, protecting users or serving a public purpose.

People wondered: if a PBC board puts the company up for sale, must it still pick the highest bidder? The Delaware Court of Chancery answered that question for the first time: no. The court said requiring a PBC’s directors to focus only on maximizing the sale price would conflict with the statute that makes them balance shareholder interests and the company’s public benefit.

What happened? The Delaware Court of Chancery ruled that the Revlon duty — the usual obligation to maximize sale price for shareholders in a cash sale — does not force the board of a public benefit corporation to sell to the highest bidder. Twitter sold to Elon Musk for $54.20 a share under ordinary Revlon pressure, but a PBC’s directors can legally weigh public benefits and other stakeholders instead of choosing the top cash offer.

STORY 4
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Ionic

How Old Mining Machines Helped Pay People Back

Celsius Network was a crypto lender that borrowed from customers and also built big rows of machines to mine bitcoin. When Celsius went bankrupt, many people who had put money into the company were left waiting for a way to get paid back.

In January 2024 a new company, Ionic Digital, was created through the court-approved reorganization to take custody of Celsius’s mining assets. Those mining machines were big electrical farms, and they could still do useful work — not just mining bitcoin, but running the kinds of calculations that power AI.

Instead of handing customers a bag of bitcoin, the bankruptcy plan gave some claim-holders equity: Ionic issued 37 million shares of Class A common stock to eligible holders of certain claims against Celsius. That meant people got ownership in the new company that now controlled the machines.

Ionic decommissioned bitcoin mining at its Ward County, Texas site and committed 234 MW of capacity to Nscale under a 126-month lease carrying $1.95 billion in contracted revenue. The lease converted the dusty mining rigs into a steady business contract that could generate cash.

When Ionic began trading on Nasdaq, the stock jumped 26% on its debut and the company was valued at about $2.8 billion. In other words, the mining assets — repurposed for AI work and backed by a long lease — became the vehicle that produced value to satisfy Celsius claim-holders.

What happened? When Celsius went bankrupt, a court-approved reorganization created Ionic Digital to acquire Celsius’s mining assets and give eligible claim-holders equity (37 million Class A shares). Ionic then leased 234 MW of Ward County capacity to Nscale under a 126-month, $1.95 billion contract and listed on Nasdaq, where its stock rose 26% to a $2.8 billion valuation.

STORY 5
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: World Liberty

A Big Promise and the Money That Went Somewhere Else

World Liberty Financial arrived with a bright idea and a strange label. It said it would build decentralised finance — new tools for money powered by crypto. But it also said something else out loud: most of the money the project made would go straight to a company called DT Marks DEFI LLC. That company is mostly owned by Donald Trump (he owns 70%).

People listened. Some invested because they liked the technology idea. Some invested because the project said it would send money to the Trumps. Justin Sun, a crypto entrepreneur, put millions into World Liberty and later said he regretted it because the products he expected never appeared and his name was attached to the project.

A small blockchain firm called ALT5 Sigma Corp. changed course to buy World Liberty tokens. To do that, ALT5 raised about $750 million from big investors, then used the money to buy tokens at about $0.20 each. News of the deal sent ALT5’s market value from roughly $100 million to about $1 billion, and Donald Trump Jr. and Eric Trump even helped mark the moment by ringing the Nasdaq opening bell.

But the buzz faded. The tokens slid to roughly $0.06 each. ALT5 changed its name to AI Financial Corp., cycled through leadership and auditors, and fell to under $60 million in value. Many of the decentralised products World Liberty had promised never showed up.

What happened? World Liberty raised money promising decentralized crypto products while contractually sending 75% of net revenues to DT Marks DEFI LLC (Trump owns 70%). A treasury vehicle (ALT5 Sigma) raised about $750 million to buy tokens at about $0.20 each; the tokens later fell to roughly $0.06 and the promised products largely never materialized.
