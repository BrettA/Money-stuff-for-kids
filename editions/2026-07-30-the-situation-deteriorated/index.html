IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Situational Awareness

The Fund That Went 'More Than All-In'

He left OpenAI with a map for the future, a stack of essays that looked ahead,

and called them Situational Awareness: The Decade Ahead — he said big computers would soon be fed.

Nvidia climbed 83% in two years, SK Hynix rose 220% in the same run,

and Anthropic’s stock bloomed over 400% in under a year — the bright work had surely begun.

Adobe slid down 51% as old software worried the crowd,

so Leopold bought the new engines and shouted his thesis loud.

He started with a few hundred million and then the money poured in,

assets swelled to more than $20 billion, then to near $45 billion at one spin.

The fund rang up returns — about 270% after fees through May,

and since it began it showed more than 1,000% gains, which made people say “hooray!”

One-fifth of the fund sat in Anthropic, a single shining pile,

and as the gains grew, Leopold borrowed more — leverage stacked mile on mile.

Borrowing from banks felt easy when the prices climbed so high,

but loans that can be called back at once don’t match a plan that thinks in decades, not by-and-by.

Then markets shivered and AI stocks dipped; margin calls came in like rain,

prime brokers — Bank of America, Goldman Sachs, JPMorgan Chase — nudged moves to limit pain.

To steady the ship, the fund sold the bulk of its public stock to Ken Griffin’s Citadel that day,

a trade that showed how brilliant long visions can wobble when short-term funding has its way.

What happened? Leopold Aschenbrenner’s hedge fund Situational Awareness grew from a few hundred million to tens of billions after big AI gains, used heavy bank leverage and margin loans, faced margin calls when AI stocks fell, and sold most of its stock portfolio to Ken Griffin’s Citadel with help from prime brokers Bank of America, Goldman Sachs and JPMorgan Chase.

IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Election manipulation

The Day Ninety-Six Cents Looked Like a Victory

On a morning when the ballots were still far away, a market woke and people came to play.

Polymarket showed small numbers that tell us what bets might be true —

then a user named Fine-Tributary stepped forward with forty-four thousand dollars in view.

They bought many "yes" contracts for Matt Mahan, who had said he'd run on Jan. 29,

and the tiny market leapt from the low teens to ninety-six cents — a reach so fast and keen.

In those markets, a price can mean a chance: ninety-six cents looked like ninety-six percent,

so headlines shouted a surge, and reporters printed it on pages prominently sent.

The New York Post and California Post ran the story loud and clear,

but the move was mostly from that one anonymous wallet, which had only traded five hundred once years prior — and then disappeared from here.

Smart traders then bought the opposing "no" side, saying, "That looks like free money,"

and the odds slid back down as markets corrected, not quite as sunny.

By the next day Polymarket still showed thirty-six percent for Mahan — far above the polls at about four,

and voices near the campaign, including Adam Kalamchi, urged supporters to donate and push bids some more.

The odd part, the funny-strange thing, is that a single $44,000 shove could make a whole market sing;

it made the race look certain for a moment, then reality and other traders pulled the numbers back from that ring.

In the end the campaign lost steam and the prices returned nearer to what the polls would say,

leaving the story of a tiny market that briefly made a giant-sounding play.

What happened? A single user called Fine-Tributary spent $44,000 buying "yes" contracts on Polymarket, pushing Mahan's price from the low teens to $0.96; the spike was amplified by media coverage, then corrected by traders and fell back toward poll levels.

IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Revlon

When Boards Have to Choose

There once was a rule with a proper name, Revlon,

That told company bosses to chase the top dollar, not mull on.

If one bidder waved $50 and one held up $60 in bright neon,

The bidders would race — and $60 would win on the button.

Twitter's board sold to Elon Musk for $54.20 a share,

Its leaders looked torn, because they worried for product and people with care.

The rule felt a little odd — sell to the richest stranger? —

Even if the staff and users feared a change that might anger.

Some firms write a promise in their papers, big and clear: "We are a PBC,"

That means the board must balance money for owners and the good for society.

So boards must weigh bright green dollars against people and promise, too,

Like splitting a scale: one side says "shareholders," the other says "public view."

The Delaware Court of Chancery took this up to test what must be done,

It said a PBC's directors need not chase the very highest gun.

They can balance pecuniary interests with those harmed or helped, and the benefit named in their charter,

So a PBC may pass on a big cash bid if the public purpose would be marred.

That is the small absurdity tucked in corporate law's shell: boards once must pick the biggest price,

Yet a PBC may answer differently when its public promise must be weighed against the vice.

What happened? The Delaware Court of Chancery ruled that boards of public benefit corporations do not have to sell to the highest bidder; instead they must balance shareholders' pecuniary interests with the best interests of those materially affected and the specific public benefit stated in the company's charter.

IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Ionic

The Computers That Paid Back

Once was a company called Celsius, loud and grand,

It borrowed at 18% and tossed cash like sand.

People said it was 'perhaps the most ridiculous' of the lot,

It 'yeeted their money directly into the sun' — that line stuck, and it’s not forgot.

Celsius also bought rigs that mined Bitcoin day and night,

Rows of humming machines that burned with electric light.

Then trouble came: courts and papers, a bankruptcy scene,

The miners sat idle where the fields had once been green.

From the mess a new name rose: Ionic Digital, IOND on the board,

It was formed in January 2024 to take the mining hoard.

Ionic gave 37 million Class A shares to people who had claims to file,

And it shut the Ward County miners down, forgoing the mining style.

Instead it leased 234 megawatts, a very big power span,

To Nscale for 126 months, a deal worth $1.95 billion, written by hand.

When Ionic hit Nasdaq its shares jumped up by 26 percent that day,

That priced the company at about $2.8 billion — markets had their way.

So customers who’d been owed money saw equity and a revenue plan,

Not a crypto price rally, but leases and AI work from the computing van.

What happened? Ionic Digital was created from Celsius’s bankruptcy reorganization; it issued 37 million Class A shares to eligible Celsius claim holders and committed 234 MW of Ward County capacity to Nscale under a 126‑month lease worth $1.95 billion, and its Nasdaq debut rose 26% to a $2.8 billion valuation.

IN MATT'S ARTICLE: World Liberty

The Coins That Mostly Went to One Place

World Liberty said, “We’ll build a grand new finance machine,”

a decentralized dream wrapped in code, all shiny and clean.

But World Liberty whispered something louder than code:

75 percent of net revenues would take a very familiar road.

They wrote DT Marks DEFI LLC on the contract, bold and neat,

and Donald Trump owned 70 percent—so most of the money’s seat.

Justin Sun, who runs Tron, put millions into the show,

even while the SEC was looking at him — the timing made the rumor grow.

He hoped the platform would make new crypto tools sing,

but the promised products mostly failed to materialize or spring.

Meanwhile a tiny company, ALT5 Sigma, changed what it did,

and raised $750 million so the World Liberty tokens it could bid.

Those tokens traded at $0.20 when ALT5’s buying spree began,

and Point72 and ExodusPoint were named among the funder clan.

Investors cheered; the stock’s market value swelled up toward $1 billion light,

Donald Trump Jr. and Eric Trump rang the Nasdaq bell one morning bright.

But later the music stopped; the tokens slid to about $0.06 apiece,

ALT5—now AI Financial Corp.—shrank under Nasdaq’s cold device.

Where money was meant for building things, it flowed instead to one name,

and many who joined later said they’d been harmed and felt the shame.

Some called it scammers scamming scammers, the Spider-Man meme fit tight,

because a project that promised revolution mostly funneled cash to one site.

What happened? World Liberty sold tokens and said it would build decentralized crypto products, but 75 percent of net revenues were contractually directed to DT Marks DEFI LLC (Donald Trump owns 70 percent); ALT5 Sigma raised $750 million from investors including Point72 and ExodusPoint to buy tokens at $0.20 each, the tokens fell to about $0.06, and investors and partners such as Justin Sun ended up with money stuck and unmet product promises.
