The Situation Deteriorated

STORY 1
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Situational Awareness

Leopold’s Giant AI Stack

Once there was a thinker named Leopold A., who left OpenAI to build a play,

A fund called Situational Awareness — he raised a few hundred million to start the day.

He saw a future bright with AI, where chips and servers hum and sing,

Nvidia climbed 83 percent, SK Hynix rose 220, Anthropic’s gains took wing.

Anthropic jumped more than 400 percent in under a year, a sparkling, roaring sight,

Leopold’s stake was about one-fifth of his assets, shining in the light.

His fund swelled to more than $20 billion, then to as much as $45 billion in July’s run,

Returns dazzled — about 270% after fees through May, over 1,000% since he’d begun.

He bet long on a slow, decade road: huge clusters, big power and memory stores,

But to buy more he borrowed quick — more than his own money, sometimes 200% or 300% or scores.

That’s the silly, scary part: to grow the bet he used short-term loans that could be called that day,

Prime brokers — Bank of America, Goldman, JPMorgan Chase — could ask for cash if prices slipped away.

When AI shares wobbled and some shorts misfired, the fund faced urgent margin demands,

So it began to unwind and seek buyers — and Ken Griffin’s Citadel met the plans.

Citadel bought the bulk of the stock pile; the long view stayed true but funding wasn’t the same,

Being right about the future didn’t stop the banks from ringing the margin bell game.

What happened? The hedge fund used heavy leverage and had long-term AI investments funded by short-term borrowing; margin calls forced sales and the fund sold most of its stock portfolio to Citadel.

STORY 2
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Election manipulation

The Day the Market Said 96 Cents

Matt Mahan waved and said, “I’ll try for governor,” on a January day in late January,

San Jose’s mayor, forty-three, stepped up — the papers said he might be merry.

But numbers don’t move magic; they tumble or they climb,

and a stranger’s heavy wallet made a sudden, loud chime.

Fine‑Tributary spent forty‑four thousand dollars on a Polymarket screen,

buying “yes” contracts that flashed a number so bright and keen.

From the low teens the price leapt — ninety‑six cents of one dollar,

a market that shouted, “He’ll win!” though the polls said four cents, smaller.

Reporters saw the spike and wrote a headline that read bold and loud,

“Matt Mahan records huge surge,” the newspapers told the crowd.

Adam Kalamchi, a friend from college, tapped LinkedIn with a plea:

“Donate, post, push up the Polymarket bids — whatever helps him be.”

The next day sharp traders sold “no” and hoovered up the bargain,

the odds fell back to thirty‑six cents — the market shed its margin.

A single $500 trade five years before was the wallet’s only other sign,

and after a while the numbers matched the polls, tidy and in line.

So markets can shout loudly and make a bright, sudden show —

but when many people check the facts, the prices come back low.

What happened? Polymarket prices rose to $0.96 after an anonymous wallet called Fine‑Tributary spent $44,000 buying yes contracts for Matt Mahan; the spike drew media coverage and a LinkedIn push by Adam Kalamchi, then traders pushed the price back down to about $0.36 the next day, closer to polling around 4%.

STORY 3
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Revlon

The Big Offers and the Nice Promise

The boardroom had envelopes, two fat ones on the table,

One said $60, neat and loud, the other $50, if you’re able.

“If the directors sell for cash,” the lawyers all would say,

“and one bid is $50 while one is $60, the $60 will take the day.”

That basic rule comes from Revlon — it tells boards what they should do,

When selling a normal company, pick the highest cash bid true.

But there’s a different kind of firm with a different name:

A public benefit corporation puts public purpose in its frame.

So its directors have to balance money with people, product, town square —

They can think of employees, users, and harms they do not want to bear.

Twitter Inc. sold to Elon Musk for $54.20 per share; the board said it was sad,

But they believed that price was best for shareholders, and cash is what they had.

People wondered: if a PBC’s board put the company up for sale, must it pick the top bid?

The Delaware Court of Chancery said no — a PBC can weigh other goods amid.

That felt surprising and a little absurd: a company might refuse the most cash for a kinder plan,

But the court said the statute lets directors balance money and benefit when they can.

What happened? The court ruled that a public benefit corporation’s board need not accept the highest cash bid; under Delaware law the board may balance shareholders’ pecuniary interests against other stakeholders and the company’s stated public benefit, so Revlon’s strict requirement to maximize sale price does not automatically apply to a PBC.

STORY 4
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Ionic

The Miners That Learned to Think

They mined for coins beneath the sun, loud fans and lights that never shun,

But Celsius’s business came undone, its loans and promises on the run.

Out of bankruptcy, a new team rose, Ionic Digital with tidy prose,

It took the rigs where the miner glows and looked at what the future chose.

In Ward County, Texas, quiet miners stood, 234 megawatts of waiting good;

Ionic paused the Bitcoin wood and made a plan that sounded odd but could.

They signed with Nscale, long and clear: a lease for ten and a half years near,

One hundred twenty-six months spelled out, $1.95 billion written out loud without a doubt.

To the people who had claims before, Ionic gave 37 million shares and more,

Then Nasdaq blinked and opened doors: IOND rose 26% and valued at $2.8 billion on the boards.

It’s strange to laugh at how it came— Celsi-us’s lending was a reckless game,

They borrowed at 18% and threw cash like confetti into flame.

Yet the quiet miners, once a side, paid back the holders who had cried,

Not with bitcoin rising high, but with machines that now compute AI.

What happened? Ionic Digital was created from Celsius Network’s bankruptcy to acquire Celsius’s mining assets, issued 37 million Class A shares to eligible holders of certain Celsius claims, decommissioned bitcoin mining at its Ward County, Texas site and committed 234 MW of capacity to Nscale under a 126-month lease carrying $1.95 billion in contracted revenue, and its Nasdaq debut rose 26% valuing the company at $2.8 billion; customers and creditors were repaid through the value generated by the mining assets, the lease, and the reorganization rather than a crypto price rally.

STORY 5
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: World Liberty

A Big Promise, a Bigger Cut

World Liberty said, 'We’ll build a brand-new kind of pay, a ledger that lives in the cloud every day.'

It sounded like magic, a future of finance bright, with decentralized tools that would shine like a light.

But right in the offer, in plain, blinking text, was something that made many people vexed:

Seventy-five percent of net revenues, big and bold, would go to DT Marks DEFI LLC—Trump’s company, we’re told.

Donald Trump owns seventy percent, and family own the rest; that slice of the pie made the whole pitch look less honest.

Justin Sun gave millions, thinking products would follow, and thought he had backed a true crypto Apollo.

He was being investigated by the SEC as he invested, a fact that made the timing feel oddly contested.

Sun says his money got stuck and his name was put on a show; 'This is the worst-case scenario,' he told reporters so.

Meanwhile ALT5 Sigma, a small blockchain firm of under two dozen, raised $750 million so its balance sheet could glisten.

They bought World Liberty tokens at twenty cents apiece, then the price fell down, its climb turned to cease.

Those tokens slid to about six cents, the market’s cheer turned thin; a company once worth a hundred million then popped up to one billion.

Donald Trump Jr. and Eric Trump rang the Nasdaq bell with pride — but a year later the buzz had mostly died.

The promised crypto products largely never showed, and investors felt harmed as the odd pattern glowed.

The strangest, funniest, most naked part of the plan: it opened a direct way to give money to Donald Trump, plain and unspanned.

People called it 'scammers scamming scammers' and compared it to meme spiders pointing at each other; the joke was that obvious, like two identical brothers.

What happened? 75 percent of World Liberty Financial’s net revenues were to flow to DT Marks DEFI LLC (Trump owns 70%). Justin Sun invested millions while under SEC scrutiny. ALT5 Sigma raised $750 million to buy tokens priced at $0.20 that later slumped to about $0.06 each, and most promised decentralized products never materialized.
