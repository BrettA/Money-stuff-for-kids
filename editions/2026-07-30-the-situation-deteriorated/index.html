STORY 1
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Situational Awareness

Leopold and the Very Full Backpack

Leopold Aschenbrenner loved big, hungry thinking machines and bright, blinking things.

He left OpenAI and started a fund called Situational Awareness with brave, ambitious dreams.

He bought SK Hynix chips and tucked a big stake in Anthropic into his pack — oh my!

His fund ballooned and ballooned until it nearly tickled the sky.

But Leopold did not only buy with his pocket change — he borrowed to buy more to play.

He stuffed his backpack until it burst and put in more than all his money that day.

His plan was for decades, slow and steady and grand,

but lenders sometimes like their money back quicker than planned.

Bank of America, Goldman Sachs and JPMorgan waved papers with a frown,

they held up a sign that said, 'MARGIN CALL!' and the market looked down.

So Leopold had to sell his shiny pile, and Ken Griffin's Citadel said, 'We'll take that load,'

Some say he was brilliant, some say he moved too fast; the market keeps humming and stories like this last.

What happened? The fund had borrowed heavily on margin; when AI stocks fell it faced margin calls and sold most of its stock portfolio to Citadel.

STORY 2
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Election manipulation

The Day the Odds Went Boom

In a tiny corner where people wagered on who might win,

Polymarket sold little tickets for a big, noisy spin.

Matt Mahan, mayor with a tidy mayoral grin,

said he would run, and the primary would begin.

Then Fine‑Tributary — a name on a lonely key —

dropped forty‑four thousand dollars on a 'yes' for he.

The price rocketed up, a blink and a sweep,

from the low teens to ninety‑six cents — too steep!

Reporters ran headlines that gleamed and that glowed,

and the internet buzzed where the rumor road flowed.

Adam Kalamchi tapped LinkedIn, urging folks to help the push,

to donate, to post, and to nudge that sudden whoosh.

But clever traders smelled free money in the air,

they bought the 'no' contracts and pulled the price back there.

The spike was loud, but it did not last long,

the market sighed, and the odds moved along.

Mahan’s numbers fell back like waves to the shore,

and his campaign’s roar was quieter than before.

What happened? An anonymous wallet spent $44,000 buying 'yes' contracts on Polymarket, briefly pushing the market-implied odds to $0.96. The spike generated press coverage, but the price then fell back and the market odds realigned with polls.

STORY 3
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Revlon

Twitter, the PBC, and the Two Bids

Twitter was a noisy blue square who liked to chirp and shout,

and it said, 'We do more than make money — we are a town square, inside and out.'

Then Elon Musk knocked, with a suitcase that jingled: $54.20 a share, neat and round,

and lawyers started whispering about what courts and rules might bound.

There is an old rule called Revlon — when a company is sold for cash,

the board must hunt the highest bid and let the richest bidder dash.

But some companies are different; they file as a PBC,

and their directors must balance dollars with the public good they mean to be.

Delaware's Court of Chancery looked at the puzzle and said: 'We must be true,

you needn't sell to the richest offer if your charter says to balance too.'

So the punchline is gentle and a little bit odd and wry:

sometimes doing good can win even if you get not the biggest pie.

What happened? Twitter was a public benefit corporation, and the Delaware Court of Chancery ruled that a PBC’s directors do not have to prioritize maximizing sale price; they can balance shareholder returns against the company’s public-benefit purpose.

STORY 4
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Ionic

The Mining Farm That Learned to Think

In a room of whirring fans and blinking lights, Celsius dug for bitcoin day and night.

They promised big interest and a fancy cheer, and put customers’ coins into things unclear.

They also bought mining rigs, all cables and glow; a little sideline that later would grow.

When Celsius went bankrupt, the court did the math; mining bits got split from the rest of the path.

Those miners became a new company named Ionic Digital, with racks and rooms and a Nasdaq recital.

Ionic issued 37 million shares to people owed money in the case, and then it made a surprising pace.

It decommissioned the Ward County site and leased 234 MW of power to Nscale for a long, long race—126 months, $1.95 billion in contracted grace.

Those rigs would stop mining coins and help AI learn—so customers’ claims could be paid from that future earn.

On Nasdaq debut, IOND rose twenty-six percent and looked quite swell; people who waited got something back—the tale ends well.

What happened? The Celsius bankruptcy transferred mining assets to Ionic Digital, which issued 37 million shares to eligible Celsius creditors. Ionic decommissioned the Ward County site and leased 234 MW of capacity to Nscale under a 126-month contract for $1.95 billion, enabling creditors to be paid from that revenue.

STORY 5
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: World Liberty

They Pitched a Crypto Revolution — and a Direct Line to Trump

World Liberty Financial told people two big things: it would build a new, decentralized kind of finance, and most of the money raised would go to a company owned by Donald Trump. That second part was loud and embarrassing — it was basically a way to give money directly to Trump.

Justin Sun, the crypto billionaire who helped build the Tron network, invested because he said he believed in the 'decentralized' part and because he wanted to help the project. He later said he felt tricked: the crypto products World Liberty promised never showed up and his money got stuck.

A tiny company called ALT5 Sigma suddenly raised about $750 million from big investors to buy World Liberty’s tokens. ALT5’s stock jumped from roughly $100 million to $1 billion in value, and Donald Trump Jr. and Eric Trump even rang the stock-exchange opening bell. But the tokens that had been bought at about 20 cents each slid to about 6 cents, and ALT5 (now AI Financial) crashed toward a much lower value.

Some people shrugged and said the whole thing was obvious: it looked like 'scammers scamming scammers' — everyone pointing fingers, like that Spider-Man meme where two Spider-Mans point at each other.
