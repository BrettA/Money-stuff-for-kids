The Situation Deteriorated

STORY 1
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Situational Awareness

The House of Big AI Bets

Leopold thought the future needed giant thinking machines,

so he built a house of bets on chips, servers, and AI scenes.

He called his fund Situational Awareness, a name so bright,

and friends gave him money because his essays seemed right.

From a few hundred million at the very start,

the pile of money grew — fast and tall, a work of art.

Two hundred seventy percent was the gain through May,

and since he began it was over a thousand, people say.

One big bet was Anthropic, one-fifth of all he’d stored,

a chunky slice of his treasure chest, a single mighty hoard.

But Leopold borrowed more to buy more, to push the bets up higher,

he borrowed from big banks and watched the market like a wire.

When AI stocks slipped downward, the banks made a loud request —

they wanted more cash right then, not later, not at rest.

That’s called a margin call: pay now or sell your stack,

and suddenly his lofty house got shaken at the back.

To meet the calls and tidy things the fund tried to make a deal,

and Citadel came forward to take much of the wheel.

So the long-term dream of power and servers for the years,

bumped into a short-term rule that made the whole room clear.

What happened? Leopold Aschenbrenner’s hedge fund borrowed heavily from prime brokers to amplify its AI stock bets, and when those stocks fell the brokers issued margin calls that forced the fund to sell positions and raise cash. The firm had grown from a few hundred million to as much as about $20–45 billion, held roughly one-fifth of its assets in Anthropic, and reported big gains (about 270% through May and over 1,000% since inception) before the losses and the sale of much of the stock portfolio to Citadel.

STORY 2
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Election manipulation

How one big online bet made a small campaign look like a giant

Matt Mahan, the mayor of San Jose, had just entered the California governor’s race. On the morning he announced, an anonymous user on Polymarket called Fine-Tributary spent $44,000 buying “yes” contracts that would pay if Mahan won.

Because so few people had bet on that market yet, the single large purchase pushed the market price way up — as if the site were saying Mahan had almost certain odds of winning. A newspaper story followed and people in Mahan’s circle asked others to help ‘push up the Polymarket bids.’

But prediction markets are watched by other traders. Soon people bought the opposite side, the odds came down, and the market moved back toward what polls were saying. The campaign’s big spike turned out to be temporary.

STORY 3
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Revlon

The Board That Had to Choose

When a company is up for sale, the board must think about money. That rule is known as the Revlon duty.

If one buyer offers fifty dollars and another offers sixty, the board usually takes sixty, plain and true. That is how the rule works for regular public companies.

Twitter’s directors sold the company to Elon Musk for fifty-four dollars and twenty cents a share. Some people worried, and the chief executive said he had doubts about the deal. Still, the directors believed the price was best for the shareholders.

But there is another kind of company called a public benefit corporation, or PBC. A PBC promises a public purpose as well as value for shareholders.

A PBC’s board must balance the shareholders’ money, the people affected by the company, and the company’s stated public benefits. That means a PBC can sometimes choose a buyer who better serves its public purpose even if that buyer pays less cash.

The Delaware Court of Chancery answered a question nobody had settled before. The court said that Revlon’s demand to focus only on maximizing sale price does not fully apply to PBCs.

So the odd, slightly funny truth is this: a company that promises to serve the public may be allowed to refuse the very highest cash offer if that offer would harm the company’s public purpose. The choice can look strange, but it follows the law for PBCs.

What happened? A Delaware court ruled that the Revlon duty — which usually requires a board to seek the highest cash price when selling the company — does not force a public benefit corporation to sell to the highest bidder. PBC directors may lawfully balance shareholder value against the corporation’s public purpose.

STORY 4
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Ionic

When Miners Became Machines for Brains

Once there was a company named Celsius that promised big interest and kept a noisy mine of Bitcoin machines in its yard.

Those machines hummed and dug for coins when crypto was bright, and people hoped the prices would climb back into sight.

Then Celsius ran out of money, and its doors had to close; customers worried about losses and counted their woes.

Out of the bankruptcy rose Ionic Digital to take the miners and make them new: not for digging for coins but to help AI think true.

Ionic issued thirty-seven million shares to people who were owed, and it listed on Nasdaq, where its value briefly glowed.

At a Ward County site in Texas the mining rigs stopped their spin, and Ionic signed a long lease with Nscale to put the center to win.

Two hundred thirty-four megawatts of power were under the plan, leased for one hundred twenty-six months to bring in contracted cash that can.

The deal carried about one point nine five billion dollars in revenue on file, a steady stream instead of a crypto-price smile.

So the customers who’d feared that their balances might be gone found that hardware and court papers could right what went wrong.

It’s odd and a little funny, and maybe slightly absurd: a lender that once ‘yeeted’ money now paid back folks with machines for AI work, not with a crypto-word.

What happened? Ionic Digital acquired Celsius’ bitcoin-mining assets under the bankruptcy reorganization, issued 37 million Class A shares to eligible claimants, and decommissioned mining at its Ward County, Texas site while leasing 234 MW of capacity to Nscale on a 126-month contract worth about $1.95 billion in contracted revenue, using that enterprise value and stock to make creditors whole.

STORY 5
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: World Liberty

The Big Promise and the Big Mix-Up

There once was a plan that sounded very grand,

World Liberty would build a brand-new money land.

They promised new products, clever and bright,

But most of the money would leave in plain sight.

Seventy-five percent of what the tokens would earn,

Was written to flow to DT Marks, a detail you'd learn.

DT Marks was a company Trump mostly owned,

So money kept moving right to his home.

Justin Sun, with Tron and millions to spare,

Said he believed in the plan and wanted to care.

But the products were missing, the work never done,

Sun found his cash stuck and felt he’d been spun.

ALT5 Sigma, a tiny firm with a new aim,

Bought bags of those tokens and chased sudden fame.

It raised lots of money, investors took note,

Its market value leapt like a boat with a float.

Donald Trump Jr. and Eric celebrated on the bell,

And for a short while the stock rode that swell.

Then tokens slid from about twenty cents down,

To near six cents each, and the buzz left the town.

ALT5 became AI Financial and shrank in its worth,

And people who’d invested were left to sort through the dearth.

The strangest thing here, the thing that made people scoff:

A treasury company bought tokens that then paid money off.

A project that promised a revolution in pay,

Ended up mostly sending the funds the same old way.

What happened? World Liberty’s token sale contractually directed 75% of net revenues to DT Marks DEFI LLC (70% owned by Donald Trump), promised decentralized products that largely never materialized, and a corporate buyer (ALT5 Sigma, later AI Financial) raised about $750 million to buy World Liberty tokens, inflating market value before the tokens fell from roughly $0.20 to about $0.06 and the company’s market capitalization collapsed.
