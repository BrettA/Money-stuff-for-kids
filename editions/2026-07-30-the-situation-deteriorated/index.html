The Situation Deteriorated

STORY 1
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Situational Awareness

Leopold’s Giant, Leaky Balloon

Leopold loved to think about thinking machines, bright and clever and bold,

He started a fund with pockets of money and stories he happily told.

He picked chips like SK Hynix and bets in Anthropic’s name,

Nvidia hummed, servers glowed, and his fund grew fast in the game.

He raised a river of money — from friends who believed his view —

A few hundred million at first, then billions pouring through.

His returns screamed upward: 270% one year, a thousandfold since the start,

So he borrowed more to buy more stock, to play his part.

If 100% is all you have, he’d say, why stop at enough?

Put 200, 300 percent more on the plate — that’s how you chase the stuff.

But borrowed money waits with a clock; the lenders can call in a bill,

If the stocks wobble down a little, that clock begins to shrill.

In July the market shivered; some AI bets slid and fell,

Sudden margin calls and hurried sales began to ring the bell.

Banks and brokers — big names — asked for cash to steady the ship,

So Leopold looked for buyers to take his long and short positions in a blip.

Ken Griffin’s Citadel stepped in and bought a big share of the pile,

Now Citadel holds the long-term bets that Leopold built with style.

It’s a story of being very right about where tech might go,

And of borrowing on short notice, which can make even wins feel low.

What happened? Situational Awareness built big, levered positions in AI stocks and used short-term borrowing from prime brokers, which triggered margin calls when markets fell. The fund sold much of its public stock holdings to Citadel after heavy losses.

STORY 2
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Election manipulation

The Day One Wallet Made a Splash

Matt Mahan said he might run. He said it one morning, then made it true that day.

On Polymarket you can buy a little promise. It pays $1 if the promise comes through.

Fine-Tributary was an anonymous wallet. It spent forty-four thousand dollars on that promise.

The price leapt from the low teens to ninety-six cents. The chart spiked like a sudden drum.

Ninety-six cents meant the market was treating it like a sure thing. That looked like a very big sum.

Reporters saw the spike and wrote about it. The story made the campaign hum.

Adam Kalamchi told friends on LinkedIn to donate and to push up the bids. The cheer sounded like a drum.

Some traders smelled a bargain and sold ‘no’ contracts. The odds slid back to a much smaller sum.

The next day Polymarket still showed thirty-six cents. Polls, by contrast, had him near four.

A single big trade had made tiny money shout, and newspapers treated the shout like more.

The market moved first because it was thin. The media moved next because it saw the grin.

That’s the funny, odd bit of what we saw: a quiet anonymous wallet made a headline law.

What happened? A single anonymous account spent $44,000 buying “yes” contracts for Matt Mahan on Polymarket, pushing the contract price up to $0.96 before other traders pushed it back toward levels closer to polls the next day. Prediction-market prices equal implied probabilities and can be moved by large bets in thin markets, which can attract media attention and campaign interest.

STORY 3
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Revlon

The Board and the Bid

Twitter sat in a room where the people who run it had to decide,

Elon Musk came walking with a bid of $54.20, clear and wide.

Boards usually have a rule they follow, called Revlon, odd as a tune,

It means when a company is for sale, the board should sell for the highest coin in the room.

If one buyer offers fifty and another offers sixty, the sixty usually wins the prize,

That’s because directors must try to give shareholders the best value with legal eyes.

But some companies sign a special promise — they’re public benefit corporations by name,

They must balance money with people and the public’s good, not chase the biggest fame.

So the question walked into the courthouse and sat down on the bench,

Would that old Revlon rule force a public-benefit board to take the highest offer at any expense?

The Delaware Court of Chancery answered on a careful, clear day,

It said a PBC’s directors need not focus only on sale price when choosing the way.

That means a board can weigh users and workers and the charter they wrote,

And law can let them say no to the top bidder if that’s what their public purpose wrote.

It sounds a little absurd, that money might not rule every deal,

But for public-benefit companies, the law lets other values have real feel.

What happened? The court said public benefit corporations do not have to sell to the highest bidder. Directors of a PBC must balance shareholder returns against the company’s stated public benefits and other stakeholders, so they can lawfully choose a buyer who better serves that public purpose even if it pays less.

STORY 4
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Ionic

The Miner That Turned Into a Helper

Celsius once said it would pay high interest, and it borrowed from many folks,

It also bought rows of mining boxes that hummed and ate up power like clocks.

When Celsius ran out of bright ideas, the lights went dim and troubles grew,

Courts sorted claims and gathered the machines — what next could those miners do?

In January a new company rose from the papers and the plan: Ionic Digital named,

It took the mining assets, counted every rack, and put the old debt claims to a frame.

Ionic sent 37 million shares to people who had claims to hold,

And said the miners would have new work, not only digging Bitcoin’s gold.

In Ward County, Texas, the rigs were shut down, their fans now quiet,

Ionic leased 234 megawatts of power to a firm called Nscale in a contract long and tight.

One hundred twenty-six months of payments were promised in the lease

— nearly two billion dollars of contracted revenue, a stream that hoped to bring peace.

On Nasdaq the new stock leapt up twenty-six percent on its first day’s show,

That priced the company at about $2.8 billion, numbers people like to know.

The twist is the funny, slightly crazy thing that makes this story ring: Celsius’ mining sideline —

a silly little footnote to its riskier loans and 18% promises — ended up helping the people who had been left behind.

Now the boxes that once chased Bitcoin’s bright, spinning sun

are being repurposed to do the heavy thinking for AI, a very different kind of run.

It’s odd and a little wild that gear bought in a boom and then put away

could, through a court plan and a long lease, be the money that helped creditors get paid.

What happened? Ionic Digital was formed in January 2024 from Celsius’s bankruptcy reorganization to acquire Celsius’s mining assets and issued 37 million Class A shares to eligible claim holders. Ionic decommissioned mining at its Ward County, Texas site and committed 234 MW to Nscale under a 126-month lease worth $1.95 billion in contracted revenue; the company debuted on Nasdaq up 26% with a valuation of about $2.8 billion.

STORY 5
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: World Liberty

The Money That Said It Would Change the World

World Liberty said, "We’ll make a brand-new crypto scene,"

and wrapped that promise up with the name of a president in between.

They told the people buying tokens, "We’ll build systems that are free,"

but they also said, "Seventy-five percent of the money goes to DT Marks DEFI."

Donald Trump owns most of that company — seventy percent is his share,

so the pitch sounded like giving cash straight to him, plain and bare.

Justin Sun from Tron put millions in and thought he’d helped the plan,

even while the SEC was looking into parts of his own span.

He later said his money got stuck and that his name was on the line,

and that the promised revolution simply failed to shine.

Meanwhile a tiny firm, ALT5, bought lots of World Liberty tokens at twenty cents apiece,

to do that it raised seven hundred fifty million dollars — a very big fleece.

ALT5’s stock jumped from about one hundred million to near one billion on the day,

and Donald Trump Jr. and Eric Trump rang the Nasdaq bell in a ceremonial way.

But the promised products never came, the token price later sank to six cents,

ALT5 changed its name to AI Financial and its market value lost its tense.

People in crypto shook their heads: "Scammers scamming scammers," they said with a scoff,

and one adviser thought of two Spider-Mans pointing fingers at each other, both calling out who’s off.

What happened? World Liberty raised money promising decentralized crypto products while also stating that 75% of net revenues would go to DT Marks DEFI LLC, a company 70% owned by Donald Trump. Investors and a treasury buyer (ALT5 Sigma Corp.) bought tokens and raised $750 million to acquire them; the tokens fell from $0.20 to about $0.06 and ALT5 (now AI Financial) lost much of its value.
