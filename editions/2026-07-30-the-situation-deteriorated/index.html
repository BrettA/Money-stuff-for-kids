The Situation Deteriorated

STORY 1
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Situational Awareness

The Fund That Loved Giant Robots

Once there was a thinker named Leopold, who wrote about giant robot brains that gobble electricity and eat up chips.

He started a fund called Situational Awareness — a few hundred million at first, then it swelled and swelled with other people’s money.

He bought sparkling shares in places that build the robot world: chip maker SK Hynix and a model-maker called Anthropic (which made up about one-fifth of the fund’s pile).

For a while, the fund grew like a rocket: 270% this year through May, and over 1,000% since it began, which made people clap and hand over more cash.

But Leopold didn’t just put the money in — he borrowed extra, and then borrowed some more, so the fund’s bets were bigger than the cash it actually had.

Imagine stacking toy blocks higher and higher while standing on a wobble board; when the table shook, the stack tasted trouble.

Stocks wobble sometimes overnight, and banks said, 'We need more money now' — those are called margin calls, and they can be very loud and urgent.

The fund had also bet the other way on some old software companies and that bet went wrong, which added to the scramble.

Prime brokers like Bank of America, Goldman Sachs and JPMorgan Chase began helping to find buyers and to manage the sell-off.

In the end, after big losses and frantic calls for cash, the fund sold the bulk of its stock portfolio to Ken Griffin’s Citadel, which stepped in and bought the long-term AI toys.

What happened? The hedge fund made concentrated, leveraged long AI bets and shorts against software, and when prices fell it faced margin calls and had to unwind positions, ultimately selling most of its stock portfolio to Ken Griffin’s Citadel.

STORY 2
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Election manipulation

How one big online bet made a small campaign look like a giant

Matt Mahan, the mayor of San Jose, had just entered the California governor’s race. On the morning he announced, an anonymous user on Polymarket called Fine-Tributary spent $44,000 buying “yes” contracts that would pay if Mahan won.

Because so few people had bet on that market yet, the single large purchase pushed the market price way up — as if the site were saying Mahan had almost certain odds of winning. A newspaper story followed and people in Mahan’s circle asked others to help ‘push up the Polymarket bids.’

But prediction markets are watched by other traders. Soon people bought the opposite side, the odds came down, and the market moved back toward what polls were saying. The campaign’s big spike turned out to be temporary.

STORY 3
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Revlon

Why a Company Can Say No to the Highest Bidder

When a company is up for sale, the directors usually have a duty — called the Revlon duty — to get the best cash price for shareholders. If one buyer offers $50 a share and another offers $60, the $60 offer normally wins.

That rule can feel odd. When Twitter’s board agreed to sell to Elon Musk for $54.20 a share, Twitter’s chief executive said he worried the sale wasn’t great for the product, employees, users, or advertisers — but he believed the price was best for shareholders.

There is a different kind of company called a public benefit corporation (PBC). A PBC’s directors are allowed to balance money for shareholders with the company’s public purpose and the interests of people affected by the company.

People wondered: if a PBC puts itself up for sale, does it still have to take the highest cash offer? The Delaware Court of Chancery said no — a PBC does not have to sell to the highest bidder because the law requires directors to balance shareholder money against the company’s public purpose.

STORY 4
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Ionic

How broken crypto companies sometimes pay customers by repurposing mining computers

A few years ago, some crypto companies like Mt. Gox and FTX went bankrupt. In those cases, crypto prices later rose and the leftover coins were worth enough in dollars to pay back customers.

Celsius Network also went bankrupt. It had borrowed from customers and used the money in risky ways — people joked it 'yeeted their money directly into the sun.' That was part of why it failed.

In January 2024 a new company called Ionic Digital was created out of Celsius’s bankruptcy to take over Celsius’s bitcoin-mining assets. Ionic gave 37 million Class A shares to people who had claims against Celsius.

Ionic stopped mining at a big site in Ward County, Texas, and agreed to lease its 234 megawatts of capacity to another firm under a 126-month (about 10.5-year) deal that will bring $1.95 billion in contracted revenue.

When Ionic’s stock started trading on Nasdaq, it jumped about 26% and the company was valued at about $2.8 billion. That value and the income from leases are ways to make customers whole after the bankruptcy.

So instead of a crypto price rally saving creditors, the mining gear and long leases — now useful for AI computing — became the thing that could pay people back.

STORY 5
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: World Liberty

The Tale of the Shiny Tokens and the Giant Share

Once upon a buzz, a company called World Liberty stood up tall,

With glittery token promises and a very, very large call.

They painted a future of decentralized finance so grand,

Then whispered the secret: seventy-five percent would go to Trump’s hand.

DT Marks DEFI LLC would get the lion’s share — a roaring, hefty slice,

Donald Trump owned seventy percent; the math was painfully precise.

Investors clapped and cheered and some put down big stacks,

One of them was ALT5 Sigma, who suddenly changed its tracks.

ALT5 raised seven hundred fifty million dollars to buy tokens at twenty cents,

Retail crowds and hedge funds piled in, swept up by the loud announcements.

For a little while ALT5’s value ballooned — from about a hundred million to a billion with flair,

Donald Trump Jr. and Eric Trump even rang the Nasdaq bell to celebrate the air.

But the glitter didn’t turn into gadgets, the decentralized dreams stayed thin,

Justin Sun and others watched in dismay as the promised products never did begin.

The tokens slumped from $0.20 to about $0.06, the market lost its gleam,

ALT5 changed its name and its leaders, and the big shiny dream popped like a seam.

Scammers scammed — or maybe just very loud planners who liked the sound of their own drum,

Either way the money stuck, the products didn’t come, and the whole thing felt quite dumb.

What happened? World Liberty sold tokens and diverted 75% of net revenues to DT Marks DEFI LLC (70% owned by Donald Trump); a treasury buyer (ALT5 Sigma) raised $750 million to buy tokens, the promised decentralized products never materialized, tokens fell from $0.20 to about $0.06, and investors including ALT5 and retail holders suffered large losses.
