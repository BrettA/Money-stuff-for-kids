IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Private markets are the new public markets: Performance fees

The Good Products and the Very Good Fees

Anthropic and OpenAI were private and grand; Most everyday investors could not lend a hand. SpaceX reached the public at $1.8 trillion in June— Its private backers had gathered that value first, and soon.

Index funds charged retail investors near 0.03%, While private funds took bigger fees for expertise: About 2% of assets, then 20% of gains— A prize for clever choices, if profit remained.

Performance fees were mostly barred for smaller accounts; Only “qualified clients” could pay those amounts. The SEC proposed a brand-new route: Let regulated funds bring private investments out.

PitchBook said private-equity fundraising had declined, With unsold companies waiting in a long, slow line. Will Dunham of the American Investment Council praised the plan, As institutions pulled back and managers sought money where they can.

Perhaps talented managers will bring products that shine, If retail investors can pay them a cut at the finish line. The products may be tarnished; their returns may not please— But one thing is certain: managers will charge the good fees.

What happened? The SEC proposed allowing regulated funds to charge retail clients performance fees of up to 20% of returns, aiming to encourage private-market managers to serve ordinary investors. The proposal could also bring new money to a private-equity industry struggling with weak fundraising and unsold assets.

STORY 2
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Private markets are the new public markets: Certificate of Smart Investment

The Very Smart Investor Button

The SEC wondered, “Must wealth be the key to investments that trade privately?” “Accredited investors” may enter today if wealth or high income has opened the way.

Earn $200,000 each year on your own, or hold a $1 million net worth, fully grown. For couples, $300,000 income will do— but what if a knowledgeable buyer falls through?

So the SEC asked for opinions about a FINRA-made test that could offer a route. It might cover leverage, fees, and dilution, plus balance sheets, conflicts, and risk concentration.

Pass every question! Step proudly outside, with an imaginary “SMART INVESTOR” button displayed. But sellers of structured notes spot it and say, “A sophisticated customer!”—then swarm right away.

The joke is that knowing what “liquidity” means does not make private investments safe or serene. A clever exam-taker may still choose the worst— and confidence can put risky sales pitches first.

One writer preferred a blunter pretend test: Sign, “This may lose everything”—then face one request. An SEC worker asks, “Really???” with a slap; “Yes, really,” you answer—and receive your odd scrap.

What happened? The SEC sought public comment on letting people qualify as accredited investors by passing a possible FINRA exam about securities, risks, financial statements, and conflicts of interest. Passing could let them buy private investments even without meeting the usual income or net-worth thresholds.

STORY 3
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Shareholder voting

The Votes That Follow the Board

At U.S. public companies, shareholders have their say: “Keep these directors? Approve this pay?” Large funds tend to answer; small owners often don’t— For ten little shares, many reasonably won’t.

Usually, silence lets the ballots that arrive decide; For mergers needing most shares, silence joins the No side. But Bloomberg’s Todd Gillespie reported Goldman’s new plan: A retail holder may give the board the steering hand.

“Whenever I’m absent, vote as directors advise.” That standing instruction makes quiet shares arise. Goldman’s individual investors hold roughly three shares in ten, So board-backed votes could grow much stronger again.

A year after executive pay nearly lost its vote, That extra support could help big pay packages float. The Securities and Exchange Commission gave Goldman the green light; Then Tesla received permission for a similar voting right.

It isn’t literally Elon Musk who marks each Yes or No; The ballots follow Tesla’s board wherever its recommendations go. ExxonMobil pioneered this management-friendly track, Though one shareholder asked, “Why not always oppose the board back?”

An AI agent might study every choice and give investors a voice, Yet “re-elect them and approve the pay” may remain the easiest choice. And at Tesla, the instruction seems especially easy to understand: “Just vote for whatever Elon wants”—though officially, it’s the board’s command.

What happened? The SEC allowed Goldman Sachs and Tesla to offer retail shareholders an option that defaults their votes to the board’s recommendations, following an approach pioneered by ExxonMobil. Goldman’s retail investors own roughly 30% of its shares, so the change may increase support for management, including executive-pay proposals.

STORY 4
IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Knicks and Rangers

Two Teams, Two Tickers

MSG Sports held two teams in one stock: the basketball Knicks and the Rangers in hockey. One bundle can soften a stumble or fall— when one side is struggling, the other stands tall.

Such mixtures may comfort the lenders of cash, who dislike risky bumps and a bankruptcy crash. But shareholders hunting for upside may choose the team they expect will have less chance to lose.

Elon Musk’s mix-and-split plans raised this question before: Does one bigger bundle bring investors—or send them out the door? A focused company tells a much clearer story, especially when one team has recently tasted glory.

The Knicks won the NBA title that June; the Rangers, rebuilding, hoped to rise soon. So the board planned a spin-off for October 26, separating the teams—and their investment picks.

MSG Knickerbockers Corp. would hold the Knicks’ name, plus the Westchester Knicks of the G League game. MSG Rangers Corp. would hold hockey instead: the Rangers, their training site, and the Hartford Wolf Pack ahead.

Want both New York teams? Buy shares in each pair. That makes your own bundle, assembled with care. Want baseball, football, or soccer as well? Try Kalshi, came the wonderfully odd punch line to sell.

What happened? MSG Sports approved a spin-off splitting its Knicks and Rangers businesses into two separately traded companies. Investors could then choose one team’s company or buy shares of both to recreate the old diversified holding.
