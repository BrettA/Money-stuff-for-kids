The MANGOS Fund and the Bet Behind the Bet

Corgi Invest launched MANGOS, an ETF to trade, A fund whose shares can be bought and sold each day. Its name holds Meta, Anthropic, NVIDIA in rows, Alphabet’s Google, OpenAI, and SpaceX: MANGOS.

But Anthropic and OpenAI are private—not on an exchange; Their shares are hard to purchase, price, or rearrange. So MANGOS did not simply buy those shares outright. It found a stranger path to track their value’s flight.

On Hyperliquid, traders make perpetual-futures bets: The “long” side gains when prices rise; the “short” side pays its debts. With private shares, no easy trade can keep the wager true— Yet Reid Noch noted such bets predicted SpaceX and Cerebras debuts.

A US fund generally cannot place those offshore bets itself, So Corgi added one more contract to the shelf. A trading firm takes the perps; the ETF instead Uses total return swaps that follow where those prices head.

Jeff Weniger said that was the exposure for now. No Anthropic or OpenAI shares were needed somehow. The fund held $68.4 million, with about five percent in each; Its swaps put private-AI prices within public traders’ reach.

It closed at $26.12; its reported NAV was $26.26. A normal-looking ETF contained some unusual tricks: A listed fund held swaps on offshore bets about stock it did not own— Financial innovation had proudly out-weirded the unknown.

What happened? Corgi Invest’s MANGOS ETF used total return swaps tied to perpetual futures prices to obtain exposure to private OpenAI and Anthropic stock without directly owning those shares. An intermediary handled the offshore perpetual-futures exposure while the US-listed ETF held the swaps.

IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Prime services

The Banks That Profited Without Trading

Once big Wall Street banks would trade all day, Using their own cash to lead the way. They bought stocks, bonds, and derivatives too, Then hoped to sell higher when prices grew.

By 2026, Jane Street and Citadel Were making markets and trading as well. The banks supplied funding for every big bet— A business called prime brokerage, built upon debt.

A margin loan is backed by assets on hand; The trading firm takes the first loss as prices descend. The bank has the senior claim and expects to be paid, But trusts the firm to manage each trade.

Mike Webb of Barclays put it just so: “Banks are struggling to compete” where market makers go. “But financing is a thing that banks do,” With a moat made of credit lines—trillions flowing through.

Hedge-fund borrowing from banks has tripled since 2020. Coalition Greenwich says prime services grew from about 10% of banks’ equities revenue in 2005 to roughly 38% this year.

Yet coveted clients gained power to say, “You cannot withdraw all your funding today.” Lock-ups grew from two weeks to thirty days, Then three months—even half a year in some cases.

So banks earned record trading profits in a curious way: They profited from trades they had lent away.

What happened? Banks increasingly financed firms such as Jane Street and Citadel instead of doing as much trading themselves. Longer lock-ups made that funding steadier for trading firms but reduced banks’ ability to withdraw credit or demand extra margin quickly.

IN MATT'S ARTICLE: Longshot bias

The Market That Sold Fun

At Kalshi and Polymarket, longshots shone all night; The least-likely YES contracts looked especially bright. “Could Jesus Christ return? Could LeBron James prevail In a U.S. presidential race?” Long odds made a sale.

Bloomberg News reporters Carolyn Silverman and Denitsa Tsekova read More than a billion transactions to see where wagers led. Since 2025 began, half of Kalshi’s volume chased Returns of at least one thousand percent—though most bets went to waste.

Recently, over sixty-five percent chased sports parlays: Several picks must all be right to bring enormous paydays. At Polymarket International, the pattern was much the same: Fifty-two percent in twenty-one months backed unlikely claims.

Suppose the true chance is two percent. The eager crowd may Bid a YES contract up to five cents—more than the odds would say. The matching NO costs ninety-five cents and pays a dollar if it’s right; Buying NO is selling the longshot dream that glows so bright.

Buy one hundred NOs for ninety-five dollars; suppose Two strange events occur, while ninety-eight finish NO. Those ninety-eight pay ninety-eight dollars when they’re through: A three-dollar profit—though rare surprises still come true.

Well-funded firms and market makers often took that side, Spreading many little risks instead of betting on one wild ride. The crowd might hit a forty-to-one shot and cheer its lucky day: Some people bought fun. Others sold the fun for pay.

What happened? Bloomberg News found that bettors on Kalshi and Polymarket devoted huge trading volume to unlikely outcomes. Traders and market-making firms taking the opposite side earned steady, modest profits on average because most longshots lost, even though a few won.

The Fifty-Cent Shareholder Lunch

A Malaysian food influencer discovered a treat: / A company’s shareholder meeting served meat.

He bought one hundred shares for eighteen dollars or so. / A share means part ownership—even when it’s low.

That ownership got him into the annual meeting that day, / Where the company’s free buffet was waiting on display.

“You buy share for profit, I buy share for huge chunk of meat. / We are not the same,” he said as he went in to eat.

But after the meeting, the stock slipped about three percent; / On eighteen dollars, that’s roughly fifty cents.

So lunch was nearly free, if you measure the trade— / Though he said that the food wasn’t very well made.

What happened? An unnamed Malaysian food influencer bought 100 shares of an unnamed public company for about $18 so he could attend its annual shareholder meeting and eat the free buffet. The stock later fell about 3%, making his market loss—and therefore his lunch cost—about 50 cents.
